HDMI to H.264 and H.265 IP Stream Latency

Converting an uncompressed HDMI video signal into an H.264 or H.265 IP stream introduces a latency penalty that typically ranges from 15 milliseconds to over 2,000 milliseconds (2 seconds). This delay is not caused by a single operation, but rather by a cumulative pipeline of frame capturing, mathematical compression, packetization, and transmission protocols. Understanding the specific components of this latency penalty reveals why compression efficiency and real-time delivery are always in direct opposition.

The Stages of the Encoding Latency Penalty

The latency penalty begins the moment uncompressed digital video leaves the HDMI port and enters the encoder. The total encoding-side delay is driven by three main stages:

  1. Ingest and Frame Buffering (1–33 ms): An HDMI receiver must ingest pixels row by row. Before an encoder can process an image, it usually requires at least a full line buffer or an entire frame buffer. At 60 frames per second, a single frame buffer instantly adds 16.6 milliseconds of latency.
  2. Compression and Spatial/Temporal Analysis (5–500+ ms): This is where the bulk of the encoding penalty occurs. H.264 (AVC) and H.265 (HEVC) rely on inter-frame prediction, comparing current frames to past and future frames to save bandwidth. The more frames the encoder analyzes simultaneously (lookahead buffers), the higher the latency.
  3. Packetization and Multiplexing (2–20 ms): Once compressed, raw video slices are encapsulated into transport containers (such as MPEG-TS) and wrapped into network protocols like RTP, RTSP, SRT, or UDP.

H.264 vs. H.265 Latency Profiles

While H.265 provides roughly 50% better bitrate efficiency than H.264 at identical visual quality, it requires roughly ten times the computational complexity.

  • H.264: Uses fixed macroblock sizes (16x16 pixels). Its simpler prediction algorithms allow hardware encoders to run in ultra-low-latency modes with penalties as low as 10 to 30 milliseconds.
  • H.265: Uses Coding Tree Units (CTUs) with dynamic sizes up to 64x64 pixels and 35 directional intra-prediction modes (compared to H.264’s 9). In resource-constrained hardware, processing these advanced algorithms adds a latency penalty of 20% to 50% compared to H.264 unless specialized, high-power application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) or field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) are deployed.

Typical Latency Tiers

The real-world latency penalty depends heavily on the hardware architecture and encoding profiles selected:

  • Ultra-Low Latency (ULL) Hardware (15–50 ms / ~1 to 3 frames): Found in professional AV-over-IP systems, medical imaging, and interactive operations. These systems disable bidirectional prediction (B-frames), eliminate lookahead buffers, use intra-refresh slices instead of full keyframes, and process video in slices smaller than a full frame.
  • Balanced Real-Time Streaming (100–350 ms): Typical for video conferencing, live drone feeds, and localized IP distribution (using RTSP or SRT). This tier balances slight lookahead optimization and rate control with manageable interactive delay.
  • High-Efficiency Broadcast Streaming (500–2,000+ ms): Used for non-interactive streaming (such as RTMP to platforms like YouTube or Twitch). The encoder maximizes visual quality and minimizes bandwidth by buffering large Groups of Pictures (GOPs) with multiple B-frames and multi-frame lookahead passes, resulting in a substantial latency penalty.

Factors That Increase the Penalty

  • B-Frames: Enabling B-frames requires the encoder to hold the current frame in memory until subsequent frames are ingested and analyzed, instantly adding multiple frames of delay.
  • Rate Control Buffering: Constant Bitrate (CBR) modes often utilize a Video Buffer Verifier (VBV) buffer. A larger buffer prevents bitrate spikes and improves quality over erratic networks, but expands the encoding latency penalty.
  • Software vs. Dedicated Hardware: Software-based encoding (using x264/x265 on general-purpose CPUs) incurs additional operating system scheduling and PCIe transfer penalties, resulting in significantly higher latency than dedicated hardware encoders using NVENC, Intel QuickSync, or standalone FPGA/ASIC appliances.
