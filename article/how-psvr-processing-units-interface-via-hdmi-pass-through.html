How PSVR Processing Units Interface via HDMI Pass-Through

Custom console peripherals like the PlayStation VR (PSVR) Processing Unit rely on active HDMI pass-through architecture to route, split, and transform audio-video signals between the host console, a virtual reality headset, and a television. This article breaks down the physical and logical interfaces behind this technology, examining how the external unit negotiates display handshakes, handles simultaneous video streams for the user and spectators, and offloads specialized real-time 3D audio processing.

The Signal Routing Architecture

The PSVR setup establishes a dual-loop topology between the console, the processing unit (PU), and the displays. Instead of connecting directly to a television, the PlayStation outputs its primary HDMI signal into the PU's HDMI input. The PU features two separate video output pathways: a dedicated output cable running directly to the headset display and an auxiliary HDMI "TV Out" port running to an external monitor.

A secondary USB connection between the console and the PU functions as a high-speed control channel. While HDMI carries raw, uncompressed video and multi-channel audio data, the USB link carries tracking telemetry, status flags, HDCP verification states, and processing commands.

EDID Negotiation and Active Interception

Standard HDMI pass-through devices are often passive splitters, but hardware like the PSVR PU acts as an active intermediary (a repeater/scaler). When powered on, the PU negotiates Display Identification Data (EDID) directly with the console.

Rather than passing the television's native capabilities straight to the PlayStation, the PU reports a custom set of display profiles. This convinces the console to output high-refresh-rate video streams—such as 1080p at 90Hz or 120Hz—tailored to the headset’s internal OLED panel, even if the connected television only supports a standard 60Hz refresh rate.

Video Demultiplexing and the Social Screen

A primary challenge of console VR is providing a shared viewing experience without degrading headset performance. The PU addresses this through hardware-level demultiplexing and scaling:

  1. Direct Feed to Headset: The PU delivers the native, barrel-distorted stereoscopic image rendered by the console's GPU straight to the headset display with minimal latency.
  2. De-warping for Spectators: Because raw VR video looks distorted and nauseating on a flat display, the PU contains custom silicon dedicated to "un-warping" the image. It crops the feed, corrects the optical lens distortion in real time, and downscales the frame rate to standard 1080p at 60Hz before transmitting it via the HDMI output to the television.
  3. Separate Social Screen Rendering: In specialized multiplayer titles, the console renders two entirely different viewpoints: one for the VR player and one for standard TV players. The console multiplexes these video feeds into a single combined transport stream over the primary HDMI cable. The PU unpacks this composite stream, routing the VR player's perspective to the headset and the separate gameplay view to the TV.

Spatial 3D Audio Processing

HDMI carries linear PCM audio from the console into the PU. To achieve realistic immersion, the PU features a custom digital signal processor (DSP) dedicated exclusively to 3D audio.

The console sends multi-channel object-based audio positions over the connection. The PU intercepts this stream, applies Head-Related Transfer Function (HRTF) algorithms to simulate sound coming from precise directional coordinates in 3D space, and outputs the resulting binaural stereo signal directly to the player's 3.5mm headphone jack. Simultaneously, it sends an un-spatialized, standard stereo or 5.1-surround audio mix down the HDMI chain to the television speakers.

Standby Pass-Through Modes

When the VR headset is powered off, the processing unit acts as a transparent HDMI switch. The internal multiplexers route the PlayStation’s output directly to the television port with negligible latency, allowing the console to function normally for traditional 2D gaming. In later hardware revisions, this pass-through pipeline included updated HDMI controllers capable of handling full 4K HDR bandwidth without requiring the user to physically swap cables.
