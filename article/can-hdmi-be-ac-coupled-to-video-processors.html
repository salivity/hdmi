Can HDMI Be AC-Coupled to Video Processors?

Standard HDMI implementations use DC-coupled Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS), but an HDMI connection can indeed be AC-coupled when interfacing with specific non-standard video processors, FPGAs, or custom display controllers. While the official HDMI specification for TMDS mandates DC coupling with 3.3V pull-up terminations at the sink, introducing series AC-coupling capacitors is a common engineering practice to interface HDMI signals with receivers that operate on different common-mode voltage domains, provided the high-speed differential pairs are correctly biased on the receiver side.

The Standard HDMI Signaling Architecture

Under standard HDMI 1.4 and 2.0 specifications, the physical layer relies on TMDS. In this architecture, the transmitter consists of open-collector or open-drain current-steering drivers, and the receiver provides \(50\,\Omega\) pull-up terminations to a 3.3V rail (\(AV_{cc}\)). Because the transmitter sinks current through the receiver's termination resistors, a direct DC path is fundamentally required for standard-compliant hardware to establish proper common-mode voltage and logic swing levels.

Interfacing with Non-Standard Processors

Non-standard video processors—such as modern FPGAs, custom ASICs, or specialized digital signal processors—frequently feature multi-gigabit transceivers designed for lower internal voltage rails (such as 1.2V, 1.8V, or 2.5V). Connecting a standard 3.3V DC-coupled TMDS signal directly to these inputs can exceed maximum voltage ratings, damage silicon, or violate the receiver's required common-mode input range.

In these non-standard scenarios, AC-coupling allows the system to bridge disparate voltage domains:

  1. Capacitor Placement: Series capacitors (typically between 10 nF and 100 nF) are inserted onto each of the three TMDS data pairs and the clock pair.
  2. DC Isolation: The capacitors block the 3.3V DC common-mode level originating from or expected by external stages, preventing direct current flow between mismatched power domains.
  3. Internal Biasing: The non-standard video processor must provide its own internal termination network and common-mode biasing (for instance, terminating to an internal common-mode voltage \(V_{cm}\) or using an integrated programmable equalizer) to establish a valid differential eye pattern.

Requirements for Successful Implementation

To successfully operate an AC-coupled HDMI link with a non-standard video processor, several criteria must be addressed:

  • DC Balance: TMDS algorithms are designed to be mostly DC-balanced by minimizing transitions, but standard TMDS is not fully DC-free under all conditions. Long runs of identical bits can cause baseline wander across coupling capacitors if the chosen capacitance is too small. Choosing appropriate capacitance values (generally 100 nF) mitigates this issue.
  • Sideband Signals Must Remain DC: Only the high-speed differential pairs (clock and data lanes) can be AC-coupled. Auxiliary channels—including the Display Data Channel (DDC/I²C), Hot Plug Detect (HPD), and Consumer Electronics Control (CEC)—are low-frequency or steady-state DC signals that cannot pass through series capacitors and must be translated using standard level-shifters.
  • Transmitter Compliance: If the source is an off-the-shelf standard HDMI transmitter, it still requires a DC path to pull up its open-drain drivers. In such cases, external bias networks or termination resistors to 3.3V must be placed on the transmitter side of the AC-coupling capacitors to allow the driver to generate the differential signal properly.

HDMI 2.1 Fixed Rate Link (FRL) Exception

It is worth noting that HDMI 2.1 introduced Fixed Rate Link (FRL) mode alongside legacy TMDS. Unlike TMDS, the FRL specification officially accommodates AC-coupling natively, using signaling mechanisms similar to DisplayPort and PCI Express. If the non-standard video processor is designed to interface with HDMI 2.1 FRL modes, AC-coupling is not only viable but standard-aligned.
