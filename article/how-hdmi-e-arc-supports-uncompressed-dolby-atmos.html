How HDMI eARC Supports Uncompressed Dolby Atmos

HDMI Enhanced Audio Return Channel (eARC) achieves the massive bandwidth required for uncompressed, object-based audio formats like Dolby Atmos and DTS:X by fundamentally overhauling the physical and logical data transmission structure used in legacy HDMI ARC. By replacing the low-bandwidth single-wire transmission of traditional ARC with a high-speed differential data channel over dedicated pins, eARC elevates audio throughput from approximately 1 Mbps up to 37 Mbps. This expanded capacity allows televisions to pass pristine, high-bitrate bitstreams—such as Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio containing spatial metadata—directly to AV receivers and soundbars without compression, downmixing, or reliance on external source-switching hardware.

Overcoming the Bottleneck of Legacy ARC

Traditional HDMI ARC (Audio Return Channel) was introduced under the HDMI 1.4 specification to reduce cable clutter. However, it was constrained by its physical architecture. Legacy ARC transmits audio over a single-ended signal using pin 14 (Utility) referenced to ground. This design limited standard ARC to an effective bandwidth of roughly 1 to 3 Mbps.

While sufficient for uncompressed two-channel stereo PCM or lossy, compressed 5.1 surround sound (such as standard Dolby Digital or DTS), legacy ARC cannot accommodate the uncompressed eight-channel PCM or high-bitrate lossless containers required for studio-quality object-based spatial audio. When standard ARC handles Dolby Atmos, it is strictly limited to the lossy Dolby Digital Plus container.

The Differential Pair Architecture

Introduced as part of the HDMI 2.1 specification, eARC solves this bandwidth bottleneck by repurposing two pins within standard High-Speed HDMI cables (with Ethernet) and Ultra High-Speed HDMI cables:

  • Pin 14: Utility / HEAC+ (HDMI Ethernet and Audio Return Channel positive)
  • Pin 19: Hot Plug Detect / HEAC- (Hot Plug Detect and Audio Return Channel negative)

Instead of using a single-ended signal susceptible to electromagnetic interference, eARC binds these two conductors into a shielded twisted pair that operates as a high-speed differential transmission line. By measuring the difference in voltage between the two lines rather than comparing a single wire to ground, eARC drastically reduces noise susceptibility and signal degradation. This differential signaling enables high-frequency transmissions without radiating excessive interference into adjacent high-speed video channels.

Bidirectional Common-Mode Signaling and Clock Recovery

eARC operates using a two-tier signaling structure that balances data transmission with device communication:

  1. Differential Mode (eARC Data Channel): Transmits the high-speed forward audio stream from the display to the audio receiver. The audio data is packetized into IEC 60958/IEC 61937 frames, running at clock rates high enough to deliver raw throughput of up to 37 Mbps.
  2. Common-Mode Data Channel (eARC Discovery and Control): Simultaneously, low-frequency control signals travel over the same pair in common mode. This bidirectional channel allows the TV and audio processor to detect each other, negotiate capabilities, perform handshakes, and synchronize clocks without relying on HDMI Consumer Electronics Control (CEC).

Because eARC embeds clock recovery directly within the differential audio stream, the receiver can reconstruct the master audio clock with minimal jitter. This phase alignment is crucial for passing wide-bandwidth, multi-channel uncompressed audio streams reliably.

Transporting Uncompressed Dolby Atmos and DTS:X

Neither Dolby Atmos nor DTS:X is a discrete audio format on its own; rather, both are spatial metadata extensions mapped over underlying audio codecs.

  • Dolby Atmos in its uncompressed form is embedded within a Dolby TrueHD stream. A full TrueHD bitstream with Atmos metadata requires dynamic bandwidth varying between 8 Mbps and 18 Mbps, far exceeding legacy ARC limits.
  • DTS:X relies similarly on the DTS-HD Master Audio container, which peaks at comparable bitrates up to 24.5 Mbps.

Because eARC provides a consistent 37 Mbps pipe, it easily clears the peak bandwidth demands of these lossless codecs. The TV acts as a transparent passthrough device: it receives the intact, uncompressed bitstream from an onboard app, internal tuner, or connected HDMI source, strips the video, and shuttles the complete TrueHD or DTS-HD MA container across the differential eARC channel to the processor.

The audio system receives the exact studio master audio alongside the dynamic spatial coordinates, mapping individual sound objects cleanly across 3D speaker layouts without dynamic range compression or channel downscaling.
