How HDMI Sinks Detect 5V Power for Hot Plug Detect

This article explains how an HDMI sink device monitors the +5V Power line on Pin 18 to detect source presence before driving the Hot Plug Detect (HPD) line high on Pin 19. It covers the electrical requirements defined by the HDMI specification, the hardware detection circuitry used inside sinks, debounce timing, and the subsequent signaling sequence that enables Display Data Channel (DDC) communication.

The Role of HDMI Pin 18 (+5V Power)

In an HDMI interface, the source is responsible for supplying power to the sink across Pin 18, designated as the +5V Power line. According to the HDMI specification, an active source must supply a minimum voltage of 4.7V up to a maximum of 5.3V, with the ability to deliver at least 55 mA of continuous current.

This line serves two primary functions:

  1. It powers the sink's internal Display Data Channel (DDC) EEPROM in unpowered or standby states.
  2. It acts as an electrical heartbeat confirming that an active source is physically connected and turned on.

Electrical Detection Mechanisms in the Sink

A sink detects the presence of this voltage rail using internal sensing hardware connected directly to Pin 18. This is typically implemented in one of three ways:

  • Resistor Divider with Microcontroller/SoC GPIO: The +5V signal is stepped down to logic-level voltage (typically 3.3V or 1.8V) using a simple resistor divider network. The output of this divider feeds directly into a General Purpose Input/Output (GPIO) pin on the sink's HDMI receiver IC or system microcontroller.
  • Analog Comparator or Voltage Detector IC: To ensure strict adherence to voltage thresholds, some designs use a dedicated supervisor IC or analog comparator with a precision reference. This setup only trips logic-high when the input reaches a set threshold (commonly around 4.5V to 4.7V), preventing brownout states or false positives from floating lines.
  • Transistor-Based Switches: Bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) or MOSFETs configured as inverters or level shifters are often used to turn on internal pull-up networks or enable receiver blocks once the gate or base threshold is overcome by the incoming 5V rail.

Back-Power Protection and Isolation

HDMI sinks must prevent current from leaking into the source or back-powering unenergized internal rails. Sinks typically incorporate reverse-current protection diodes or low-loss P-channel MOSFET switches on Pin 18. This isolation ensures that if the sink is fully powered on while the source is off, the sink will not back-feed voltage onto the cable, keeping the Pin 18 reading completely dependent on the source's power state.

Debouncing and Stabilization

Physical HDMI cable insertion can cause mechanical contact bounce, leading to transient voltage spikes and dips on the 5V line. A sink does not immediately respond to the first detection of voltage. Instead, it implements hardware filtering (via an RC circuit) and software debouncing within the receiver's firmware. The sink requires the +5V line to remain stable within nominal operating limits for a designated duration—typically between 50 ms and 100 ms—before treating the connection as valid.

Transition to Driving Hot Plug Detect (Pin 19)

Once the sink validates the continuous presence of the +5V line and ensures its internal receiver hardware and Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) memory are ready for communication, it activates Pin 19 (HPD).

The sink asserts HPD by pulling the line to a logic-high state (at least 2.4V, typically pulled up to 5V or driven by a 3.3V/5V logic buffer). When the source detects this high voltage level on Pin 19, it initiates the DDC transaction over the I2C bus (Pins 15 and 16) to read the sink's EDID and begin the video handshaking process.
