How HDMI eARC Uses Pins 14 and 19 for Data

Enhanced Audio Return Channel (eARC), introduced in the HDMI 2.1 specification, drastically improves audio bandwidth and stability by repurposing physical HDMI pins 14 and 19 into a dedicated, balanced differential pair. Unlike legacy ARC, which used a single-ended conductor with limited capability, eARC uses these two pins to establish a high-speed, bi-directional, half-duplex communication channel. This architecture simultaneously supports uncompressed, object-based multi-channel audio formats up to approximately 37 Mbps and robust discovery, status, and lip-sync metadata exchanges.

The Role of Pins 14 and 19

In standard HDMI connectors, Pin 14 is designated as the Utility pin (previously used for the single-ended ARC line or HDMI Ethernet Channel HEAC+) and Pin 19 is the Hot Plug Detect (HPD / HEAC-) pin.

Standard ARC relied solely on Pin 14 as a single-ended wire. This configuration suffered from low signal-to-noise ratios, jitter, and bandwidth limits that capped transmission to compressed 5.1 surround sound or uncompressed stereo.

eARC pairs Pin 14 and Pin 19 together to create a shielded, twisted differential pair (HEAC pair). Instead of measuring a signal against ground, eARC measures the relative voltage difference between the two pins, dramatically improving noise immunity and enabling higher transmission frequencies.

Common-Mode vs. Differential-Mode Separation

To maintain backward compatibility and preserve fundamental HDMI operations, eARC isolates its low-frequency functions and high-frequency data using two distinct electrical modes over the same two pins:

  • Common-Mode Signaling: Low-frequency detection and link maintenance are handled in common mode. This preserves standard Hot Plug Detection (HPD) functionality on Pin 19 and enables the "eARC Heartbeat." The heartbeat is a periodic DC-bias signal generated to detect eARC-compatible hardware, initialize pairing, and confirm continuous link stability without interfering with the high-speed data stream.
  • Differential-Mode Signaling: The actual eARC data transfer—consisting of the high-bitrate audio stream and control messaging—is modulated across Pins 14 and 19 as a balanced differential voltage signal.

Bi-Directional Half-Duplex Operation

The eARC Data Channel operates as a half-duplex link, meaning data transmits in both directions across the pair, but not simultaneously. The link divides its operational bandwidth between two primary functions:

  1. The Audio Payload Stream: The bulk of the transmission time is allocated to sending uncompressed LPCM (up to 8 channels, 24-bit/192 kHz) or bitstream formats like Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio from the display device (eARC Transmitter) to an audio receiver or soundbar (eARC Receiver).
  2. eDDC (enhanced Display Data Channel): Interleaved with or preceding audio transmission, the link switches directions to execute bidirectional handshakes. Using the eDDC protocol, the audio receiver advertises its audio capabilities (supported formats, channel configurations) to the transmitter, and both devices exchange dynamic lip-sync latency correction data.

Because transmission and reception share the same differential pair, the transceivers at each end rapidly alternate their drive states between transmitting and receiving according to strict timing schedules defined by the HDMI 2.1 protocol.

Benefits of the Dedicated Architecture

By coupling differential signaling with half-duplex packet coordination over pins 14 and 19, eARC removes the reliance on HDMI CEC (Consumer Electronics Control) for device discovery and audio setup. The direct, isolated communication channel guarantees reliable hardware handshaking, eliminates handshake-induced audio drops, and provides the necessary bandwidth for modern object-based audio codecs such as Dolby Atmos and DTS:X.
