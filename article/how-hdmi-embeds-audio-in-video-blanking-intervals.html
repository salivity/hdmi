How HDMI Embeds Audio in Video Blanking Intervals

This article explains how an HDMI transmitter multiplexes digital audio into an active video stream by converting audio samples into structured packets and transmitting them during horizontal and vertical video blanking intervals. Rather than relying on a separate physical line for sound, HDMI repurposes inactive display transmission windows to interleave high-bandwidth digital audio directly within the primary Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) data channels.

The Purpose of Video Blanking Intervals

In traditional video transmission, blanking intervals—Horizontal Blanking (HBLANK) and Vertical Blanking (VBLANK)—were originally designed for cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors to allow the electron gun time to reposition to the start of the next line or frame. In modern digital displays, the physical beam retrace is obsolete, but the timing intervals remain intact within digital video timing standards (such as CEA-861).

An HDMI transmitter takes advantage of these idle intervals. When pixel transmission halts between lines or full frames, the transmitter switches states to carry auxiliary payloads, specifically multi-channel audio data and metadata.

Operating Modes of an HDMI Link

An HDMI transmission cycles through three distinct operational periods across its data lanes:

  1. Video Data Period: The transmitter sends 8b/10b-encoded RGB or YCbCr pixel data.
  2. Control Period: The transmitter sends synchronization signals (HSYNC and VSYNC) and control preambles using specific 2-bit-to-10-bit patterns.
  3. Data Island Period: Occurring entirely within the HBLANK and VBLANK windows, this mode is used to send audio data, audio clock regeneration information, and auxiliary info-frames.

Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR)

Before raw audio can be embedded, the transmitter must solve the problem of clock domain mismatch. Video clocks (pixel clocks) and audio clocks (such as 44.1 kHz, 48 kHz, or 192 kHz) are rarely integer multiples of one another and operate asynchronously.

The HDMI transmitter continuously calculates and transmits Audio Clock Regeneration packets containing two values:

  • \(N\) (Numerator): A predefined integer matching the audio sample rate.
  • \(CTS\) (Cycle Time Stamp): A counter dynamically measured against the pixel clock.

The receiver uses these \(N\) and \(CTS\) parameters to reconstruct a jitter-free audio master clock (\(128 \times f_s\) or \(512 \times f_s\)) synchronized to the incoming video pixel clock.

Packetization of Audio Samples

Audio enters the HDMI transmitter via standard interfaces such as I2S or S/PDIF. The transmitter buffers these incoming linear PCM or encoded bitstream (e.g., Dolby Digital, DTS) samples and organizes them into standardized 32-byte Data Island packets.

Each 32-byte packet consists of:

  • Header (4 bytes): 1 byte for the packet type (such as Audio Sample Packet or Audio Clock Regeneration), 2 bytes for timing/sequence flags, and 1 byte of parity data generated by a BCH(32,24) error-correcting code.
  • Payload (28 bytes): Divided into four 7-byte subpackets. Each subpacket holds up to one audio sample (up to 24 bits) across multiple audio channels, along with validity bits, channel status bits, and parity bits.

Subpackets are protected by an internal BCH(38,28) error-correction algorithm to safeguard against bit errors across the physical HDMI cable.

Transmission and TERC4 Encoding

When the video stream transitions into a blanking period, the transmitter initiates the audio insertion process:

  1. Preamble and Guard Bands: The transmitter signals the start of a Data Island Period by transmitting a unique 8-pixel control preamble, followed by a two-character Data Island Guard Band to synchronize the receiver's decoders.
  2. TERC4 Encoding: Instead of the standard 8b/10b encoding used for video pixels, the transmitter converts the 4-bit nibbles of the Data Island packets into 10-bit symbols using TMDS Error Reduction Coding (TERC4). TERC4 uses a smaller set of highly differentiated symbols optimized to minimize electromagnetic interference (EMI) and bit errors.
  3. Multi-Lane Distribution: The packet bytes are serialized across TMDS Data Channels 1 and 2, while Channel 0 continues to carry timing information (HSYNC and VSYNC) along with packet header bits.
  4. Closing Guard Band: After the packet queue empties or the blanking interval ends, the transmitter closes the Data Island with a trailing guard band and transitions back to the Control Period or resumes the Video Data Period for active pixels.
