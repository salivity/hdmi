How Chroma Subsampling 4:2:0 Saves HDMI Bandwidth

Chroma subsampling 4:2:0 is a color encoding method that significantly reduces the data rate required to transmit video across an HDMI interface. By taking advantage of the human eye's physiological sensitivity to brightness over color, 4:2:0 reduces the resolution of color information while keeping brightness intact. This process cuts the total uncompressed video data payload in half compared to a full-bandwidth signal, allowing high-resolution and high-refresh-rate formats—such as 4K at 60Hz or HDR content—to travel reliably within the hardware throughput limits of HDMI standards.

To understand how 4:2:0 saves bandwidth, it is necessary to examine how video is split into components. Digital video is typically transmitted across HDMI using the YCbCr color space rather than RGB. In YCbCr, Y represents luma (brightness and contrast), while Cb (blue-difference) and Cr (red-difference) carry the chroma (color) information. Because the human visual system relies heavily on luminance to perceive fine lines, edges, and detail, the luma channel must be preserved at full resolution. Color resolution, however, can be aggressively reduced before the human eye detects a drop in visual quality.

Chroma subsampling notation is expressed as a three-part ratio, typically analyzed across a reference block of four pixels wide by two pixels high (a 4x2 grid of 8 pixels total):

  • 4:4:4 (No Subsampling): Every single pixel maintains its own discrete luma, Cb, and Cr values. In an 8-bit color environment, each pixel requires 24 bits of data (8 bits for Y, 8 bits for Cb, and 8 bits for Cr).
  • 4:2:0 (Subsampling): Full luma resolution is preserved across all pixels, but color is sampled only once for every 2x2 cluster of four pixels. Across a 4x2 grid, the top row takes two chroma samples, and the bottom row takes zero discrete chroma samples, instead sharing the row above it.

The resulting bandwidth reduction can be quantified through basic data calculation. In an 8-pixel block at 8-bit color depth:

  • 4:4:4: 8 pixels × 3 components = 24 bytes of data (an average of 24 bits per pixel).
  • 4:2:0: 8 luma samples + 2 Cb samples + 2 Cr samples = 12 bytes of data (an average of 12 bits per pixel).

By eliminating three-quarters of the raw chrominance samples, 4:2:0 achieves an overall 50% data rate reduction compared to full 4:4:4 video.

This 50% reduction has direct implications for HDMI transmission. HDMI interfaces operate under strict maximum bandwidth thresholds defined by their physical signaling rate—such as the 18 Gbps limit of HDMI 2.0 (operating via TMDS) or the 48 Gbps limit of HDMI 2.1 (using FRL). Uncompressed 4K video at 60 frames per second with 10-bit or 12-bit HDR exceeds the 18 Gbps capacity of HDMI 2.0 when sent as 4:4:4 or RGB. By applying 4:2:0 subsampling, the required transmission bitrate drops cleanly below the 18 Gbps ceiling, allowing the display and source device to establish a stable, artifact-free connection without needing physical hardware upgrades.
