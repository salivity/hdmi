Can HDMI Switch Audio Sample Rates Dynamically?

HDMI can dynamically transmit variable audio sample rates—such as switching from 44.1 kHz to 96 kHz on the fly—because the HDMI specification packetizes audio and relies on dynamic clock regeneration. While the interface supports these transitions natively without requiring a full video renegotiation, hardware constraints on the receiving end often introduce a brief audio dropout or silence as the digital-to-analog converter (DAC) locks onto the new sampling frequency.

How HDMI Handles Audio Sample Rates

Unlike analog connections, HDMI does not transmit audio as a continuous analog waveform. Instead, it embeds audio data into "data islands" located within the blanking intervals of the video stream.

Because audio is packetized, the HDMI transmitter defines the stream parameters using Audio InfoFrames and a system called Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR):

  • Audio InfoFrames: These metadata packets tell the sink device (such as an AV receiver or soundbar) the format, channel count, and sample rate of the incoming audio stream.
  • Audio Clock Regeneration (\(N\) and \(CTS\)): Because the audio clock is independent of the video pixel clock, HDMI uses two values—\(N\) (numerator) and \(CTS\) (Cycle Time Stamp)—to allow the receiver to reconstruct the exact audio clock frequency locally.

When an audio source changes from 44.1 kHz to 96 kHz, it updates the Audio InfoFrame metadata and shifts the \(N\) and \(CTS\) values to reflect the new target frequency.

The Real-World Experience: The Relocking Gap

Although HDMI dynamically accepts different sample rates without dropping the video signal, the transition is rarely seamless to the human ear.

When the sample rate changes:

  1. The receiving device detects the shift in the ACR parameters.
  2. The receiver's internal Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) must stabilize and lock onto the new clock frequency.
  3. Most audio processors momentarily mute the output to prevent loud popping or clicking noises during clock stabilization.

This process typically causes an audible gap in sound lasting anywhere from a fraction of a second up to two seconds, depending on the speed of the AV receiver or soundbar.

OS and Software Constraints

In practice, dynamic rate switching also depends heavily on the source operating system and software playback mode:

  • Shared Audio Mode: Operating systems like Windows, macOS, and gaming consoles generally force a single, static sample rate (usually 48 kHz) for all system audio. The OS internally resamples 44.1 kHz or 96 kHz streams to avoid constant hardware renegotiation.
  • Exclusive Mode / Bitstreaming: To trigger true dynamic sample rate switching over HDMI, the media player software must use an exclusive mode (such as WASAPI Exclusive or ASIO on Windows, or direct integer mode on macOS). In this mode, the playback software bypasses the system mixer, passes the native stream directly to the HDMI controller, and forces the HDMI link to adapt its sample rate dynamically to the source file.
