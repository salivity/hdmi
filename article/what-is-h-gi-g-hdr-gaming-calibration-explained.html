What Is HGiG? HDR Gaming Calibration Explained

High Dynamic Range (HDR) gaming offers striking visuals, but mismatched brightness standards between displays and gaming platforms often degrade image quality. The HDR Gaming Interest Group (HGiG) solves this problem by providing a standardized framework for HDR calibration. This article examines what HGiG is, why it was developed, and how it establishes reliable HDR calibration guidelines across HDMI connections to eliminate visual clipping and preserve creator intent.

What Is HGiG?

Formed in 2018 by leading display manufacturers and video game companies—including Sony, Microsoft, and LG—HGiG is not a hardware standard, HDR format, or proprietary technology. Instead, it is an industry collective that produces best-practice guidelines for HDR game development and display communication.

Unlike film and television, which use fixed brightness levels masterfully mapped to target formats, video games render scenes in real-time. Because displays have vastly different brightness limits (peak luminance) and black levels, game output requires precise adaptation to look correct on any specific screen.

The Double Tone Mapping Problem

To understand how HGiG functions, it is essential to understand the issue it eliminates: double tone mapping.

Tone mapping is the algorithm a device uses to scale an HDR signal so it fits the physical capabilities of a specific screen. When a gaming console maps a game's dynamic range and sends that signal over an HDMI cable, many modern televisions automatically apply their own dynamic tone mapping (DTM) on top of the console's output.

This dual-layer processing often leads to severe visual degradation:

  • Blown-out highlights: Cloud details, sun glare, and bright effects clip into pure white.
  • Crushed blacks: Shadow details disappear into solid darkness.
  • Color distortion: Luminance shifts cause saturation and contrast errors.
  • Input lag: Extra TV-side processing can increase latency.

How HGiG Calibrates Across HDMI Connections

HGiG solves double tone mapping by dividing responsibilities cleanly between the console and the display across the HDMI pipeline.

When you select the "HGiG" setting on a compatible television, the display disables its internal dynamic tone mapping. Instead of recalculating brightness frame by frame, the TV enters a pass-through state that hard-clips only at its true physical capabilities.

The calibration framework works across the HDMI connection through the following steps:

  1. Hardware Communication via EDID: Displays convey their basic performance parameters to connected consoles over HDMI using Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) or DisplayID. This informs the source device of the display's native capabilities.
  2. System-Level Calibration: Consoles like the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X/S implement HGiG guidelines via system-level HDR calibration menus. When the TV is set to HGiG, the user sets the console's minimum and maximum luminance markers to match the television's physical limit without interference from display-side algorithms.
  3. Source-Based Tone Mapping: With the accurate parameters established, the console handles 100% of the tone-mapping workload directly within the game engine. The console renders the scene, compresses the dynamic range to match the exact limits of the connected TV, and sends a final, ready-to-display image over the HDMI cable.

Key Benefits of HGiG Calibration

  • Accurate Artistic Intent: Highlights, mid-tones, and shadows appear exactly as the game developers intended.
  • Consistent Visuals: Brightness levels remain stable between different scenes without artificial fluctuations caused by TV algorithms.
  • Optimized Latency: Bypassing TV-side tone mapping algorithms reduces image processing pipelines, minimizing input lag for competitive gaming.

By shifting the tone mapping responsibility entirely to the source device and enforcing display transparency across HDMI, HGiG ensures predictable, accurate, and responsive HDR gaming performance.
