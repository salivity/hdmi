Why Disable Dynamic Tone Mapping for HGiG HDR Gaming

Disabling Dynamic Tone Mapping (DTM) on your display is essential for achieving accurate HDR gaming fidelity when utilizing the HDR Gaming Interest Group (HGiG) standard. While DTM is designed to artificially brighten scenes by altering dynamic range frame-by-frame, it interferes with the console-level tone mapping governed by HGiG guidelines. Turning off DTM—or setting your display strictly to HGiG mode—prevents the display from processing the image a second time, ensuring accurate highlight detail, consistent scene luminance, and preservation of the game developer’s original visual intent.

The Role of HGiG

HGiG is not an HDR format like HDR10 or Dolby Vision; rather, it is a set of industry guidelines created by gaming and display manufacturers. Under HGiG, the tone mapping process is offloaded entirely from the television to the source device, such as a PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X, or PC.

During system calibration, the console learns the exact minimum and maximum luminance capabilities of your specific display panel. When running an HGiG-compliant game, the console clips and maps the game's dynamic range to match those hardware limits precisely. This setup ensures that the signal arriving over HDMI already matches the exact optical limits of your screen.

The Issue of Double Tone Mapping

Dynamic Tone Mapping functions by analyzing incoming video frames in real time and dynamically adjusting contrast and brightness to make scenes appear punchier or more vivid. When DTM remains enabled alongside an HGiG-calibrated console, it creates a processing conflict known as "double tone mapping."

Because the console has already compressed the image to fit the panel’s capabilities, the TV receives an already-mapped signal. If DTM analyzes this signal, it treats the pre-mapped highlights as raw data and attempts to re-map them. This redundant processing distorts the gamma curve, frequently pushing mid-tones too high and shifting luminance values arbitrarily depending on what is on screen.

Preserving Specular Highlights and Contrast

The most noticeable benefit of disabling DTM is the recovery of specular highlight detail. When a TV applies DTM over an HGiG signal, it often pushes bright areas beyond the panel’s physical clipping point to boost perceived brightness. This causes fine textures inside explosions, clouds, sunlight, and reflections to blow out into flat, featureless patches of pure white. Disabling DTM allows bright elements to roll off naturally according to the display's calibrated threshold, retaining critical texture and depth.

Eliminating Fluctuating Luminance

DTM operates scene-by-scene or frame-by-frame. In gaming, where camera movement is rapid and unpredictable, DTM can cause visual instability. Swiveling your in-game camera toward a light source can cause the television's processing to abruptly dim or brighten the entire screen. Disabling DTM locks the tone curve in place, eliminating distracting brightness shifts and keeping the environment stable regardless of camera angle.

Achieving Creator Intent

Game developers master their environments with specific lighting conditions in mind to convey mood, scale, or gameplay mechanics—such as dark corridors that require flashlights. Enabling DTM artificially lifts shadow details, washing out black levels and ruining the intended atmosphere. By disabling DTM in favor of HGiG, dark scenes remain dark, high-contrast scenes maintain their punch, and the rendering pipeline produces the precise visual experience intended by the creators.
