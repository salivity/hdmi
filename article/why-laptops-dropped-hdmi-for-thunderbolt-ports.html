Why Laptops Dropped HDMI for Thunderbolt Ports

Modern laptop manufacturers have largely phased out native HDMI ports in favor of Thunderbolt ports to achieve thinner form factors, reduce internal hardware clutter, and offer superior multi-functional performance. While HDMI has served as the standard display interface for decades, its physical size and single-purpose nature conflict with the industry's push toward sleek, versatile, and high-efficiency portable computers. By adopting Thunderbolt—which utilizes the universal USB-C connector—manufacturers can deliver higher bandwidth, multi-monitor support, power delivery, and high-speed data transfer through a single compact opening.

Physical Space and Laptop Thickness

The standard full-size HDMI (Type-A) port is relatively tall and deep compared to modern chassis dimensions. As consumer preference shifted toward ultra-thin laptops and ultra-portables, internal vertical clearance became a major engineering bottleneck. The USB-C physical connector used by Thunderbolt is significantly slimmer, allowing manufacturers to reduce overall chassis thickness without compromising structural integrity or sacrificing space needed for internal battery cells and cooling solutions.

Multi-Functional Versatility

An HDMI port serves only one purpose: outputting uncompressed audio and video to an external display. In contrast, Thunderbolt is a converged protocol capable of handling multiple streams of data simultaneously over one interface. A single Thunderbolt port can:

  • Transmit video signals natively using DisplayPort protocols.
  • Transfer high-speed data using PCI Express (PCIe) and USB architectures.
  • Provide bi-directional power delivery (USB-PD) to charge the laptop or power external peripherals.

Replacing multiple legacy ports—such as proprietary barrel-style charging jacks, dedicated display connectors, and USB-A slots—with a few uniform Thunderbolt ports simplifies the overall physical layout of the machine.

Bandwidth and Display Scalability

Native HDMI implementations on laptops often lag behind current standards due to controller limitations, frequently capping outputs to a single 4K display at 30Hz or 60Hz. Thunderbolt 4 and Thunderbolt 5 offer massive bidirectional bandwidth—ranging from 40 Gbps to as high as 120 Gbps.

This high throughput allows a single Thunderbolt port to drive two or more 4K monitors at 60Hz, or even high-refresh-rate 8K displays, through native daisy-chaining or a single docking station. HDMI cannot natively support multi-display daisy-chaining from a single port, restricting multi-monitor desktop setups.

Simplified Internal Engineering

Routing traces on a motherboard for dedicated HDMI circuitry requires separate display controllers, passive components, and distinct power rails. Thunderbolt integration has been heavily streamlined at the silicon level, particularly by Intel, which integrated Thunderbolt controllers directly into the CPU package. This allows laptop designers to cut down on motherboard component count, reduce manufacturing complexity, and improve thermal and energy efficiency.

Future-Proofing and Ecosystem Consolidation

By standardizing on Thunderbolt over the universal USB-C form factor, hardware makers align their devices with a forward-compatible ecosystem. The port remains fully compatible with USB4, standard USB-C devices, DisplayPort, and, via inexpensive passive adapters or cables, legacy HDMI displays. This flexibility shifts the burden of legacy compatibility to external docks and cables, allowing the host machine to remain modern, minimal, and durable for years to come.
