How 8b/10b Encoding Works in Legacy HDMI

This article provides an overview of how 8b/10b line coding functions within legacy HDMI standards, spanning HDMI 1.0 through HDMI 2.0. It explains the physical layer mechanics of Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS), detailing how 8-bit video, audio, and auxiliary data are mapped into 10-bit symbols to minimize electromagnetic interference, maintain direct current (DC) balance, and ensure reliable clock recovery across high-speed serial links.

In legacy HDMI specifications, data transmission across the physical interface relies on TMDS, which employs a specialized 8b/10b encoding algorithm. The process transmits three primary data channels (typically carrying Red, Green, and Blue or color-difference components alongside embedded audio and packet data) synchronized to a dedicated clock channel. Before transmission across copper differential pairs, every 8-bit byte of payload data is converted into a 10-bit symbol.

The first stage of this encoding process focuses on transition minimization. An 8-bit byte enters the encoder, which evaluates whether XOR or XNOR logic produces fewer logic transitions (shifts from 0 to 1 or 1 to 0). Minimizing transitions dramatically reduces high-frequency electromagnetic interference (EMI) and decreases signal degradation over cable runs. A ninth bit is appended to the stream to signal to the receiver whether XOR or XNOR processing was applied, enabling exact reconstruction of the original byte.

The second stage enforces DC balance by generating a tenth bit. High-speed serial receivers depend on balanced signals where the aggregate number of transmitted ones and zeros remains approximately equal over time. Without DC balance, baseline wander occurs, shifting the signal voltage threshold and inducing bit errors. The HDMI encoder monitors the ongoing difference between transmitted ones and zeros—a metric known as running disparity. If the disparity tilts too far in one direction, the encoder inverts the first eight bits of the current 9-bit symbol and sets the tenth bit to indicate inversion, maintaining overall parity.

Beyond video pixels, the 10-bit encoding space provides critical out-of-band signaling. Because an 8-bit space yields only 256 combinations while a 10-bit space supports 1,024 combinations, the unused code words are mapped to control tokens. Legacy HDMI uses these specific 10-bit symbols to encode horizontal and vertical synchronization pulses (HSync and VSync), preambles, and data island markers during blanking intervals. Unlike video data symbols, these control tokens are deliberately designed with maximum transitions, enabling the receiver’s phase-locked loop (PLL) to quickly lock onto the signal and maintain clock synchronization.

While modern HDMI 2.1 introduced Fixed Rate Link (FRL) using 16b/18b encoding to support higher bandwidths, the TMDS 8b/10b scheme remains the foundational transmission protocol required for backward compatibility and operation up to 18 Gbps in legacy HDMI hardware.
