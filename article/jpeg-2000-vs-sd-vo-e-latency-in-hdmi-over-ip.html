JPEG 2000 vs SDVoE Latency in HDMI Over IP

When deploying HDMI over IP systems, latency is a critical performance metric governed largely by the compression technology used. This article examines the latency performance of JPEG 2000 versus SDVoE (Software Defined Video Over Ethernet), detailing how their underlying compression methods operate, their exact latency figures, and how this performance gap affects real-world audio/visual environments.

Compression Architecture: Frame-Based vs. Line-Based

The latency difference between JPEG 2000 and SDVoE stems primarily from how each technology processes video data.

  • JPEG 2000 (Frame-Based): JPEG 2000 uses discrete wavelet transform (DWT) technology to compress images mathematically. Because it compresses whole images rather than individual lines, an encoder must buffer an entire frame of video before it can process, compress, and transmit the packet over the network. The decoder at the receiving end must then perform the reverse process before displaying the frame.
  • SDVoE (Line-Based): SDVoE is built upon line-based compression technology (typically using the AptoVision chipset). Rather than waiting for a full frame to buffer, SDVoE processes and transmits video line by line. Video lines are packetized and streamed almost instantaneously across the network switch.

Quantitative Latency Comparison

The mechanical difference between full-frame buffering and line-by-line processing yields a substantial latency contrast:

Metric JPEG 2000 SDVoE
Typical Latency 17 ms to 50 ms (1 to 2+ frames) < 100 microseconds (< 0.1 ms)
Network Requirement Typically 1 Gbps Typically 10 Gbps
Processing Method Full-frame wavelet encoding Line-based streaming / uncompressed
Perceptibility Noticeable in interactive setups Zero-latency / Imperceptible

JPEG 2000 Latency Details

At 60 frames per second (fps), a single video frame lasts approximately 16.67 milliseconds. Because JPEG 2000 requires buffering on both the encode and decode ends, total system latency rarely drops below one full frame (approx. 17 ms) and commonly stretches to two or three frames (33 ms to 50 ms). If network jitter buffers or video scaling are introduced into the signal chain, latency can exceed 60 ms.

SDVoE Latency Details

SDVoE avoids frame buffering entirely for native resolutions. It transmits video at the microsecond level—generally under 100 microseconds (0.1 ms), which represents a fraction of a single video frame line. Even when performing scaling, color space conversion, or audio downmixing, SDVoE introduces virtually zero perceptible delay.

Real-World Application Impacts

The latency gap dictates which technology is suitable for specific AV environments:

  • Interactive Computing and KVM: Applications requiring real-time mouse and keyboard tracking (KVM) suffer significantly under JPEG 2000. A delay of 30–50 ms causes a tangible "spongy" or sluggish feel during cursor movements. SDVoE provides real-time response indistinguishable from a direct local HDMI cable connection.
  • Live Events and In-Room Monitoring: When projecting a live speaker onto an in-room display (image magnification, or IMAG), JPEG 2000's latency causes visible lip-sync mismatch and a noticeable echo effect between the physical speaker and the screen. SDVoE delivers the image instantaneously, eliminating lip-sync discrepancies.
  • Medical and Mission-Critical Operations: Operating rooms, financial trading floors, and high-stakes military control rooms require instantaneous visual feedback. SDVoE is standard in these environments because operators cannot tolerate the delay of frame-buffered codecs.
  • Passive Signage and General Distribution: For digital signage, background video distribution, or sports bars where viewers are not interacting directly with the system, JPEG 2000’s latency of 1 to 2 frames is generally unnoticeable. In these applications, JPEG 2000's ability to operate on cheaper, ubiquitous 1 Gbps network switches often outweighs the need for the sub-millisecond performance of SDVoE's 10 Gbps infrastructure.
