HDMI Velocity of Propagation and Signal Arrival Times

Velocity of propagation (VoP) measures how fast an electrical signal moves through a transmission medium relative to the speed of light in a vacuum. Inside an HDMI copper cable, VoP directly governs signal arrival times across all data and clock channels. Variations in VoP—caused primarily by differences in the cable's dielectric insulation and manufacturing tolerances—cause signal delay, intra-pair skew, and inter-pair skew. When arrival times drift beyond the strict tolerance limits of HDMI specifications, the receiving display experiences bit errors, audio-video desynchronization, or complete signal loss.

What Determines Velocity of Propagation in HDMI

Electrical signals do not travel at the theoretical speed of light (\(c\)) through a copper wire; instead, their speed is constrained by the electromagnetic properties of the surrounding insulation material, known as the dielectric.

The relationship between the velocity of propagation and the material's dielectric constant (\(\epsilon_r\)) is expressed by:

\[\text{VoP} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}}\]

In standard HDMI cables using solid or foamed polyethylene (PE) or polypropylene (PP) insulation, the VoP typically ranges between 65% and 75% of the speed of light (roughly \(1.95 \times 10^8\) to \(2.25 \times 10^8\) meters per second). A higher dielectric constant slows the signal down, whereas a lower dielectric constant allows the signal to travel faster.

Signal Arrival Time and Latency

The absolute arrival time (\(t\)) of a data packet at the display receiver is simply the physical length of the cable (\(d\)) divided by the effective speed of the signal (\(v\)):

\[t = \frac{d}{\text{VoP} \times c}\]

Over a short 1-meter cable, propagation delay is roughly 4.5 to 5 nanoseconds, which is negligible for human perception. However, across longer cable runs of 5 to 15 meters, total propagation delay increases proportionately. While absolute latency rarely affects passive viewing, predictable signal timing is vital for the digital processing engines within modern displays.

Differential Signal Arrival: Intra-Pair Skew

HDMI uses Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) in HDMI 1.4/2.0 and Fixed Rate Link (FRL) in HDMI 2.1. These architectures rely on differential pairs: two matched conductors carrying inverted copies of the same high-frequency signal (\(+\) and \(-\)).

The receiver reconstructs the digital data by measuring the difference in voltage between the two lines:

  • If the dielectric material is non-uniform or the twist rate causes slight length discrepancies between the two wires, the VoP or physical path length differs.
  • This discrepancy causes intra-pair skew, where one half of the differential signal arrives fractions of a picosecond earlier than the other.
  • As intra-pair skew increases, the differential signal becomes distorted, leading to phase cancellation, reduced eye height in the eye diagram, jitter, and unrecoverable bit errors.

Multi-Lane Synchronization: Inter-Pair Skew

An HDMI cable contains multiple high-speed lanes: three data lanes and one clock lane in TMDS mode, or four data lanes in FRL mode.

Inter-pair skew occurs when signals travel at different speeds down separate conductor pairs within the same cable jacket:

  • Physical variations in the dielectric foamer, insulation thickness, or twisting tension among separate pairs produce minor VoP variations between lanes.
  • High-definition video requires that pixel data distributed across multiple lanes arrives synchronously or within an acceptable deskew window.
  • HDMI receivers use FIFO (First-In, First-Out) buffers to realign mismatched arrival times across lanes. If the VoP discrepancy causes an arrival delay that exceeds the receiver's deskew buffer capacity, the link drops synchronization, manifesting as digital noise ("sparkles"), line drops, or a black screen.

Managing VoP in High-Bandwidth Standards

As standards advance to HDMI 2.1 delivering up to 48 Gbps (12 Gbps per lane), timing tolerances shrink to mere tens of picoseconds. To maintain uniform VoP and stable signal arrival times, manufacturers employ cellular-foamed dielectrics injected with inert gas (like nitrogen) to lower the dielectric constant toward 1.0, paired with precision back-twisting machinery to ensure structural and temporal symmetry across all twisted pairs.
