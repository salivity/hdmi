HDMI Compliance Testing for Noise Emissions

HDMI compliance testing evaluates both differential-mode and common-mode noise emissions to ensure signal integrity and regulatory electromagnetic compatibility (EMC). High-speed HDMI architectures—utilizing Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL)—must transmit data reliably without corrupting the signal or radiating excessive electromagnetic interference (EMI). Testing involves specialized test point adapters, high-bandwidth real-time oscilloscopes, and frequency analyzers to isolate, measure, and verify that differential signals stay within standardized eye-mask limits and common-mode voltages do not exceed strict electromagnetic emission thresholds.

Differential-Mode Noise Evaluation

Differential-mode signals carry the intentional data payloads across complementary line pairs (\(D+\) and \(D-\)). Noise in this mode manifests as variations between these two lines, directly degrading the data stream.

  1. Test Setup and Signal Acquisition: Technicians connect the HDMI source to an approved HDMI Compliance Test Point Access (TPA) fixture. High-bandwidth differential probes or matched coaxial cables route the high-speed data pairs to a digital storage oscilloscope (typically with an analog bandwidth of 16 GHz to 20 GHz or higher, depending on the HDMI specification version).

  2. Eye-Diagram and Mask Testing: Differential-mode noise directly causes amplitude distortion and phase jitter. The compliance software folds millions of acquired bit transitions into an eye diagram. The test evaluates the signal against standardized eye masks that define minimum horizontal (timing/jitter) and vertical (voltage amplitude) margins. Encroachment upon the mask indicates excessive differential-mode noise.

  3. Jitter Decomposition: The test system breaks differential noise down into deterministic jitter (DJ) and random jitter (RJ). Periodic jitter (PJ) and inter-symbol interference (ISI) are scrutinized to confirm that differential noise does not close the receiver's internal eye, which would cause bit errors.

Common-Mode Noise Evaluation

Common-mode noise represents identical voltage fluctuations present on both conductors of a differential pair relative to the ground reference. Because it drives high-frequency currents along the cable shield, common-mode noise is the primary source of radiated EMI that fails commercial regulatory standards like FCC or CISPR.

  1. Summation and Mathematical Processing: Rather than using a single differential probe that inherently cancels common-mode signals, the compliance setup captures each conductor of the pair individually using single-ended channels on the oscilloscope. The compliance software then calculates the instantaneous common-mode voltage:

    \[V_{CM} = \frac{V_{D+} + V_{D-}}{2}\]

  2. AC Common-Mode Voltage Limits: The HDMI specification defines strict peak-to-peak AC common-mode voltage limits (\(V_{CM-AC}\)). The software measures the peak-to-peak fluctuations of \(V_{CM}\) over a designated sample length to ensure that imbalances, driver asymmetry, or ground bounce do not push the voltage above allowed compliance boundaries (typically limited to tens of millivolts).

  3. Frequency-Domain Analysis (FFT) and Radiated Emissions: Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithms are applied to the calculated common-mode time-domain signal. This identifies dominant harmonic spikes matching the HDMI clock or data bit rate. Additionally, formal electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) testing places the operating device within an anechoic chamber to confirm that common-mode currents escaping through the cable do not radiate beyond regulatory thresholds.

Mode Conversion Analysis

A critical aspect of noise evaluation is mode conversion, where differential signals unintentionally convert into common-mode noise due to physical trace-length mismatches (skew), layout asymmetry, or parasitic capacitances. Compliance procedures utilize mixed-mode S-parameters—measured via Vector Network Analyzers (VNAs) or Time Domain Reflectometry (TDR)—focusing specifically on \(S_{CD21}\) (differential-to-common mode conversion). Minimizing mode conversion ensures that high-speed differential transitions do not transform into common-mode electromagnetic radiation.
