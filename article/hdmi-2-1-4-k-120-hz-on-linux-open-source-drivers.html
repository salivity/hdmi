HDMI 2.1 4K 120Hz on Linux Open-Source Drivers

Users attempting to run 4K displays at 120Hz via open-source graphics drivers on Linux frequently find their refresh rate capped at 60Hz. While modern graphics cards and displays physically possess the required hardware capabilities, a combination of legal restrictions, intellectual property governance, and technical protocol shifts established by the HDMI Forum fundamentally prevents open-source drivers from implementing HDMI 2.1's high-bandwidth capabilities.

The Shift from TMDS to Fixed Rate Link (FRL)

Displaying uncompressed 4K content at 120Hz with full 8-bit or 10-bit color requires a data throughput between 32 and 40 Gbps. Under the legacy HDMI 2.0 specification, signaling relied on Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS), which capped total bandwidth at 18 Gbps—enough for 4K at 60Hz, but insufficient for higher refresh rates.

To bridge this bandwidth gap, the HDMI 2.1 specification introduced Fixed Rate Link (FRL). FRL replaces TMDS by using up to four lanes operating at configurable data rates up to 12 Gbps per lane, enabling aggregate bandwidths of up to 48 Gbps. FRL also mandates complex link training algorithms, advanced forward error correction (FEC), and packet-based transport protocols to maintain signal integrity over physical cables.

The HDMI Forum's NDA and Open-Source Licensing Conflict

The primary barrier preventing open-source drivers (such as AMD's open-source amdgpu driver and the Mesa graphics stack) from supporting FRL is legal rather than computational.

The HDMI Forum, which governs the HDMI standard, strictly controls the HDMI 2.1 specification under rigorous Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs). Manufacturers and software vendors must sign these NDAs to access the technical documentation detailing FRL link training, register configurations, and signaling parameters. Crucially, the terms of the HDMI Forum's licensing forbid making the implementation details, registers, and protocols public.

Linux kernel drivers are licensed under the GNU General Public License version 2 (GPLv2). GPLv2 requires that all source code, logic, and hardware manipulation routines be fully auditable and publicly visible. Exposing the exact sequences and timing algorithms required to establish an FRL connection within an open-source codebase directly breaches the HDMI Forum's confidentiality agreements.

Failed Negotiations and Closed-Firmware Limitations

In an effort to resolve this standoff, AMD spent months negotiating with the HDMI Forum to find a viable technical middle ground. The goal was to determine if portions of the HDMI 2.1 FRL logic could be integrated into the open-source driver or offloaded safely. In early 2024, the HDMI Forum officially rejected AMD's proposal, stating that no open-source implementation of HDMI 2.1 features could be approved.

Offloading the entire FRL link-training logic to closed GPU firmware (such as the AMD Display Core Engine firmware) is technically problematic. Link training requires dynamic, low-latency interaction between the display driver, the display hardware, and the Linux kernel's Direct Rendering Manager (DRM) subsystem. Encapsulating this behavior entirely inside hardware firmware would require significant architectural redesigns that are neither practical nor supported by current GPU hardware designs.

Why Proprietary Drivers and DisplayPort Are Unaffected

Proprietary, closed-source drivers—such as Nvidia’s proprietary Linux driver—do not face this obstacle. Because Nvidia distributes its core driver as compiled binary blobs, it can sign the HDMI Forum's NDA and implement FRL without exposing the protected technical specifications to the public.

By contrast, the VESA DisplayPort standard is an open specification. DisplayPort 1.4 (via Display Stream Compression) and DisplayPort 2.1 provide equal or superior bandwidth to HDMI 2.1 without requiring non-disclosure restrictions on driver implementation. Consequently, open-source Linux drivers fully support 4K at 120Hz and beyond over DisplayPort connections, leaving HDMI 2.1 as an isolated exception restricted by closed-spec governance.
