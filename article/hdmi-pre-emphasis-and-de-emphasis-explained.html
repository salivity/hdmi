HDMI Pre-Emphasis and De-Emphasis Explained

High-speed HDMI signals degrade as they travel through copper cables due to high-frequency attenuation and dielectric losses, resulting in signal distortion known as inter-symbol interference. To combat this issue, HDMI transmitters use signal conditioning techniques called pre-emphasis and de-emphasis. This article explains how transmitter-side equalization counteracts cable losses, how pre-emphasis and de-emphasis function at the physical layer, and how these mechanisms ensure clear signal reception at the display.

Cable Losses and Inter-Symbol Interference

Copper HDMI cables act as low-pass filters. As data rates climb—reaching up to 18 Gbps in HDMI 2.0 (TMDS) and 48 Gbps in HDMI 2.1 (Fixed Rate Link, or FRL)—two primary factors cause signal degradation:

  • Skin Effect: Higher-frequency signals travel primarily along the outer surface of a conductor, increasing resistance at high frequencies.
  • Dielectric Loss: The insulating material surrounding the conductors absorbs signal energy, which escalates rapidly as frequency increases.

Because high-frequency components attenuate faster than low-frequency components, rapid signal transitions (edges) flatten out over distance. When a single bit does not have enough time to settle before the next bit begins, energy spills over into adjacent bit periods. This is called inter-symbol interference (ISI), and it causes the signal's "eye diagram" to close, making it impossible for the receiver to reliably distinguish between binary 1s and 0s.

How Pre-Emphasis Works

Pre-emphasis equalizes the signal by actively boosting high-frequency components before the signal leaves the transmitter.

In digital video streams, high frequencies correspond to transitions (changing from a 0 to a 1, or from a 1 to a 0), while low frequencies correspond to consecutive identical bits (such as a string of 1s or 0s).

When pre-emphasis is applied:

  1. The transmitter detects a bit transition.
  2. It increases the voltage swing for the first bit period following the transition above the nominal level.
  3. If the signal remains at that logic level for subsequent bits, the voltage returns to the normal level.

When this boosted transition travels through the cable, the channel's natural high-frequency attenuation reduces the boosted edge back down to nominal amplitude. As a result, the signal arrives at the receiver balanced across all frequencies.

How De-Emphasis Works

De-emphasis achieves the same relative frequency balancing as pre-emphasis, but it does so by attenuating low frequencies instead of boosting high frequencies.

When de-emphasis is applied:

  1. The transition bit (the first bit after a logic change) is driven at the full nominal voltage swing.
  2. Any subsequent consecutive bits at the same logic level are driven at a reduced, attenuated voltage.

In HDMI transmitter design, de-emphasis is frequently preferred over pure pre-emphasis. Driving transitions at higher voltages requires extra headroom, consumes more power, and can cause electromagnetic interference (EMI) or cross-talk between adjacent differential pairs. De-emphasis maintains the nominal peak-to-peak voltage limits while achieving the relative high-frequency boost required to overcome channel loss.

Physical Layer Implementation

HDMI transmitters implement pre-emphasis and de-emphasis within their Current-Mode Logic (CML) output drivers using a tapped delay line, functionally behaving as a Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filter.

A typical two-tap or three-tap FIR filter setup includes:

  • Main Tap (\(C_0\)): Drives the current bit value.
  • Post Tap (\(C_1\)): Evaluates the immediately preceding bit to adjust the current bit's amplitude depending on whether a transition occurred.
  • Pre Tap (\(C_{-1}\)): In advanced standards like HDMI 2.1 FRL, a pre-tap may be used to counteract pre-cursor ISI by shaping the signal immediately before a transition.

The transmitter’s internal digital logic continuously evaluates the incoming bitstream. When a transition occurs, auxiliary current sources inside the CML driver switch on or off. By summing or subtracting these auxiliary currents with the primary driver current, the transmitter dynamically modulates the differential output voltage across the termination resistors.

Equalization in HDMI Standards

In older HDMI implementations (HDMI 1.4/2.0 using TMDS), transmitters often use fixed equalization profiles designed to handle standard cable lengths.

Under the HDMI 2.1 specification, the Fixed Rate Link (FRL) architecture introduces a formalized link training process:

  1. The transmitter and receiver (sink) exchange capability information.
  2. The transmitter sends test patterns using predefined Transmitter Equalization (TX EQ) presets containing specific ratios of pre-emphasis and de-emphasis.
  3. The sink measures the bit error rate (BER) and eye opening, then requests specific adjustments until an optimal balance is achieved.

Through this coordinated filtering, the HDMI transmitter distorts the signal in the exact inverse shape of the cable's loss profile, delivering an open eye and error-free video to the display.
