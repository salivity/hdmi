HDMI 2.1 FRL 16b/18b vs TMDS Encoding Explained

The transition from older HDMI standards to HDMI 2.1 marks a fundamental shift in transmission architecture, replacing the legacy Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) with Fixed Rate Link (FRL) utilizing 16b/18b line coding. While TMDS served as the backbone for HDMI versions 1.0 through 2.0b, its 8b/10b structure and separate clock channel created hardware and bandwidth bottlenecks. FRL with 16b/18b coding substantially reduces transmission overhead, reallocates physical lanes for data delivery, embeds the clock signal directly into the data stream, and introduces robust error correction to support uncompressed 4K at 120Hz and 8K at 60Hz.

Coding Efficiency and Overhead

The most immediate technical difference between the two systems is transmission efficiency. TMDS uses an 8b/10b encoding scheme where every 8 bits of payload data are converted into a 10-bit symbol to maintain DC balance and minimize signal transitions. This results in a 20% protocol overhead, meaning only 80% of the raw bandwidth carries actual audio and video payload.

In contrast, HDMI 2.1 FRL implements 16b/18b encoding, mapping 16 bits of data to an 18-bit transmission code. This reduces link overhead to approximately 11.1%, achieving roughly 88.9% efficiency. This reduced overhead allows significantly more uncompressed data to pass across the physical medium at any given clock rate.

Lane Architecture and Clocking

Legacy TMDS uses four physical differential pairs: three dedicated data lanes and one dedicated physical clock lane. Because the clock runs on a dedicated lane, its frequency scales directly with the pixel clock of the incoming video format, capping total raw bandwidth at 18 Gbps under HDMI 2.0 (6 Gbps across three lanes).

FRL eliminates the dedicated physical clock lane entirely. Instead, the receiver relies on a Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) mechanism that extracts timing information directly from the transitions in the incoming data. By eliminating the dedicated clock lane, HDMI 2.1 repurposes the fourth physical lane as an additional data channel. FRL can operate across either three or four lanes at fixed, pre-negotiated link rates (3, 6, 8, 10, or 12 Gbps per lane), pushing total raw bandwidth to a maximum of 48 Gbps (12 Gbps across four lanes).

Data Transmission and Packetization

TMDS transmits uncompressed video synchronously in a continuous raster stream that mirrors the native timing of the display, dividing the timeline into video data periods, data island periods (for audio and metadata), and control periods during blanking intervals.

FRL replaces this format-dependent streaming with a fixed-rate, packetized structure. Video, audio, and metadata are packaged into standard FRL Character Blocks. Because the link operates at fixed speeds regardless of video resolution or frame rate, any unused bandwidth is filled with null or idle characters. This decoupling of physical transmission rates from source video timings provides greater reliability and simpler physical-layer transceiver designs.

Forward Error Correction (FEC)

High-frequency signals over copper cabling are prone to attenuation, crosstalk, and electromagnetic interference. TMDS does not include link-level Forward Error Correction (FEC); it relies entirely on physical cable quality and receiver equalization to maintain signal integrity, which limits reliable scaling beyond 18 Gbps.

HDMI 2.1 integrates Reed-Solomon Forward Error Correction (RS-FEC) directly into the 16b/18b FRL framework. The transmitter calculates parity data and appends it to transmission blocks, allowing the receiver to identify and correct bit errors without requesting retransmission. This error correction allows FRL to operate reliably across real-world cables at speeds up to 48 Gbps.
