How Source Clock Phase Noise Affects HDMI Audio

Severe phase noise in an HDMI source device clock fundamentally degrades digital audio transmission, leading to audible distortion, timing errors, and potential signal loss. Because HDMI transmits audio packets embedded within the video blanking interval rather than sending a dedicated physical audio clock line, the receiving device must reconstruct the audio clock using an Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) mechanism. When the source clock has high phase noise—meaning rapid, random fluctuations in the timing phase—the downstream receiver struggles to recover a stable clock, resulting in elevated jitter, buffer underflow or overflow, and analog conversion artifacts.

The HDMI Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) Bottleneck

HDMI relies on the video Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) clock to convey audio timing. The source generates Cycle Time Stamp (CTS) and numeric multiplier (N) values that describe the relationship between the audio master clock and the video pixel clock.

When a source device exhibits severe phase noise on its reference or TMDS clock, the timing of the transmitted packets and the stability of the CTS values fluctuate erratically. The sink device (such as an AV receiver, soundbar, or DAC) uses these values in a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) to synthesize the original master audio clock (MCLK). If the incoming signal has high phase noise, the sink's PLL either tracks the noise or fails to smooth it out adequately, propagating timing instability into the audio processing chain.

Audible Jitter and Sound Degradation

Phase noise in the frequency domain translates directly to jitter in the time domain. When this phase noise passes through to the Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) in the sink device:

  • Elevated Noise Floor: Jitter randomizes the sample conversion intervals, producing broadband phase modulation that raises the overall noise floor, reducing the effective dynamic range and Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR).
  • Harmonic and Non-Harmonic Sidebands: High-frequency phase noise mixes with the audio frequencies, generating unwanted sidebands around pure tones. This introduces harshness, graininess, and a loss of clarity, particularly in high-frequency transients and micro-dynamics.
  • Imaging and Spatial Distortion: In multichannel audio setups, inconsistent clock phase across decoded channels compromises stereo imaging and surround-sound spatial accuracy.

Buffer Errors: Pops, Clicks, and Dropouts

To tolerate small clock drift, HDMI receivers employ First-In, First-Out (FIFO) elastic buffers. However, severe phase noise can cause rapid frequency deviations beyond the tracking speed of the sink’s clock recovery loop.

When this occurs, the rate at which audio packets arrive deviates significantly from the rate at which the DAC consumes samples. This discrepancy leads to:

  • Buffer Underflow: The DAC requests a sample before one is available, resulting in micro-silences, clicks, or popping noises.
  • Buffer Overflow: Samples arrive faster than the buffer can discharge, forcing the receiver to discard audio frames, causing audible stuttering or skipped playback.

Complete Loss of PLL Lock

If the phase noise exceeds the capture or lock range of the receiver’s clock recovery circuit, the PLL will completely lose synchronization. Modern HDMI receivers typically respond to a lost clock lock by temporarily muting the analog output to prevent damaging high-amplitude digital noise from reaching the speakers. The listener experiences intermittent audio dropouts, extended silence, or continuous toggling between audio formats as the receiver repeatedly attempts to re-lock onto the stream.

Mitigation in Downstream Hardware

The severity of these symptoms depends heavily on the architecture of the sink device. High-end AV processors employ advanced jitter attenuation, such as Asynchronous Sample Rate Conversion (ASRC), multi-stage PLLs, or FIFO re-clocking systems with local crystal oscillators to isolate the DAC from the noisy source. Lower-cost sinks that use basic PLL circuits with wide loop bandwidths will pass the source's phase noise directly to the audio output, maximizing performance degradation.
