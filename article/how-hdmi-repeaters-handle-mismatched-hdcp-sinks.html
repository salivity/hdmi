How HDMI Repeaters Handle Mismatched HDCP Sinks

An HDMI repeater—such as an AV receiver, video splitter, or matrix switcher—acts as an intermediary device between a digital content source and one or more downstream displays (sinks). When downstream sinks possess mismatched High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) capabilities, such as one display supporting HDCP 2.2 and another only supporting HDCP 1.4, the repeater is responsible for negotiating the encryption handshake to ensure secure transmission without completely breaking playback. Depending on the sophistication of the repeater hardware, it manages these disparities through lowest-common-denominator fallback, active HDCP down-conversion, stream dropping, or independent port isolation.

The Role of the HDMI Repeater in HDCP Topology

In an HDCP-protected chain, a repeater acts simultaneously as a sink to the upstream source (like a media player or set-top box) and as a source to downstream sinks (such as TVs, monitors, or soundbars). The repeater collects the downstream topology, including the HDCP version, device count, and depth of the connection tree, and compiles this information into a downstream capability report that it communicates back to the original transmitter.

Lowest-Common-Denominator Fallback

The standard baseline behavior for basic HDMI splitters and repeaters without advanced processing is falling back to the lowest common denominator:

  • If a source outputs 4K content requiring HDCP 2.2, but one of the active displays only supports HDCP 1.4, the repeater reports HDCP 1.4 compliance back to the source.
  • The content source responds by either restricting the output resolution (often dropping from 4K to 1080p) to comply with HDCP 1.4 rules, or refusing to output the protected stream altogether.
  • Consequently, both the modern display and the legacy display receive the degraded signal, even though one monitor is technically capable of higher-grade encryption and resolution.

Active HDCP Down-Conversion and Decryption

More sophisticated HDMI repeaters and distribution amplifiers feature dedicated internal processing engines capable of active HDCP translation:

  • Independent Encryption Handshakes: The repeater establishes an HDCP 2.2 handshake with the upstream video source, allowing the source to transmit full-bandwidth, protected 4K HDR content.
  • Internal Decryption and Re-encryption: The repeater decrypts the incoming stream inside a secure processing environment. It then branches the video paths: re-encrypting the signal with HDCP 2.2 for the compliant sink, while simultaneously re-encrypting a secondary signal with HDCP 1.4 for the legacy sink.
  • Downscaling Pairing: In many instances, repeaters combine HDCP 1.4 translation with resolution downscaling (converting 4K to 1080p on the legacy port only), ensuring compliance with content protection rules while maintaining full 4K delivery on the primary display.

Independent Stream Routing and Isolation

In matrix switches and advanced multi-zone AV receivers, downstream ports operate independently rather than duplicating a single clock:

  • If different inputs are mapped to different outputs, the repeater enforces HDCP validation only along the active routing path.
  • If a single source is routed to multiple outputs simultaneously, an advanced switch can drop transmission to the incompatible display—showing an HDCP error message or a black screen on the non-compliant display—while maintaining uninterrupted playback on the HDCP-compliant screen.

Error Handling and Authentication Revocation

When an HDMI repeater encounters an unresolvable mismatch—such as a non-compliant HDCP 1.x device attempting to play content strictly flagged by the source as "Type 1" (which mandates HDCP 2.2 or higher without downstream conversion allowed):

  1. The repeater reports the presence of a non-Type 1 capable device in the downstream tree.
  2. The source halts transmission of the audio/video stream.
  3. The repeater outputs an on-screen display error or enters an unauthenticated state, cutting the video output to protect the source content from potential interception.
