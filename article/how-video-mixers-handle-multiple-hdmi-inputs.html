How Video Mixers Handle Multiple HDMI Inputs

Modern video production often requires switching between several cameras and digital sources on the fly without dropouts, glitches, or visual artifacts. Compact video mixers, such as the Blackmagic ATEM Mini series, make this possible by integrating advanced internal processing pipelines that automatically synchronize, scale, and format multiple HDMI signals in real time. This article explains the underlying hardware mechanisms—such as frame synchronizers, format converters, and digital multiplexing—that allow modern switchers to handle diverse HDMI feeds seamlessly.

Internal Frame Synchronization

Historically, switching between multiple video sources required an external synchronization signal known as "genlock" to ensure every camera sent video frames at the exact same microsecond. Consumer and prosumer HDMI cameras do not have genlock capabilities. Modern mixers solve this by placing a dedicated hardware frame synchronizer on every single HDMI input. When a camera feed enters the mixer, the incoming video frames are briefly stored in a high-speed memory buffer (RAM). The mixer’s internal clock reads the frames out of the buffer in perfect lockstep with its own system clock, ensuring that transitions between sources occur without rolling, screen tearing, or black frames.

Automatic Format and Resolution Conversion

Cameras and computers output a wide variety of resolutions and refresh rates, ranging from 1080p at 24 frames per second to 1080p at 60 frames per second, or even interlaced formats like 1080i. Advanced switchers include dedicated hardware up-, down-, and cross-converters on each channel. When a user defines the switcher’s operational standard (for instance, 1080p at 59.94 fps), the mixer automatically detects the incoming signal on each port and recalculates the spatial resolution and frame timing to match that target standard before the signal reaches the switching bus.

Color Space and Dynamic Range Management

HDMI devices transmit video across different color spaces, typically either RGB (common for computers and gaming consoles) or YUV/YCbCr (common for broadcast and mirrorless cameras). Switchers contain input processing chips that read the Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) handshake from the connected device. The mixer’s field-programmable gate array (FPGA) then translates the incoming color data into a unified internal color space (usually 10-bit YUV 4:2:2). This standardization ensures consistent color fidelity, prevents clipping, and provides the overhead necessary for keying, picture-in-picture processing, and digital video effects (DVE).

Audio De-Embedding and Routing

HDMI transports both video and multi-channel audio within the same digital stream. Upon entering the mixer, the embedded digital audio is separated (de-embedded) from the video signal. The audio tracks are routed directly to an internal digital signal processor (such as the Fairlight audio engine inside ATEM devices), where they can be equalized, dynamically compressed, delayed to compensate for camera latency, and mixed alongside separate analog mic inputs. Concurrently, the video signal proceeds to the switching matrix, allowing operators to either isolate camera audio or configure audio-follow-video (AFV) automation.

FPGA-Driven Real-Time Processing

The core of a modern compact mixer is typically a high-performance FPGA rather than a traditional software-based computer processor. The FPGA executes all operations—synchronization, scaling, transitions, graphics overlays, and multiview rendering—at the hardware level with near-zero latency (usually less than two frames of delay). This deterministic processing pipeline ensures that no matter how many HDMI inputs are active, the device maintains uniform responsiveness and visual stability.
