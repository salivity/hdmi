What Are MaxCLL and MaxFALL in HDMI HDR Streams?

MaxCLL and MaxFALL are foundational static metadata metrics used in High Dynamic Range (HDR) video to guide display tone mapping. This article explains the definitions and technical significance of Maximum Content Light Level (MaxCLL) and Maximum Frame Average Light Level (MaxFALL), followed by the exact mechanism through which HDMI carries these parameters from playback devices to modern displays.

Defining MaxCLL and MaxFALL

In static HDR formats such as HDR10 (governed by SMPTE ST 2086 and CTA-861 standards), mastering engineers include metadata describing the brightness boundaries of the encoded video content.

  • MaxCLL (Maximum Content Light Level): This integer value represents the brightness of the single brightest pixel found anywhere across the entire video program, expressed in candelas per square meter (cd/m²), commonly referred to as nits. MaxCLL ensures the display is aware of the highest peak specular highlight it must resolve.
  • MaxFALL (Maximum Frame Average Light Level): This value represents the highest average brightness of all pixels within any single frame of the entire video program, also measured in nits. While MaxCLL tracks an isolated peak pixel, MaxFALL reflects the most power-demanding, uniformly bright scene (such as a full-screen explosion or a bright, sunlit snowfield).

Displays use both values to execute dynamic tone mapping. If a television has a peak brightness capability of 800 nits, but a movie has a MaxCLL of 4,000 nits and a MaxFALL of 400 nits, the display’s internal processor adjusts its tone curve to compress the brightest highlights without dimming the overall scene unnecessarily.

How MaxCLL and MaxFALL Travel Over HDMI

HDMI transports MaxCLL and MaxFALL as static metadata packets rather than embedding them directly into the RGB or YCbCr pixel stream. This transmission occurs through specific protocol structures defined by the Consumer Technology Association (CTA) and the HDMI specification.

1. Packaging into the DRM InfoFrame

MaxCLL and MaxFALL are packaged inside a specialized HDMI data packet called the Dynamic Range and Mastering (DRM) InfoFrame, defined under the CTA-861.3 and CTA-861-G extensions.

The DRM InfoFrame payload contains several structured bytes:

  • EOTF Type: Specifies the electro-optical transfer function (e.g., SMPTE ST 2084 / PQ).
  • Mastering Display Color Primaries and White Point: Chromaticity coordinates for the display used during mastering.
  • Mastering Display Luminance: The minimum and maximum luminance capabilities of the mastering monitor.
  • MaxCLL: A 16-bit integer (occupying 2 bytes) representing the peak content light level in 1-nit steps.
  • MaxFALL: A 16-bit integer (occupying 2 bytes) representing the peak frame average light level in 1-nit steps.

2. Transmission During the Data Island Period

HDMI bandwidth is divided into two primary transmission states across its Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) channels: the Active Video Period and the Blanking Intervals (horizontal and vertical blanking).

MaxCLL and MaxFALL do not travel during the active video period. Instead, the source device (such as a 4K Blu-ray player, gaming console, or streaming box) transmits the DRM InfoFrame during the Data Island period within the blanking interval. Data Islands use a specialized 4-bit to 10-bit encoding (TERC4) with packet-error detection to ensure robust delivery alongside packetized multi-channel audio.

3. Sink Reception and Tone-Mapping Execution

Because HDR10 uses static metadata, the source device typically sends the DRM InfoFrame continuously or repeatedly at regular intervals throughout the stream. Once the receiving display (the sink) receives and unpacks the DRM InfoFrame, its scalar processes the MaxCLL and MaxFALL parameters to calibrate its tone-mapping algorithm, ensuring highlight detail and color saturation fit the panel's physical hardware constraints.
