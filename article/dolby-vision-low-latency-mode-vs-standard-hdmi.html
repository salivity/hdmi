Dolby Vision Low Latency Mode vs Standard HDMI

Dolby Vision delivers dynamic HDR metadata over HDMI using two distinct methods: Standard Dolby Vision (Display-led) and Low Latency Dolby Vision (Player-led, or LLDV). This article explains how HDMI handles both formats, detailing the transport methods, the handshake mechanisms between devices, and where tone mapping occurs within the video signal chain.

Standard Dolby Vision (Display-Led)

In Standard Dolby Vision, the display's onboard processor handles all tone mapping and image rendering.

  1. The EDID Handshake: During the initial HDMI handshake, the display advertises its Dolby Vision capabilities via the Extended Display Identification Data (EDID), detailing its peak brightness, color gamut, and processing algorithms.
  2. HDMI Transport via RGB Tunneling: Because native 12-bit YCbCr 4:2:2 with high-bandwidth dynamic metadata was not natively supported by early HDMI specifications without triggering compatibility issues, Dolby engineered "RGB Tunneling." The playback source packages the 12-bit YCbCr 4:2:2 video data and the frame-by-frame dynamic metadata into a standard 8-bit RGB 4:4:4 video signal container. To conventional HDMI repeaters and switches, this transmission appears as an ordinary 8-bit RGB signal.
  3. Display Processing: Once the display receives the signal, its proprietary Dolby Vision hardware demuxes the 8-bit RGB container, extracts the hidden 12-bit video stream and the synchronized metadata, and performs display-specific tone mapping tailored precisely to the physical capabilities of that panel.

Dolby Vision Low Latency Mode (Player-Led / LLDV)

Dolby Vision Low Latency Mode (LLDV) shifts the heavy computational burden of tone mapping from the display to the source playback device (such as an Apple TV, Xbox, or Blu-ray player).

  1. The EDID Handshake: The display advertises LLDV compatibility in its EDID, sending fixed target parameters to the source, including maximum luminance, minimum black level, and color primaries.
  2. Source-Side Processing: The source device reads the dynamic metadata of the content alongside the display's capabilities. It processes the tone mapping internally before transmitting the signal over HDMI.
  3. HDMI Transport: Instead of using RGB Tunneling, LLDV transmits the already tone-mapped image as a standard 10-bit or 12-bit YCbCr 4:2:2 signal. The stream uses standard HDMI Dynamic Range and Mastering (DRM) InfoFrames—similar to an HDR10 transmission—to notify the display of the HDR format.
  4. Direct Display Output: The display bypasses its internal Dolby Vision processing engine and renders the decoded video directly to the screen, resulting in lower processing latency.

Key Technical Differences

  • Tone Mapping Location: Standard processes tone mapping on the display; LLDV processes tone mapping on the source playback device.
  • HDMI Signal Structure: Standard uses a proprietary 8-bit RGB 4:4:4 tunneling wrapper to conceal 12-bit data and dynamic metadata. LLDV outputs an open 10-bit or 12-bit YCbCr 4:2:2 stream with HDR InfoFrames.
  • Latency: LLDV significantly reduces input lag because the display avoids secondary decoding and tone mapping, making it the primary profile for gaming consoles and displays with lower-powered internal chipsets, such as projectors and select televisions.
