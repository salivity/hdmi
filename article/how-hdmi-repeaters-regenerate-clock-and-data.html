How HDMI Repeaters Regenerate Clock and Data

An HDMI repeater prevents digital dropouts—such as screen flickering, pixel "sparkling," or complete signal loss—by actively restoring degraded high-speed audio and video streams over long cable runs. Rather than simply amplifying incoming noise, a repeater intercepts the weakened Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) signals, corrects phase distortion, strips out jitter through Clock and Data Recovery (CDR), and reconstructs pristine digital waveforms before transmitting them downstream.

Cable Attenuation and Digital Dropouts

HDMI signals consist of high-frequency digital pulses. Over long distances, copper cables act as low-pass filters: resistance and capacitance round off square-wave transitions, reduce signal amplitude, and introduce inter-symbol interference (ISI). Furthermore, phase jitter shifts the timing of the pulses relative to the clock. When the receiving display's decoder can no longer distinguish between a logical high (1) and a logical low (0) within the required timing window, bit errors occur, leading to HDCP handshake failures, dropped frames, or total picture loss.

Equalization

The regeneration process begins at the repeater’s input stage with an active equalizer. High-frequency components suffer more loss than lower frequencies over copper wire. The equalizer applies an inverse frequency response curve to the incoming transmission, boosting the attenuated high-frequency transitions while leaving the lower-frequency components intact. This flattens the frequency response, opens the collapsed digital "eye diagram," and restores the basic shape of the signal so the internal circuitry can read it reliably.

Clock and Data Recovery (CDR)

Equalization alone does not remove timing errors (jitter). To solve this, the repeater uses a Clock and Data Recovery circuit, typically driven by a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL).

  1. Clock Extraction: In HDMI 1.4/2.0 (TMDS), a dedicated differential clock line provides the baseline frequency, which the PLL locks onto. In HDMI 2.1 (FRL), the clock is embedded directly into the data lanes, and the CDR locks onto the bit transitions to extract the clock timing.
  2. Jitter Filtering: The PLL acts as a low-pass filter for phase variations, creating a completely new, rock-solid clock reference that is synchronized to the data rate but free of the phase noise accumulated along the cable.
  3. Resampling: Using this clean internal clock, the CDR circuit samples the equalized data stream at the center of each bit period, where the signal-to-noise ratio is highest. This locks the incoming bits into clear, unambiguous binary states.

Retiming and Signal Reconstruction

Once the raw bits are extracted, the repeater’s driver stage reconstructs the transmission from scratch. It generates new square waves with sharp rise and fall times, correct voltage swings, and standardized differential impedances. Because the data has been resampled by a stabilized PLL clock, the "jitter budget" is effectively reset to zero.

Output Transmission

The reconstructed data streams and clock are re-transmitted across the downstream HDMI link with full standard signal margins. By passing a mathematically clean, compliant signal to the display, the repeater ensures the display's internal receiver can decode every frame without bit errors, completely eliminating digital dropouts and maintaining continuous HDCP encryption.
