HDMI 2.1 Microstrip and Stripline PCB Routing Guide

Designing a printed circuit board (PCB) for HDMI 2.1 requires handling data rates up to 12 Gbps per lane across four Fixed Rate Link (FRL) lanes, totaling an aggregate bandwidth of 48 Gbps. At these microwave frequencies, PCB traces behave as transmission lines where parasitic effects, signal degradation, and electromagnetic interference (EMI) become primary design risks. This article outlines the critical microstrip and stripline routing practices necessary to preserve signal integrity, manage impedance, control skew, and minimize insertion loss in an HDMI 2.1 layout.

Impedance Matching and Stackup Architecture

HDMI 2.1 specifies a target differential impedance (\(Z_{diff}\)) of 100 \(\Omega\) \(\pm 10\%\) (often tuned tightly to \(100\ \Omega \pm 5\%\) for production margins) and a single-ended impedance (\(Z_0\)) typically between 45 \(\Omega\) and 55 \(\Omega\).

  • Solid Reference Planes: Microstrip traces must run over an uninterrupted, continuous ground (GND) plane. Stripline traces must be sandwiched between two continuous ground planes. Never route high-speed lanes across split power or ground planes, as this disrupts the return current path, spikes loop inductance, and causes impedance discontinuities.
  • Microstrip vs. Stripline Selection: Microstrip routing yields faster propagation velocities and eliminates via transitions when connecting the HDMI connector directly to the transmitter or receiver IC. However, stripline routing is strongly preferred for longer runs because the dual reference planes contain electromagnetic radiation, shield the differential lanes from external noise, and eliminate far-end crosstalk (FEXT).

Substrate Selection and Loss Mitigation

Standard FR-4 exhibits excessive dielectric loss (loss tangent or \(\tan\delta > 0.02\)) at 6 GHz (the Nyquist frequency for 12 Gbps FRL).

  • Low-Loss Dielectrics: Select low-loss, high-speed materials such as Isola FR408HR, I-Speed, Nelco N4000-13, or Panasonic Megtron 6, with a loss tangent below 0.005.
  • Skin Effect and Copper Roughness: At 12 Gbps, current travels entirely along the outer surface of the copper. Specify very low profile (VLP) or reverse-treated foil (RTF) copper to reduce conductor losses induced by surface roughness.
  • Fiber Weave Effect: Glass weave skew occurs when one trace of a differential pair sits over a glass bundle while the other sits over resin. Prevent weave-induced phase skew by routing traces at an angle (10° to 45°) relative to the PCB weave, using zig-zag routing, or specifying mechanically spread glass fabrics (e.g., 1067 or 1086 types).

Skew and Length Matching

HDMI 2.1 enforces strict timing budgets across the FRL differential pairs.

  • Intra-Pair Skew: The positive and negative traces of a single differential pair must be tightly matched. Keep intra-pair skew under 0.15 ps (approximately 1 to 2 mils or 25 to 50 \(\mu\)m in typical FR-4 variants). Any length compensation required due to package pin breakouts or connector geometry must be applied immediately adjacent to the point where the mismatch occurs.
  • Tuning Geometry: When adding serpentine compensation, use small jog heights (\(< 3\times\) trace width) and wide spacing to prevent parasitic capacitive coupling between adjacent loop segments.
  • Inter-Pair Skew: The length delta between different differential pairs (Lanes 0 through 3 and the legacy clock lane, if used) should be kept within 0.5 to 1.0 mm to satisfy HDMI 2.1 channel-to-channel timing constraints.

Crosstalk Prevention

  • Inter-Pair Spacing: Maintain a separation of at least \(3\times\) to \(5\times\) the dielectric height (\(h\)) to the reference plane between adjacent differential pairs (the \(3W\) to \(5W\) rule).
  • Stripline Shielding: In stripline configurations, maintain adequate spacing between adjacent high-speed pairs and insert continuous ground vias (stitching vias) along the routing corridors to suppress side-to-side coupling.
  • Isolation from Non-HDMI Traces: Keep high-speed HDMI 2.1 lanes well isolated from noisy signals such as switch-mode power supply (SMPS) switching nodes, clocks, and high-current traces.

Via Optimization and Layer Transitions

Vias introduce parasitic capacitance and inductance that can destroy the channel eye diagram at 12 Gbps.

  • Minimize Via Count: Ideally, keep the high-speed differential pairs on a single routing layer to avoid layer transitions altogether.
  • Ground Return Vias: When a signal layer transition is mandatory, place at least two GND stitching vias directly adjacent to the differential signal vias. This maintains a compact, low-impedance return current path through the stackup.
  • Via Back-Drilling: Eliminate via stubs by specifying back-drilling on through-hole vias, or employ blind and buried microvias. Unused via stubs act as open-circuit quarter-wave resonators, creating deep notches in the channel's frequency response.
  • Anti-Pad Optimization: Enlarge the anti-pad (clearance area on internal non-connected copper planes) around the differential vias to reduce parasitic shunt capacitance, which otherwise causes localized impedance dips below 85 \(\Omega\).

Component Footprint Optimization

Discontinuities often concentrate around surface-mount device (SMD) pads for AC-coupling capacitors, ESD protection diodes, and the HDMI Type-A connector.

  • Ground Plane Voiding: The physical width of SMT pads is typically wider than the 100 \(\Omega\) differential traces, introducing localized excess capacitance. Remove (void) the solid ground plane on the layer directly beneath the component pads (e.g., under the ESD diode or connector pads) down to the next reference layer. This increases loop inductance and restores the local differential impedance to 100 \(\Omega\).
  • Component Selection: Use low-capacitance ESD protection arrays designed specifically for ultra-high-speed interfaces, with parasitic capacitance \(C_{io} \le 0.2\text{ pF}\). Ensure the internal routing inside the diode package allows straight-through pad-on-trace routing without stubs.
  • Flow-Through Routing: Route traces straight through the pads of capacitors and ESD diodes. Avoid T-junctions or branch traces running to shunt components, as these small stubs create destructive high-frequency signal reflections.
