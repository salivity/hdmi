Why HDMI QMS Requires Constant Video Resolution

Quick Media Switching (QMS) eliminates the brief black screen—often called the HDMI "bonk"—that occurs when a display shifts between different playback frame rates, but it strictly requires the underlying video resolution to remain unchanged. This article explains how QMS uses Variable Refresh Rate (VRR) mechanics to switch frame rates seamlessly, why altering the display resolution breaks this mechanism by forcing a hardware-level clock renegotiation, and why maintaining a consistent pixel raster is mandatory for uninterrupted playback.

The Foundation of QMS: HDMI VRR

Quick Media Switching is built directly on top of HDMI 2.1’s Variable Refresh Rate (VRR) technology. In standard HDMI transmission, a fixed number of frames are sent per second using rigid timing parameters. When a source device switches from a 24 Hz film to a 60 Hz user interface, conventional HDMI requires the source and sink (display) to tear down the video link and establish a new clock frequency.

QMS avoids this interruption by keeping the underlying video clock and transmission link active. Instead of changing the fundamental link speed, it dynamically adjusts the vertical blanking interval (VBLANK)—the period of time between the end of one frame and the beginning of the next. By extending or shortening this interval, the source device can change how frequently active video frames are delivered without ever dropping the connection.

Why Changing Resolution Breaks the Process

While frame rates can be modulated by manipulating timing intervals between frames, video resolution dictates the physical structure of each individual frame.

  1. Pixel Clock and Bandwidth Allocation: Changing the resolution (for example, from 1080p to 4K) alters the total number of active pixels transmitted per line and per frame. This shift demands an immediate change in the pixel clock rate and the bandwidth configuration across the HDMI Fixed Rate Link (FRL) or TMDS lanes.
  2. Link Training and Resynchronization: When the bandwidth and data structures change, the display's timing controller cannot simply stretch an interval to adapt. The source and the display must undergo a full hardware handshake, known as link training, to lock onto the new data rate. During this handshake, video output must be suppressed, inevitably causing the screen to go black.
  3. Display Scaler Reconfiguration: Altering resolution requires the display's internal scalar and image processing pipeline to reset its frame buffers to accommodate the new raster dimensions, which inherently interrupts active display rendering.

The Requirement for a Fixed Raster

Because QMS utilizes VRR exclusively to manipulate time rather than space, the transport raster—comprising the horizontal resolution, vertical resolution, color space, and bit depth—must stay constant.

For QMS to function, the source device (such as an Apple TV or modern media player) must upscale or downscale all media to a uniform output resolution (typically 4K). By handling all resolution scaling internally on the source side, the HDMI connection remains locked to a single, stable format. QMS can then freely vary the vertical blanking intervals to transition instantly between 23.976 Hz, 24 Hz, 50 Hz, and 60 Hz content without causing the display to lose sync.
