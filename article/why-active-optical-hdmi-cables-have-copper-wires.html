Why Active Optical HDMI Cables Have Copper Wires

Active Optical Cables (AOC) use fiber optics to transmit high-bandwidth video and audio signals over long distances without degradation, yet they still incorporate traditional copper wiring inside their jackets. This hybrid construction is necessary because fiber optic strands only transmit light, not electricity. The copper conductors inside the cable are primarily responsible for delivering electrical power from the source device to the active microchips at the display end, while also carrying low-speed bidirectional control signals that are impractical to convert into light.

Powering the Active Optical Engine

At each end of an active optical HDMI cable sits a miniature transceiver system known as an optical engine:

  • The Source End (Transmitter): Converts electrical HDMI signals from a PC, console, or media player into optical pulses via microscopic laser diodes.
  • The Display End (Receiver): Uses photodiodes and amplifier chips to convert those light pulses back into electrical signals that the television or monitor can process.

These active components—especially the optical-to-electrical converter chips in the receiver head—require continuous electrical power to function. Because glass and plastic optical fibers cannot conduct electrical current, copper conductors must run alongside the fiber strands to deliver the required +5V power and ground lines directly from the source device's HDMI port to the receiver circuitry. Without these copper lines, the receiving end would have no power to decode the light signals unless it was plugged into an external wall adapter.

Handling Low-Speed Auxiliary Channels

Beyond supplying electrical power to the receiver, the internal copper wires handle essential HDMI handshake and control protocols. While high-speed video and audio data (TMDS or FRL lines) benefit immensely from the speed and interference immunity of optical fiber, HDMI also relies on several low-speed, bidirectional communication channels:

  • Display Data Channel (DDC): Facilitates the exchange of Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) and High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) encryption keys.
  • Hot Plug Detect (HPD): Detects when a display is connected or disconnected.
  • Consumer Electronics Control (CEC): Allows devices to control each other via remote (e.g., turning on a TV when a console boots).

Converting these low-frequency, bidirectional signals to light and back would require complex, expensive transceivers at both ends of the cable. Utilizing simple copper twisted pairs for these auxiliary channels is significantly more cost-effective, reduces latency during device handshakes, and avoids unnecessary signal conversion complexity.

The Hybrid Advantage

Active optical HDMI cables are fundamentally hybrid cables. By assigning high-speed, bandwidth-heavy data to optical fibers and reserving copper wiring for power delivery and low-speed control channels, manufacturers achieve thin, flexible, and interference-free cables capable of spanning dozens of meters without requiring external power supplies.
