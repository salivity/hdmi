Active vs Passive DisplayPort to HDMI Adapters

When connecting a DisplayPort source to an HDMI display, choosing between an active and a passive adapter determines whether your setup will work correctly. While both cables look virtually identical on the outside, the fundamental difference lies in how and where the digital signal conversion takes place. Passive adapters rely on the computer's graphics card to convert the signal, whereas active adapters feature a built-in semiconductor chip that performs the signal translation directly inside the adapter itself.

How DisplayPort and HDMI Differ

DisplayPort and HDMI do not use the same transmission standards. DisplayPort sends data in packets, similar to how Ethernet or USB connections work, using low-voltage differential signaling. HDMI, on the other hand, utilizes Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS). Because these communication protocols are fundamentally incompatible, a video signal must be translated from DisplayPort's packet format into HDMI's TMDS format before an HDMI monitor can display an image.

What is a Passive Adapter?

A passive DisplayPort-to-HDMI adapter is essentially a physical pin-converter with no internal processing hardware. It depends entirely on a feature in the source computer called Dual-Mode DisplayPort, often labeled as DP++.

When you plug a passive adapter into a DP++ port:

  • The graphics card detects the HDMI connection via the adapter.
  • The graphics card’s internal clock and hardware switch transmission modes, outputting an HDMI-compatible TMDS signal directly through the DisplayPort connector.
  • The adapter simply routes the pins to match the physical layout of an HDMI connector.

If your source port does not support DP++, a passive adapter will not transmit a video signal.

What is an Active Adapter?

An active DisplayPort-to-HDMI adapter contains an integrated circuit (a digital conversion chip) housed inside the connector casing.

When you use an active adapter:

  • The graphics card outputs its standard, native DisplayPort signal without needing to adjust its protocol.
  • The internal chip inside the adapter actively receives the DisplayPort data stream, decodes it, and re-encodes it into an HDMI TMDS signal.
  • The display receives a properly formatted HDMI signal regardless of whether the source port supports DP++.

Because active adapters handle the processing independently, they draw a small amount of power from the DisplayPort connector to run the internal converter chip.

Key Differences in Real-World Use

1. Multi-Monitor Setups

Most graphics cards that support DP++ only possess enough internal timing clocks to drive one or two legacy (HDMI/DVI) signals simultaneously. If you want to connect three or more monitors—such as in an AMD Eyefinity or NVIDIA Surround configuration—passive adapters will typically fail past the second display. Active adapters are required for multi-monitor arrays because they allow the GPU to output native DisplayPort signals across all ports, bypassing the GPU's legacy clock limitations.

2. Resolution and Refresh Rates

High resolutions and refresh rates (such as 4K at 60Hz, 120Hz, or HDR content) require substantial bandwidth. Many passive conversions are limited to 1080p or 4K at 30Hz because DP++ legacy modes are often restricted to older HDMI standards. Achieving 4K at 60Hz or higher almost universally requires an active adapter capable of converting DisplayPort 1.2 or 1.4 into HDMI 2.0 or 2.1.

3. Compatibility with Docks and KVM Switches

Docking stations, USB-C multi-port hubs, and KVM switches frequently lack DP++ support on their DisplayPort outputs. Using a passive adapter with these devices frequently results in a "No Signal" error. An active adapter guarantees compatibility across virtually any video source.

Which One Should You Buy?

  • Choose a passive adapter if you are connecting a single monitor, your computer’s port has the DP++ symbol, and you only require standard resolutions up to 1080p or 4K at 30Hz. Passive adapters are simpler and slightly less expensive.
  • Choose an active adapter if you are running a multi-monitor workstation, connecting via a docking station or KVM switch, gaming at high refresh rates, or outputting 4K at 60Hz or higher. An active adapter works in every scenario where a passive adapter works, making it the more reliable and future-proof choice.
