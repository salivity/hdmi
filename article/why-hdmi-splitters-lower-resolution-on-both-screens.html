Why HDMI Splitters Lower Resolution on Both Screens

When you connect two TVs with different native resolutions to an HDMI splitter, you may notice that your high-end display suddenly drops its picture quality to match the lower-resolution screen. This common issue happens because most standard HDMI splitters duplicate a single video signal rather than rendering two independent streams. To prevent the lower-resolution TV from displaying a blank screen or an "Unsupported Format" error, the source device and splitter default to the lowest common denominator between the two connected screens.

Understanding EDID and the HDMI Handshake

Every HDMI display contains Extended Display Identification Data (EDID). This is a small digital profile that communicates the monitor's capabilities—such as supported resolutions, refresh rates, and color formats—to your source device (like a gaming console, cable box, or media player).

Under normal circumstances, your source reads this EDID directly from the TV and outputs the best possible signal. However, when you introduce an HDMI splitter, the source can still only transmit one uniform video stream. The splitter must read the EDID from both displays and resolve the conflict.

The "Lowest Common Denominator" Rule

If Display A is a 4K TV and Display B is a 1080p TV, transmitting a 4K signal would cause Display B to show a black screen, as its hardware cannot decode or scale that resolution. Because basic splitters are passive passthrough devices without internal image processors, they handle this conflict by reading both EDIDs and signaling the media source to output 1080p. As a result, both displays receive a 1080p image, effectively downgrading the 4K television.

HDCP Compatibility Constraints

A similar issue occurs with High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP). Modern 4K content generally requires HDCP 2.2, whereas older 1080p screens often only support HDCP 1.4. If an HDMI splitter detects that one connected screen lacks the necessary HDCP version to display protected 4K media, the source will drop the resolution across the entire chain to prevent a playback failure.

How to Fix the Problem

To prevent a lower-resolution TV from compromising the picture on your primary display, you need hardware that can manage or alter the video stream independently:

  • Use a Splitter with Built-In Downscaling: Advanced splitters feature internal scalers. These devices accept a full 4K signal from your source, pass it untouched to your 4K display, and actively downscale the secondary output to 1080p for the older TV.
  • Choose a Splitter with Manual EDID Management: Splitters with physical EDID switches allow you to force the source device to output at 4K regardless of the secondary display. Note that the secondary display will remain blank unless it has its own downscaler.
  • Add an In-Line Downscaler: Placing an HDMI downscaler inline on the cable running directly to the 1080p television allows the primary splitter to run at full 4K while converting the signal right before it hits the lower-spec screen.
