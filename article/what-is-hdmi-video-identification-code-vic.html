What Is HDMI Video Identification Code (VIC)?

The HDMI Video Identification Code (VIC) is a standardized numerical value used in digital video systems to designate specific combinations of resolution, refresh rate, aspect ratio, and timing parameters. This article explains the origin and purpose of the VIC under the CTA-861 standard, how it abstracts complex display timings into simple integers, and how source devices and displays use it through EDID and AVI InfoFrames to establish a synchronized video connection.

What Is a Video Identification Code?

A Video Identification Code (VIC) is an integer defined by the Consumer Technology Association within the CTA-861 (formerly CEA-861) standard, which is incorporated into HDMI specifications. In digital video transmission, establishing a video stream requires both the transmitting device (source) and the receiving display (sink) to agree on dozens of low-level parameters.

Rather than continuously transmitting an entire table of signal timings, the standard assigns a static number to each standardized format. For example, VIC 16 maps to a 1080p signal at 60 Hz with a 16:9 aspect ratio, while VIC 97 maps to a 4K UHD signal (3840x2160) at 60 Hz.

How VIC Specifies Resolution

The VIC maps directly to active video geometry, defining:

  • Active Horizontal and Vertical Pixels: The exact addressable display area (e.g., 1920×1080, 3840×2160, or 1280×720).
  • Scan Type: Whether the image is progressive (each line drawn sequentially, denoted as "p") or interlaced (alternating odd and even lines, denoted as "i").
  • Picture Aspect Ratio: The intended geometric ratio of the active picture, such as 4:3, 16:9, or 21:9.

Because different formats can share the same pixel count but differ in aspect ratio, separate VICs exist for these variations. For instance, standard-definition 720x480p at 60 Hz uses VIC 2 for a 4:3 aspect ratio and VIC 3 for a 16:9 aspect ratio.

How VIC Specifies Timing Parameters

Resolution only describes the visible pixels. The underlying hardware requires strict electronic timings to synchronize the pixel stream. A VIC predefines all of these parameters in a standardized lookup table:

  • Pixel Clock Frequency: The rate at which individual pixels are transmitted, measured in megahertz (MHz).
  • Field/Frame Rate: The frequency at which complete frames are drawn (e.g., 24 Hz, 50 Hz, 59.94 Hz, or 60 Hz).
  • Blanking Intervals: The non-visible periods between lines and frames historically used for cathode-ray tube beam reset, now used in digital systems for data transmission (audio, metadata, and control packets). A VIC specifies:
    • Front Porch: The period between the end of visible data and the sync pulse (both horizontal and vertical).
    • Sync Pulse Width: The duration of the synchronization signal that aligns the scan lines and frames.
    • Back Porch: The period between the sync pulse and the start of the next line or frame of active video.
  • Sync Polarities: Whether the horizontal and vertical synchronization pulses are active-high or active-low.

By indexing these values, a source device outputs signals with exact microsecond tolerances without needing manual timing negotiation.

How HDMI Devices Use VIC

The exchange of VIC data occurs through two primary mechanisms:

  1. EDID (Extended Display Identification Data): When an HDMI display connects to a source, it provides an EDID block. Within the CTA-861 Extension of this block, the display includes a Short Video Descriptor (SVD) list containing the VICs it natively supports.
  2. AVI InfoFrame (Auxiliary Video Information): Once the source selects a supported mode, it transmits the video stream along with an AVI InfoFrame embedded during the horizontal blanking intervals. The InfoFrame contains the active VIC, informing the display precisely which predefined timing format is arriving so the display can immediately lock onto the signal.
