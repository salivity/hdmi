HDMI FCC Part 15 Radiation Limits Explained

This article examines how the HDMI specification addresses electromagnetic radiation under the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Part 15 rules. While the HDMI specification does not establish its own independent radiation thresholds, it mandates that all certified devices and cables adhere to national statutory EMC (electromagnetic compatibility) regulations, including FCC Part 15 Subpart B in the United States. To ensure devices satisfy these legal limits, the HDMI specification dictates strict physical layer electrical standards, differential signaling protocols, and cable shielding requirements designed specifically to minimize radiated emissions.

FCC Part 15 Subpart B Radiation Limits

FCC Part 15 Subpart B sets statutory limits on unintentional radiators—electronic devices that generate radio frequency (RF) energy during internal operation but do not intentionally broadcast it. Because HDMI devices operate in consumer environments, they typically must qualify under FCC Class B limits, which are stricter than Class A commercial limits.

For radiated emissions measured at a distance of 3 meters, FCC Part 15 Class B mandates:

  • 30 MHz to 88 MHz: 40.0 dBµV/m (100 µV/m)
  • 88 MHz to 216 MHz: 43.5 dBµV/m (150 µV/m)
  • 216 MHz to 960 MHz: 46.0 dBµV/m (200 µV/m)
  • Above 960 MHz: 54.0 dBµV/m (500 µV/m)

Because high-speed HDMI pixel clocks and data rates reach several gigahertz (up to 48 Gbps in HDMI 2.1), compliance testing routinely extends up to the 5th harmonic of the highest fundamental frequency, often reaching 40 GHz.

The HDMI Specification's Approach to FCC Compliance

The HDMI Compliance Test Specification (CTS) does not redefine or duplicate the decibel-microvolt limits established by regulatory agencies. Instead, the HDMI specification states that any product carrying the HDMI trademark must comply with the relevant local government regulations for electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) in the territories where it is sold.

In the United States, passing FCC Part 15 is a legal prerequisite for commercial distribution. An HDMI device that fails FCC Part 15 radiated emission testing cannot legally be marketed, effectively voiding commercial HDMI compliance.

Engineered Controls to Limit Radiation

To allow hardware developers and cable manufacturers to pass FCC Part 15 testing, the HDMI specification standardizes several physical-layer parameters to control RF leakage:

  • Differential Signaling (TMDS and FRL): HDMI uses Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) in HDMI 1.4/2.0 and Fixed Rate Link (FRL) in HDMI 2.1. By transmitting equal and opposite signals over tightly coupled twisted pairs, the electromagnetic fields cancel each other out, vastly reducing radiated emissions.
  • Transition Minimization: The 8b/10b encoding algorithm used in TMDS reduces the number of transitions (switching between 0 and 1), which directly lowers the high-frequency harmonic energy that creates radiation spikes.
  • Controlled Slew Rates and Rise Times: The specification enforces tight tolerances on signal rise and fall times. Preventing excessively sharp signal edges eliminates high-frequency RF components that easily radiate from printed circuit board (PCB) traces and connectors.
  • Connector and Cable Shielding: The HDMI physical specification outlines detailed mechanical requirements for 360-degree shielding around connectors and continuous outer braided shielding over internal foil-wrapped differential pairs. This prevents the cable assembly from acting as an antenna for high-frequency common-mode currents.

By integrating these electrical, mechanical, and architectural controls directly into the standard, the HDMI specification ensures that properly designed source, sink, and cable products maintain electromagnetic radiation levels well beneath FCC Part 15 ceilings.
