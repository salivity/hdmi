Can HDCP Keys Be Revoked Globally?

High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) contains a native, globally distributed revocation mechanism designed to disable compromised hardware. If the cryptographic keys of a specific HDMI receiver chipset are extracted or compromised, the licensing authority can revoke those keys globally using System Renewability Messages (SRMs). Once revoked, any compliant transmitting device—such as a streaming box, Blu-ray player, or gaming console—will refuse to send encrypted digital content to that receiver.

The Mechanism: System Renewability Messages (SRMs)

HDCP manages trust through Digital Content Protection LLC (DCP), the organization that licenses and distributes keys. Every authorized HDCP receiver contains a unique set of cryptographic keys accompanied by an identifier: a Key Selection Vector (KSV) in HDCP 1.x or a public key certificate in HDCP 2.x.

When a receiver's keys are dumped, cloned, or otherwise compromised, DCP adds the corresponding KSV or certificate ID to a cryptographically signed revocation list called an SRM.

How Revocation Propagates

Transmitters enforce revocation. Because millions of display devices lack direct internet connections, the HDCP standard relies on both online and offline vectors to distribute updated SRMs:

  • Physical Media: New commercial media, such as Ultra HD Blu-ray discs, carry the latest SRM embedded on the disc. When played, the player reads the SRM, verifies DCP's digital signature, and permanently writes the updated revocation list to its internal, non-volatile memory.
  • Operating Systems and Firmware: Devices connected to the internet—such as PCs, smart TVs, Apple TVs, and game consoles—receive updated SRMs through routine operating system, driver, or firmware updates.
  • Streaming Applications: Content platforms and DRM modules (like Microsoft PlayReady or Google Widevine) can push updated revocation tables directly to media players via software.

Once a transmitter stores an updated SRM, it inspects the KSV or certificate of any downstream HDMI receiver during the initial HDCP handshake. If the receiver's identifier matches an entry in the SRM, the handshake terminates immediately, and no protected video stream is transmitted.

Limitations of Global Revocation

While the architecture supports global revocation, practical enforcement faces distinct challenges:

  1. Shared Key Implementations: Ideally, every manufactured device receives a unique key set. However, manufacturers sometimes clone the same key set across entire product lines to reduce costs. Revoking that key invalidates every legitimate unit sold of that model, creating significant consumer backlash.
  2. Master Key Leaks: In 2010, the HDCP 1.x "master key" was calculated and leaked online. A master key allows bad actors to generate valid, unrevoked keys at will. Because DCP cannot predict which generated keys might appear, traditional KSV blacklisting becomes largely ineffective against determined hardware clone manufacturers.
  3. Offline Transmitters: Transmitters that never connect to the internet and never play newly pressed physical media will never update their internal SRM tables, allowing compromised receivers to continue functioning with those legacy sources indefinitely.
