SBTM and Dynamic HDR Compatibility Over HDMI

Source-Based Tone Mapping (SBTM) and dynamic HDR formats such as Dolby Vision and HDR10+ cannot actively apply tone mapping simultaneously to the same video pipeline over an HDMI link. While SBTM is designed to coexist within the broader HDMI 2.1a specification alongside dynamic HDR technologies, they represent two fundamentally different approaches to processing high dynamic range video. Consequently, an HDMI source and display must negotiate which device handles tone mapping, preventing both systems from operating concurrently on the same signal.

Understanding SBTM and Dynamic HDR

To understand why concurrent operation does not occur, it is essential to look at where tone mapping takes place:

  • Dynamic HDR (Dolby Vision, HDR10+): The source sends the video stream accompanied by dynamic metadata (frame-by-frame or scene-by-scene instructions). The display (sink) uses this metadata to tone-map the incoming signal according to its specific panel brightness, contrast, and color volume capabilities.
  • Source-Based Tone Mapping (SBTM): Introduced in HDMI 2.1a, SBTM shifts the tone-mapping responsibility to the source device (such as a PC, gaming console, or set-top box). The display communicates its physical capabilities to the source, and the source optimizes the output signal to match the display’s limits before sending it across the HDMI cable.

Why Concurrent Operation Is Not Supported

Tone mapping adjusts the dynamic range of an image so that it fits within the target display's physical boundaries. Applying two separate tone-mapping algorithms simultaneously results in severe image degradation:

  1. Redundant Processing: If a source uses SBTM to map highlights and shadow detail directly to the display's exact capabilities, the resulting signal requires no further adjustment. Applying a dynamic HDR tone curve at the display level on top of an already tone-mapped SBTM image would lead to double tone mapping, causing crushed blacks, clipped highlights, and inaccurate colors.
  2. Metadata Conflict: Dynamic HDR depends on metadata instructing the display how to adapt content. SBTM outputs content that is already tailored to the display's native parameters, rendering dynamic tone-mapping instructions unnecessary.
  3. Pipeline Arbitration: Over an HDMI link, the source and display establish an agreed-upon signal path during the HDMI handshake. The system either routes the signal through a source-side processing pipeline (SBTM) or passes raw content with metadata to activate the display’s internal dynamic HDR engine.

How SBTM Handles Mixed Dynamic Content

The primary purpose of SBTM is not to replace dynamic HDR for full-screen movies, but to solve the issue of mixed-format environments. In gaming and desktop environments, SDR graphics (such as user interface elements, chat overlays, or menu systems) often sit alongside HDR video or 3D graphics.

Instead of forcing the entire screen into an arbitrary display-side tone-mapping profile, SBTM allows the source device to:

  • Receive the display's peak brightness and color limits.
  • Internally combine SDR, static HDR, and dynamic HDR assets.
  • Map the composite image into a single, cohesive signal tailored specifically for the display.

In this context, the source may read the dynamic HDR metadata of a specific video asset internally, map it alongside other graphical elements, and then output a unified signal using SBTM. However, the transmission across the HDMI link itself does not run SBTM and display-led dynamic HDR concurrently.
