Understanding HDMI eARC Lip-Sync Correction

The mandatory lip-sync correction mechanism in the HDMI eARC (Enhanced Audio Return Channel) protocol ensures that audio and video remain perfectly aligned without requiring manual user intervention. Modern displays use complex video processing algorithms that introduce noticeable display lag, while external sound systems process high-bitrate audio at different speeds. By integrating an automated, bidirectional timing handshake into the eARC standard, the protocol eliminates the synchronization mismatches that commonly affected older connection standards.

The Problem with Modern Audio-Video Latency

Modern televisions perform extensive real-time video processing, including upscaling, frame interpolation, HDR mapping, and dynamic tone mapping. These visual enhancements introduce processing latency, delaying the moment a frame is physically displayed on screen.

Conversely, external audio gear such as AV receivers (AVRs) and soundbars process raw audio signals much faster than a television renders video frames. In earlier iterations of the Audio Return Channel (ARC), dynamic lip-sync correction was merely optional. Because many manufacturers chose not to implement it, the audio frequently played ahead of the corresponding visual action, forcing users to manually adjust millisecond delay sliders in their device settings.

The Mandatory Shift in HDMI eARC

Under the HDMI 2.1 specification, dynamic lip-sync correction transitioned from an optional feature to a mandatory requirement for any device certified for eARC.

If a device advertises eARC capability, it must support the automated exchange of timing data. This mandate guarantees baseline interoperability across different manufacturers, ensuring that an eARC-enabled television from one brand automatically synchronizes with an eARC-enabled soundbar or receiver from another.

How the Mechanism Operates

The lip-sync correction mechanism relies on the dedicated bidirectional eARC Data Channel (eADC), which runs over the existing pins previously allocated for the HDMI Ethernet Channel and standard ARC.

  1. Latency Detection: The television continuously monitors its own internal video processing latency. If the picture mode changes—such as switching from a high-processing cinema preset to Auto Low Latency Mode (ALLM) for gaming—the television updates its internal latency metrics.
  2. Timing Compensation Exchange: Through the eADC, the television transmits its current video latency values to the connected audio processor.
  3. Dynamic Buffer Adjustment: The audio receiver reads these timing packets and dynamically adjusts its internal audio delay buffer in real time, holding the audio signal for the exact duration required for the video frame to appear.

Real-World Benefits

By enforcing this mechanism at the protocol level, eARC reliably handles high-bandwidth, uncompressed formats—such as 8-channel uncompressed LPCM, Dolby TrueHD, and DTS-HD Master Audio, as well as object-based formats like Dolby Atmos and DTS:X—without timing drift. Spoken dialogue matches mouth movements automatically, regardless of changing frame rates, content resolutions, or display processing modes.
