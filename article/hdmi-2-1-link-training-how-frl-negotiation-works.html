HDMI 2.1 Link Training: How FRL Negotiation Works

HDMI 2.1 introduced Fixed Rate Link (FRL) technology to achieve bandwidths up to 48 Gbps, replacing the legacy Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) architecture. To ensure high-bandwidth video signals transmit reliably over varying cable lengths and qualities, HDMI 2.1 devices employ an automated calibration process known as link training. This article details what link training is, why it is essential for FRL stability, and the exact step-by-step negotiation protocol devices use to establish the highest possible reliable data rate.

What Is Link Training in HDMI 2.1?

Link training is an active communication and calibration routine performed between an HDMI source (such as a gaming console or graphics card) and an HDMI sink (such as a television or monitor). Unlike legacy TMDS, which used three data channels and a dedicated clock channel, FRL embeds the clock signal within the data lines across either three or four lanes.

Because high-frequency signals at up to 12 Gbps per lane are highly susceptible to attenuation, electromagnetic interference, and jitter, link training tests the physical channel before regular video data is sent. It adjusts equalizer settings and dynamically verifies that the bit error rate (BER) remains within acceptable thresholds, preventing issues such as signal drops, black screens, or visual artifacts.

FRL Bandwidth Levels

HDMI 2.1 defines six distinct FRL throughput levels across two lane configurations:

  • FRL1: 3 Lanes at 3 Gbps (9 Gbps total)
  • FRL2: 3 Lanes at 6 Gbps (18 Gbps total)
  • FRL3: 4 Lanes at 6 Gbps (24 Gbps total)
  • FRL4: 4 Lanes at 8 Gbps (32 Gbps total)
  • FRL5: 4 Lanes at 10 Gbps (40 Gbps total)
  • FRL6: 4 Lanes at 12 Gbps (48 Gbps total)

How Devices Negotiate the Highest Stable Data Rate

The link training process operates over the I2C-based Display Data Channel (DDC) and utilizes the Status and Control Data Channel (SCDC) registers alongside the high-speed differential lanes. The negotiation follows a structured sequence:

1. Capability Discovery (EDID & SCDC)

When a connection is established, the source reads the sink’s Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) and SCDC registers. The sink advertises its maximum supported FRL data rate, lane configurations, and whether it supports link training with or without dedicated training patterns.

2. Rate Request and Initialization

The source assesses the required bandwidth for the target video mode (resolution, refresh rate, color depth, and chroma subsampling). It then writes to the SCDC registers to request an initial FRL rate—typically the highest shared capability between the two devices. The sink prepares its internal clock recovery and equalizer circuits for the selected rate.

3. Transmission of Link Training Patterns (LTP)

Once both devices agree on the target rate, the source begins transmitting standardized Link Training Patterns (LTPs) across the active FRL lanes. These patterns consist of pseudo-random bit sequences (PRBS) designed to stress the physical medium and test for high-frequency loss, cross-talk, and inter-symbol interference.

4. Sink Evaluation and Feedback

While receiving the test patterns, the sink’s Adaptive Equalizer analyzes the incoming signal:

  • The sink continuously calculates the signal integrity and error rates.
  • If the signal is degraded, the sink adjusts its internal equalization parameters to compensate.
  • The sink writes updates back to the SCDC status registers, notifying the source whether each individual lane has achieved pattern lock or if errors persist.

5. Adjustment or Fallback

HDMI 2.1 link training operates closed-loop:

  • Success: If all active lanes successfully lock and maintain the required Bit Error Rate within the specified timeout period, the sink signals a "pass" condition in the SCDC register.
  • Failure and Fallback: If the sink fails to achieve lock within the standard time limit—often due to a sub-par or overly long cable—the link training fails. The source immediately drops to the next lowest FRL rate (for example, falling from FRL6 down to FRL5 or FRL4) and restarts the pattern test. If all FRL tiers fail, the connection falls back to legacy TMDS mode.

6. Transition to Active Video

Once the highest viable FRL rate is confirmed and locked, the source halts the Link Training Patterns and initiates the transmission of normal FRL packets containing video, audio, and metadata. The connection remains locked at this verified rate during standard operation, ensuring a stable, uninterrupted display signal.
