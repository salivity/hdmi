How HDMI 2.0 Increased Pixel Clock to 600 MHz

The transition from HDMI 1.4 to HDMI 2.0 increased the maximum pixel clock limit from 340 MHz to 600 MHz, expanding the total bandwidth from 10.2 Gbps to 18.0 Gbps. This architectural jump allowed displays to support 4K resolution at 60 frames per second with full 24-bit RGB/4:4:4 color. Rather than redesigning the physical connector or adding extra data lanes, HDMI 2.0 achieved this higher throughput over existing Category 2 (High Speed) copper cables through three core technical modifications: changing the clock lane signaling ratio, mandating pseudo-random data scrambling, and implementing active signal equalization.

Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) Baseline

Both HDMI 1.4 and HDMI 2.0 rely on TMDS technology across four shielded differential pairs: three data lanes and one dedicated clock lane. In TMDS, each 8-bit byte of color data is encoded into a 10-bit character to balance DC bias and limit transitions.

In HDMI 1.4, the maximum character rate (and effective pixel clock for standard 24-bit color) capped out at 340 MHz. Because each character is 10 bits, the maximum bit rate was:

\[\text{340 MHz} \times 10\text{ bits} = 3.4\text{ Gbps per lane}\]

Across the three TMDS data lanes, this produced a cumulative throughput of 10.2 Gbps. Pushing the pixel clock to 600 MHz meant driving data at 6.0 Gbps per lane (\(600 \text{ MHz} \times 10\text{ bits}\)), yielding the 18.0 Gbps total throughput of HDMI 2.0. Achieving this over legacy wiring required overcoming significant physical constraints, specifically high-frequency attenuation, crosstalk, and electromagnetic interference (EMI).

The 1:40 Clock Divider Mode

In HDMI 1.4, the physical TMDS clock line operated at a 1:1 ratio with the TMDS character clock (a 1:10 ratio with the bit rate). At a 340 MHz character clock, the physical clock line oscillated at 340 MHz.

Running the physical clock line at 600 MHz would have resulted in severe transmission loss and EMI compliance failures on standard cables. To resolve this, HDMI 2.0 introduced a dual-mode clock structure:

  • For rates \(\le\) 3.4 Gbps (character clock \(\le\) 340 MHz): The interface maintains standard HDMI 1.4 behavior with a 1:10 clock-to-bit ratio.
  • For rates > 3.4 Gbps up to 6.0 Gbps (character clock up to 600 MHz): The transmitter switches to a 1:40 clock-to-bit ratio.

Under the 1:40 ratio, the physical clock lane runs at one-fourth the character clock rate. For the maximum 600 MHz character clock (6.0 Gbps data lanes), the clock line operates at only 150 MHz:

\[\frac{600\text{ MHz}}{4} = 150\text{ MHz}\]

The sink device (such as a TV or monitor) uses an internal Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) to detect this signal, multiply the 150 MHz reference frequency by 4 to recover the 600 MHz character clock, and deserialize the 6.0 Gbps data streams.

Mandatory Data Scrambling

At 6.0 Gbps, repetitive bit patterns in video signals—such as blanking intervals or solid color fields—create concentrated peaks of RF energy. These spectral spikes frequently breach EMI limits and cause signal degradation due to adjacent pair crosstalk.

HDMI 2.0 resolved this by requiring a pseudo-random bit sequence (PRBS) data scrambler on all three data lanes whenever the pixel clock exceeds 340 MHz. The scrambling algorithm whitens the transmitted spectrum, dispersing radio frequency emissions evenly across a broad frequency band rather than concentrating power at specific harmonics. This reduction in peak energy allows high-speed transmissions to clear regulatory interference standards while maintaining signal integrity across unshielded or standard-grade twisted pairs.

Receiver Equalization and Eye Margins

Doubling the effective signal rate from 3.4 Gbps to 6.0 Gbps causes significant high-frequency roll-off (skin effect and dielectric loss) over standard cable runs, completely closing the data "eye" at the receiver's inputs.

HDMI 2.0 revised the sink's physical layer requirements, mandating Continuous Time Linear Equalization (CTLE) and Decision Feedback Equalization (DFE) at the receiver end. These active signal-processing techniques boost high-frequency components, suppress inter-symbol interference (ISI), and reconstruct the 600 MHz eye diagram from an otherwise degraded analog signal.
