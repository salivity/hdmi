What Is the HDMI Data Island Period?

The HDMI Data Island period is a dedicated transmission phase within the HDMI specification used to transport high-priority non-video data, including multi-channel audio, synchronization markers, and auxiliary metadata. By utilizing the brief pauses between active video rendering—known as horizontal and vertical blanking intervals—the Data Island period allows HDMI to deliver rich, synchronized audio and device configuration data alongside high-resolution video streams across the same physical Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) lines.

How the Data Island Period Functions

HDMI divides its signal transmission into three distinct periods: the Video Data Period, the Control Period, and the Data Island Period. While the Video Data Period carries raw pixel color information and the Control Period transmits synchronization signals (HSYNC and VSYNC) between active video frames, the Data Island period occurs specifically within the horizontal and vertical blanking intervals.

To guarantee high integrity for sensitive data like audio and configuration tables, the Data Island uses a specialized encoding scheme known as TERC4 (TMDS Error Reduction Coding 4-bit). TERC4 converts 4-bit data words into 10-bit symbols on TMDS channels 1 and 2, which provides superior noise immunity and error detection compared to standard video transmission. TMDS channel 0 carries the HSYNC and VSYNC signals combined with packet framing characters during this period to maintain display timing.

Data during this phase is organized into fixed-size, 32-byte packets consisting of a 4-byte header and a 28-byte payload, protected by Bose-Chaudhuri-Hocquenghem (BCH) error correction parity bits.

Non-Video Information Carried in the Data Island

The Data Island carries several distinct categories of non-video data vital for rendering, timing, and device interoperability:

1. Digital Audio Data

  • Audio Sample Packets: Carries up to 8 channels of uncompressed LPCM audio (at sample rates up to 192 kHz) or compressed multi-channel bitstreams such as Dolby Digital, DTS, Dolby TrueHD, and DTS-HD Master Audio.
  • Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR): Transmits precise timing values (Cycle Time Stamp, or CTS, and continuous factor \(N\)) that allow the receiving device to reconstruct the fractional audio clock from the video pixel clock without phase drift or jitter.
  • Audio Metadata: Carries Channel Status bits and Audio Content Protection (ACP) packets to govern sample rate indicators, copy management, and digital rights handling.

2. InfoFrames

Defined under the CTA-861 standard, InfoFrames provide the receiving display with critical contextual parameters required to decode and present the video and audio correctly:

  • Auxiliary Video Information (AVI) InfoFrame: Informs the sink of the source's color space (RGB, YCbCr 4:2:2, YCbCr 4:4:4), colorimetry (BT.709, BT.2020), aspect ratio, pixel repetition factors, and quantization ranges (Full vs. Limited).
  • Audio InfoFrame: Defines channel counts, speaker placement allocations, and downmix coefficients.
  • Dynamic Range and Mastering (DRM) InfoFrame: Transmits static HDR metadata (SMPTE ST 2086), including mastering display color volume, MaxFALL (Maximum Frame-Average Light Level), and MaxCLL (Maximum Content Light Level).
  • Source Product Description (SPD) InfoFrame: Identifies the source device by name and product type (e.g., game console, Blu-ray player).
  • Vendor-Specific InfoFrames (VSIF): Transmits proprietary or extended features such as 3D format signaling, Dolby Vision metadata, and HDMI Forum Variable Refresh Rate (VRR) attributes.

3. General Control Packets (GCP)

The General Control Packet handles immediate hardware control operations across the interface:

  • Deep Color Indication: Informs the receiver when video data uses higher bit depths (10-bit, 12-bit, or 16-bit per channel) rather than standard 8-bit color, directing the sink to adjust its pixel-unpacking pipeline.
  • AVMUTE: A hardware-level flag used by the source during resolution changes or stream initialization to silently mute video and audio output, preventing visible image artifacts or loud audio pops.
