Eye Diagrams for HDMI TMDS Signal Jitter Analysis

An eye diagram is an essential oscilloscope display used in high-speed digital electronics to evaluate the overall health, noise, and timing characteristics of a transmission channel. When applied to High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) lanes, the eye diagram overlays repetitive transitions of serial data to expose signal degradations. This article explains how an eye diagram is constructed and details the exact mechanics of using horizontal eye measurements to detect, quantify, and analyze deterministic and random signal jitter to ensure HDMI compliance and reliable video transmission.

Understanding the Eye Diagram

An eye diagram is formed by superimposing multiple segments of a continuous digital waveform onto a single time base, synchronized with a reference clock. As logic transitions—low-to-high, high-to-low, and static high or low states—accumulate on the display, they trace out an image resembling an open human eye.

A wide, clear center indicates a clean signal with high noise immunity and minimal timing distortion. Conversely, a degraded signal causes the "eye" to close, indicating potential bit errors due to noise, attenuation, or timing variations.

HDMI TMDS Signal Characteristics

HDMI utilizes Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) to transmit high-resolution video and audio. In traditional HDMI configurations (such as HDMI 1.4 and 2.0), the interface operates across three dedicated differential data lanes and one differential clock lane, running at gigabit-per-second rates.

Because TMDS uses high-frequency differential serial signaling, the transmission lines are vulnerable to physical impairments such as trace mismatch, impedance discontinuities, cable loss, and inter-symbol interference (ISI). Evaluating these physical layers requires looking beyond simple voltage readings and examining the temporal stability of every bit period (unit interval).

How the Eye Diagram Evaluates Signal Jitter

Jitter is the deviation of a digital signal's transition edges from their ideal positions in time. In an HDMI TMDS lane, excessive jitter directly compromises the receiver’s ability to sample incoming bits accurately. The eye diagram isolates and measures jitter along the horizontal time axis through several key parameters:

  • Crossing Point Thickness: The primary indicator of jitter is the width of the horizontal intersection points where rising and falling edges cross. In a perfect, jitter-free signal, all edges cross at a precise point in time, appearing as a razor-thin intersection. In reality, timing instability causes edges to arrive early or late, thickening these crossing bands.
  • Separation of Jitter Types: An eye diagram helps distinguish between Deterministic Jitter (DJ) and Random Jitter (RJ). Deterministic jitter, typically caused by bandwidth limitations and ISI, appears as distinct, split trajectories at the crossing points. Random jitter, caused by thermal noise and semiconductor behavior, appears as a Gaussian blur or spreading across the transitions.
  • Eye Width: The horizontal opening of the eye represents the available time margin for the receiver to latch data without error. Engineers measure eye width at the crossing voltage threshold to calculate total jitter, which is the unit interval (UI) minus the eye width.
  • HDMI Compliance Mask Testing: HDMI specifications define standard "keep-out" masks—typically a polygon placed in the center of the eye and boundaries at the top and bottom. Signal transitions must not breach the central mask. Jitter pushes the crossing points horizontally inward; if jitter is excessive, edges violate the mask boundary, signifying that the TMDS lane fails compliance and is prone to high Bit Error Rates (BER).

By analyzing the horizontal boundaries, edge thickness, and mask clearance of an eye diagram, hardware engineers can accurately characterize timing jitter on HDMI TMDS lanes and implement necessary equalization or board-level design corrections.
