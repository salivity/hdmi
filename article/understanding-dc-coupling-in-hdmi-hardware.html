Understanding DC-Coupling in HDMI Hardware

This article provides an overview of how direct-current (DC) coupling operates within standard High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) hardware implementations. It breaks down the physical-layer architecture of Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS), the roles of the transmitter (source) and receiver (sink), the specific termination voltage topology, signal swing characteristics, and the engineering trade-offs inherent in choosing a DC-coupled interconnect over an AC-coupled design.

The TMDS Physical Layer Architecture

Standard HDMI (versions 1.0 through 2.0) relies on Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) across four differential pairs: three dedicated to serial data (color channels, audio, and auxiliary data packets) and one dedicated to the pixel clock. Unlike interfaces such as DisplayPort or PCI Express, which mandate in-line capacitors to block DC current, standard HDMI TMDS lanes form a continuous, conductive DC path between the source and the sink.

Transmitter and Receiver Topologies

The physical layer uses Current Mode Logic (CML) to steer electrical currents across the transmission lines:

  • The Sink (Receiver): The sink device supplies the reference voltage and termination. Each incoming signal line is terminated through a 50-ohm pull-up resistor connected directly to a shared termination rail, designated as \(AV_{CC}\) (nominally +3.3V). The characteristic differential impedance of the transmission line is matched to 100 ohms (50 ohms per single-ended trace).
  • The Source (Transmitter): The source device typically contains an open-drain (or open-collector) differential transistor pair without internal pull-up resistors to the main power supply. Instead, it relies entirely on the sink’s pull-up network to draw operating current.

When an HDMI cable bridges the source and sink, it closes a direct electrical circuit that supplies current from the sink's 3.3V rail down to the source’s internal current sink.

Signal Generation and Voltage Swings

Because the transmitter is an open-drain current-steering architecture, the voltage levels on the differential pair are defined directly by the sink’s termination voltage and the transmitter’s switched current:

  1. Logic High State: The source turns off the drive transistor on that specific trace, allowing no current to flow. The trace is pulled directly up to the termination rail (\(AV_{CC}\)), establishing a single-ended high state at approximately +3.3V.
  2. Logic Low State: The source switches on the drive transistor, sinking approximately 10 mA to 12 mA of current through the trace. Across the 50-ohm termination resistor at the sink, this current creates a nominal voltage drop of 500 mV to 600 mV. Consequently, the single-ended low state settles at roughly +2.7V to +2.8V.

The resulting single-ended signal swings between roughly 2.8V and 3.3V, yielding a standard differential peak-to-peak amplitude between 400 mV and 1000 mV. Both lines remain heavily biased toward the 3.3V rail, maintaining a high common-mode voltage.

Detection and Electrical Safeguards

The DC-coupled nature of the bus also provides functional benefits for connection detection:

  • Sink Presence Detection: Because the source’s drivers lack internal pull-up power, the transmitter can monitor the high-speed lines to verify whether the sink's 3.3V termination rail is present. This works alongside the separate 5V line and Hot Plug Detect (HPD) pin to ensure hardware is securely mated before high-speed transmission begins.
  • Ground Reference Integrity: Because current returns directly between the devices over the cable's ground and shield conductors, DC coupling requires a stable, common ground reference between the source and sink to prevent ground-loop offsets from distorting the logic thresholds.

Differences in HDMI 2.1 (Fixed Rate Link)

While legacy TMDS operation remains strictly DC-coupled to maintain backward compatibility, HDMI 2.1 introduced Fixed Rate Link (FRL) mode to support higher bandwidths (up to 48 Gbps). FRL replaces the separate clock line with four high-speed data lines and allows for AC-coupling topologies using series capacitors, similar to DisplayPort, to mitigate high-speed common-mode shifts and improve signal integrity across long traces. However, all hardware supporting legacy HDMI backwards compatibility must retain the DC-coupled TMDS hardware architecture.
