Can HDMI Audio Support 384 kHz Stereo?

This article examines whether an HDMI connection can transmit high-resolution stereo audio files sampled at 384 kHz. While the HDMI specification theoretically supports audio sample rates up to and beyond 384 kHz when calculated across total bandwidth, real-world consumer hardware, operating systems, and receiver implementations almost universally limit Linear PCM (LPCM) transmission to a maximum of 192 kHz at 24 bits.

The HDMI Specification

From a purely technical perspective, the HDMI standard allows for 384 kHz stereo playback. Starting with HDMI 2.0, the specification expanded audio capabilities to support an aggregate audio sample frequency of up to 1536 kHz across all channels. Because a stereo configuration uses only two channels, transmitting 384 kHz audio requires an aggregate bandwidth of 768 kHz (384 kHz × 2), which falls well within the 1536 kHz boundary established by HDMI 2.0 and carried forward into HDMI 2.1.

Real-World Hardware and Software Limitations

Despite the specification allowing the bandwidth, practical support is virtually nonexistent in consumer environments:

  • Audio/Video Receivers (AVRs) and Soundbars: Almost all commercial AV processors, receivers, and soundbars feature HDMI controller chips capped at a maximum input sample rate of 192 kHz for LPCM audio. Even if an AVR's internal DAC can decode 384 kHz files, its HDMI input receivers will not accept a 384 kHz digital stream.
  • Operating Systems: Mainstream operating systems, including Windows, macOS, and Linux, restrict HDMI audio endpoints to standard consumer formats. The sound control panels typically max out audio output options over HDMI at 24-bit/192 kHz.
  • Source Devices: Dedicated media players, graphics cards, and streaming devices typically do not provide clocks or firmware profiles configured to output 384 kHz PCM over an HDMI link.

Common Behavior During Playback

If you attempt to play a 384 kHz stereo file through an HDMI connection, one of two things usually occurs:

  1. Downsampling: The media playback software (such as Foobar2000, Roon, or Audirvana) or the operating system's audio mixer automatically downsamples the stream to 192 kHz, 96 kHz, or 48 kHz to match the handshake negotiated via the display's or receiver's EDID (Extended Display Identification Data).
  2. Silence or Error: If the software attempts an un-resampled bit-perfect pass-through to an unsupported HDMI endpoint, playback will fail, producing an error or complete silence.

Alternatives for Native 384 kHz Playback

To listen to native 384 kHz / 32-bit or 24-bit stereo files without downsampling, HDMI is not the appropriate interface. Instead, use an asynchronous USB Audio Class 2.0 connection connected directly to an external digital-to-analog converter (DAC) that explicitly supports 384 kHz PCM decoding.
