How HDMI Displays Convert YCbCr to Analog RGB

Modern displays receiving an HDMI input often accept video encoded in the digital YCbCr color space to save transmission bandwidth. To render this signal on an analog-driven panel or legacy CRT, the display's internal video processor must read HDMI metadata, interpolate compressed color data, apply a reverse color space matrix to restore digital RGB values, and finally pass those values through Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs) to generate raw analog RGB voltages.

1. Packet Reception and Metadata Decoding

When an HDMI source transmits video, the data arrives as serialized digital packets over Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) channels. Alongside the video stream, the source sends Auxiliary Video Information (AVI) InfoFrames. The display’s internal receiver decodes these InfoFrames to identify crucial stream properties:

  • The color space format (YCbCr 4:4:4, 4:2:2, or 4:2:0).
  • The colorimetry standard applied (typically ITU-R BT.601 for standard definition, BT.709 for high definition, or BT.2020 for ultra-high definition).
  • The quantization range (limited range 16–235 or full range 0–255).

2. Chroma Upsampling

If the input signal utilizes chroma subsampling (4:2:2 or 4:2:0), the chroma components (\(C_b\) and \(C_r\)) contain fewer horizontal and/or vertical samples than the luma component (\(Y\)). Before color conversion can occur, the display’s scaler or video processor upsamples the chroma channels to a full 4:4:4 representation using interpolation filters, matching the resolution of the luminance channel.

3. Digital Matrix Transformation (YCbCr to Digital RGB)

The display’s digital signal processor (DSP) performs a hardware-level matrix multiplication to derive discrete red, green, and blue values from luminance and color-difference values.

For high-definition video using the standard BT.709 matrix, the normalized conversion equations applied in hardware logic are:

  • \(R = Y + 1.5748 \cdot (C_r - 128)\)
  • \(G = Y - 0.1873 \cdot (C_b - 128) - 0.4681 \cdot (C_r - 128)\)
  • \(B = Y + 1.8556 \cdot (C_b - 128)\)

Hardware lookup tables (LUTs) or multiply-accumulate (MAC) units execute these calculations at pixel-clock speeds, outputting parallel digital RGB bitstreams (typically 8-bit, 10-bit, or 12-bit per color channel).

4. Digital-to-Analog Conversion (DAC)

Once the signal exists as digital RGB, it is routed to three dedicated, high-speed Digital-to-Analog Converters—one each for Red, Green, and Blue. The DAC takes the binary numeric values and translates them into proportional, continuous voltage levels.

For standard analog video interfaces (such as internal VGA stages or CRT drivers), these output levels typically map to a standard scale ranging from 0.0V (absolute black) to 0.7V (peak white) across a 75-ohm termination load.

5. Final Analog Amplification and Driving

The continuous analog RGB signals are passed through low-pass reconstruction filters to eliminate high-frequency quantization noise introduced by the DACs. Video output amplifiers then boost these weak voltage signals to the physical levels required to drive the display hardware—whether that involves modulating electron gun cathodes in a CRT or setting the analog bias voltages across liquid crystal subpixels in an analog-driven LCD panel.
