HDMI 5V Overcurrent Protection Circuit Design

Hardware designers must protect the HDMI +5V power rail (Pin 18) from short circuits, miswired cables, and excessive current demands from connected sink devices. This article examines the standard current-limiting protection circuits used on HDMI source ports, detailing the implementation, advantages, and trade-offs of Polymeric Positive Temperature Coefficient (PTC) devices, dedicated power distribution switches (load switches), integrated port protection ICs, and discrete current-limiting topologies.

The HDMI 5V Rail Requirements

The HDMI specification mandates that a source device provide a +5V output on Pin 18 capable of supplying at least 55 mA to power the Display Data Channel (DDC) circuitry and High-Definition Content Protection (HDCP) EEPROM in the sink device. Modern applications—such as HDMI dongles, active optical cables (AOCs), and signal splitters—routinely draw closer to 200 mA to 500 mA. To comply with safety standards such as IEC/UL 62368-1 and prevent board damage during cable shorts, the source must implement overcurrent protection that typically caps output current between 0.5 A and 1.5 A.

PTC Resettable Fuses (Polyfuses)

A Polymeric Positive Temperature Coefficient (PTC) thermistor is the most basic and low-cost solution for HDMI 5V current limiting. Placed in series with the 5V power supply, the PTC exhibits low resistance under normal operating conditions. When an overcurrent or short-circuit event occurs, resistive self-heating forces the polymer matrix to expand, causing its resistance to increase sharply and limiting current to a safe trickle.

  • Advantages: Low component cost, minimal PCB footprint, and passive, autonomous reset capability once the fault and heat dissipate.
  • Disadvantages: Slow trip times (often milliseconds to seconds), significant voltage drop across the internal resistance (\(R_{ON}\)) under heavy loads, and a high sensitivity to ambient operating temperature.

Dedicated Power Distribution Switches (Load Switches)

Integrated power distribution switches, often referred to as eFuses or intelligent load switches, are the current industry standard for HDMI source design. Devices like the TI TPS20xx series or Diodes Inc. AP21xx series feature an internal high-side P-channel or N-channel MOSFET managed by integrated current-sensing and control circuitry.

These ICs offer:

  • Accurate Current Clamping: The switch acts as a constant-current source during an overload, actively regulating the maximum delivered current to a fixed threshold (e.g., 500 mA or 1.0 A) or a threshold defined by an external resistor.
  • Fast Response Time: Current-limiting circuits engage within microseconds, protecting sensitive power management ICs (PMICs) and upstream voltage regulators from voltage dips.
  • Reverse Current Blocking: Prevents current from flowing back into the source system if an externally powered HDMI sink supplies an improper voltage onto Pin 18.
  • Fault Reporting: An active-low open-drain flag pin (\(\overline{\text{FAULT}}\) or \(\overline{\text{OC}}\)) alerts the host processor to take software-level protective actions.
  • Thermal Shutdown: If an extended short causes the internal junction temperature to exceed safety margins (typically around 135°C to 150°C), the device shuts off entirely until it cools down.

Integrated HDMI Interface Protection ICs

For high-density boards, designers often utilize application-specific interface protection ICs (such as those from Nexperia, STMicroelectronics, or Semtech). These chips combine high-speed electrostatic discharge (ESD) protection diodes for Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) lanes, I²C level-shifting buffers for the DDC lines, and an integrated current-limited 5V power switch into a single package.

Using an integrated solution simplifies routing around the dense HDMI connector, reduces parasitic inductance, and guarantees that the 5V current-limiting response aligns with HDMI compliance testing while satisfying space-constrained layout constraints.

Discrete Transistor Current Limiters

In specialized, cost-sensitive designs where standard load switches are unavailable, designers may implement a discrete current limiter using bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) or a combination of a BJT and a P-channel MOSFET.

In a standard two-transistor limiter, a small current-sense resistor sits in series with the rail. When the current exceeds the designed limit, the voltage drop across the resistor reaches approximately 0.6 V to 0.7 V, turning on a sensing BJT. This transistor then pulls up the gate of the pass MOSFET (or steals base drive from the pass BJT), choking the conduction path and holding the output to a safe current limit. While inexpensive, this topology consumes more board space and exhibits wider current threshold tolerances due to temperature-induced variations in the BJT base-emitter forward voltage (\(V_{BE}\)).
