Dielectric Constant of HDMI Cable Insulation

The insulating material between the high-speed conductors in an HDMI cable typically features a dielectric constant (\(\varepsilon_r\) or Dk) ranging between 1.5 and 2.3, depending on whether solid or foamed insulation is used. Foamed polyethylene (Foam PE) is the industry standard for the high-speed Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) and Fixed Rate Link (FRL) lines, yielding a low dielectric constant between 1.5 and 1.9. This article details the specific values for common insulation materials used in HDMI cables, the reasons behind their selection, and how the dielectric constant impacts cable performance.

Typical Dielectric Constant Values by Material

HDMI cables contain multiple twisted conductor pairs, each wrapped in a primary dielectric to maintain electrical separation and signal integrity. The dielectric constants for the primary insulations used include:

  • Foamed Polyethylene (Cellular PE / Skin-Foam-Skin PE): 1.5 to 1.9
    This is the most common material used for high-speed differential pairs in HDMI 2.0 and HDMI 2.1 cables. Gas (usually nitrogen) is injected into the polyethylene during extrusion, creating micro-air pockets. Because air has a dielectric constant of approximately 1.0, this foaming process significantly reduces the overall effective dielectric constant.
  • Solid Polyethylene (Solid PE): 2.25 to 2.35
    Often utilized in lower-tier cables, non-critical sub-channels (such as the DDC or utility lines), or legacy HDMI cables that operate at lower transmission frequencies.
  • Fluorinated Ethylene Propylene (FEP / Teflon): 2.1
    Occasionally used in specialized, high-temperature, or premium ultra-thin cables for its low dissipation factor and thermal resilience.
  • Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC): 3.0 to 4.5
    PVC is rarely used directly between the high-speed differential conductors due to its high dielectric constant and loss tangent. Instead, it is predominantly reserved for the outer cable jacket.

Why the Dielectric Constant Matters in HDMI Design

The dielectric constant directly influences the electrical characteristics required to comply with HDMI Forum and HDMI Licensing Administrator standards:

  1. Impedance Control: HDMI specifications require the high-speed differential pairs to have a nominal differential impedance of 100 ohms ± 10%. A lower dielectric constant allows manufacturers to maintain proper conductor spacing and keep the cable flexible while meeting the 100-ohm threshold.
  2. Signal Propagation Velocity: Signal velocity is inversely proportional to the square root of the dielectric constant (\(V = c / \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\)). Lower values mean the electrical signals travel closer to the speed of light, which minimizes propagation delay and reduces intra-pair skew (timing mismatch between positive and negative lines in a pair).
  3. Signal Attenuation: Materials with lower dielectric constants generally exhibit a lower dielectric dissipation factor (\(\tan\delta\)). This reduces high-frequency dielectric losses, preserving signal amplitude and edge steepness over distances up to several meters without requiring active amplification.
