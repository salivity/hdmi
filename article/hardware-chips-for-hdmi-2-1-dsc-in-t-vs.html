Hardware Chips for HDMI 2.1 DSC in TVs

Display Stream Compression (DSC) over HDMI 2.1 enables televisions to receive visually lossless, ultra-high-resolution, and high-refresh-rate video signals—such as 4K at 144Hz or 8K at 60Hz—without exceeding interface bandwidth limits. This article examines the internal silicon architecture required to decode DSC on modern televisions, outlining the specific roles of the HDMI 2.1 receiver physical layer (PHY), integrated System-on-Chips (SoCs), proprietary custom silicon, and dedicated hardware decompression engines.

Integrated TV SoCs vs. Dedicated Bridges

In modern television architecture, DSC 1.2a decoding is rarely handled by a single, standalone microchip. Instead, it is integrated directly into the television's primary video processing System-on-Chip (SoC) or managed via a dedicated HDMI 2.1 receiver bridge before passing uncompressed pixel data to the panel timing controller (T-Con).

To process DSC over HDMI 2.1, the internal silicon must feature a dedicated hardware-based VESA DSC decompression engine paired with a Fixed Rate Link (FRL) physical interface. Software-based decoding cannot be used because DSC must be decompressed in real time with virtually zero latency (often less than a single scan line) to prevent display lag and audio-video desynchronization.

Leading Commercial TV SoCs with Native DSC Support

Most smart TV manufacturers license or purchase commercial SoCs that combine the HDMI 2.1 physical receiver, the DSC hardware decompression pipeline, and central image processing onto a single die:

  • MediaTek Pentonic Series (Pentonic 700, 1000, and 2000): These are the most common commercial chips handling DSC in modern TVs. Found in high-end displays from brands like Sony, TCL, Hisense, and Philips, these SoCs feature native hardware DSC 1.2a engines alongside full 48Gbps HDMI 2.1 FRL receivers capable of decompressing 4K 120Hz/144Hz and 8K 60Hz video streams.
  • Novatek (NT72690 series): Widely used by tier-one and tier-two manufacturers, these chips provide integrated HDMI 2.1 receiver capabilities with built-in multi-slice DSC decompression blocks for 4K high-frame-rate gaming and video.
  • Realtek (RTD2885 / RTD2173 series): Realtek produces both full TV SoCs and dedicated display bridge ICs. Their hardware includes dedicated logic pipelines for decompressing VESA DSC streams up to 48Gbps input rates.

Proprietary Custom Silicon

Leading television manufacturers often design in-house processors to handle HDMI 2.1 inputs and DSC decompression:

  • LG Electronics (Alpha 9 and Alpha 11 Processors): LG uses custom silicon integrating proprietary front-end HDMI 2.1 controllers and hardware DSC decoders directly into the Alpha series application processors, allowing 4K 120Hz/144Hz 4:4:4 10-bit decompression across all full-bandwidth HDMI ports.
  • Samsung (NQ8 AI and Neural Quantum Processors): Samsung integrates multi-lane HDMI 2.1 FRL receivers and DSC decompression engines into their custom display processors, commonly found either on the main board or inside the external One Connect Box.

Critical Hardware Blocks Inside the Silicon

Regardless of whether a TV uses a commercial MediaTek chip or custom silicon, the silicon itself must contain the following specific hardware sub-blocks:

  1. HDMI 2.1 FRL Physical Layer (PHY): Handles the electrical signaling and clock recovery for Fixed Rate Link transmission at speeds up to 12Gbps per lane across 4 lanes (48Gbps aggregate).
  2. Link Layer & Protocol Engine: Demultiplexes packets, identifies the DSC picture parameter set (PPS), and routes compressed video stream slices to the decoder.
  3. VESA DSC 1.2a Hardware Decompression Core: A parallel array of entropy decoders and sub-stream processors. This block typically relies on licensed silicon IP—such as the Synopsys DesignWare HDMI 2.1 with DSC IP or Rambus/Hardent DSC IP cores—fabricated directly onto the SoC silicon.
  4. Line Buffers and Memory Interfaces: High-speed internal SRAM buffers that store line-by-line data during pixel reconstruction before pushing the raw pixel data over V-by-One or internal DisplayPort (eDP) interfaces to the display panel's timing controller.
