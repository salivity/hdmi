How HDMI Sinks Detect Source Standby Mode

When an HDMI source device such as a game console, streaming box, or PC enters low-power standby mode, the connected HDMI sink (a TV, monitor, or AV receiver) must quickly identify this change to display a "No Signal" warning or initiate its own power-saving routines. Sinks achieve this primarily by monitoring Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) clock signals, listening for specific Consumer Electronics Control (CEC) network commands, and tracking link-layer encryption and power states.

1. Cessation of TMDS or FRL Clock and Data Lines

The primary and fastest physical method a sink uses to detect standby is observing the high-speed video data lines.

  • TMDS (HDMI 1.4/2.0): In active operation, the source constantly sends a continuous differential clock signal on the dedicated TMDS Clock channel. When entering standby, the source disables its internal TMDS transmitter. The sink's internal HDMI receiver (PHY) detects the collapse of differential transitions and registers a loss-of-clock state (often referred to as an "unlocked" state).
  • FRL (HDMI 2.1): HDMI 2.1 replaces the separate clock line with embedded clocking across all lanes. When entering low power, the source stops transmitting active character blocks and idle patterns. The sink's link-training engine detects the loss of synchronization and concludes the source is no longer transmitting.

2. Consumer Electronics Control (CEC) Standby Commands

For modern consumer electronics, standby detection often happens at the protocol layer before physical video signals are cut. If HDMI-CEC is enabled on both devices:

  • The source sends a direct or broadcast CEC command containing the <Standby> opcode (hex value 0x36) over Pin 13.
  • Upon receiving this packet, the sink explicitly knows the source is intentionally powering down, allowing the sink to immediately turn off or switch to an alternate active input without waiting for a signal-loss timeout.

3. HDCP Encryption and Handshake Failure

Active HDMI connections typically run High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP). HDCP requires continuous communication, link synchronization, and periodic re-authentication over the Display Data Channel (DDC). When a source enters sleep mode, the HDCP cipher engine stops processing. The sink identifies the failure of the cipher state and recognizes the video session as terminated.

4. +5V Power Line Status (Pin 18)

The HDMI interface includes a dedicated +5V Power line provided by the source.

  • Complete Drop: Some sources cut the +5V power entirely when in deep sleep, instantly signaling their inactive status to the sink's monitoring circuitry.
  • Persistent Power: Many modern sources intentionally keep the +5V line active during standby. This maintains power to the sink’s Hot Plug Detect (HPD) detection circuits and allows the source to quickly wake up and query the sink's Extended Display Identification Data (EDID). Therefore, while a loss of +5V always indicates standby or disconnection, the presence of +5V alone does not guarantee the source is awake.

Sink Reaction and Processing

Once an HDMI sink detects the loss of valid video clocks or receives a CEC shutdown command, it typically debounces the state for a few seconds to avoid triggering false alarms caused by resolution or refresh-rate switches. After confirming the lack of an active stream, the sink enters its own low-power routine, shifts to another input, or displays a sleep timer dialog.
