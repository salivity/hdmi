HDMI TMDS Receiver Termination Voltage

This article provides an overview of the common-mode termination voltage required at the receiver end of a High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) lane. It outlines the specific voltage standards, explains the termination topology used by HDMI sinks, and details how this pull-up voltage facilitates high-speed digital transmission between source and sink devices.

The Standard Termination Voltage

In an HDMI TMDS interface, the required nominal termination voltage (\(V_{term}\)) at the receiver (sink) end is +3.3 V DC.

The HDMI specification defines this reference level as \(AV_{cc}\) (or \(V_{term}\)), with a standard operating tolerance typically between 3.15 V and 3.45 V (3.3 V ± 5%). This pull-up rail must be supplied internally by the HDMI receiver device to bias the high-speed differential signal lines properly.

Receiver Termination Architecture

Each HDMI TMDS data lane and clock lane consists of a differential pair (a positive line and a negative line). The receiver termination topology requires:

  • A 50-ohm resistor connected from the positive line (\(D+\)) to the +3.3 V termination rail.
  • A 50-ohm resistor connected from the negative line (\(D-\)) to the +3.3 V termination rail.

This configuration establishes a single-ended impedance of 50 ohms to ground/rail for each trace and provides the standard 100-ohm differential termination necessary to match the characteristic impedance of the HDMI cable and transmission lines.

How the Termination Operates

HDMI TMDS relies on current-steering logic drivers at the transmitter (source) and passive pull-up termination at the receiver (sink):

  1. Current Sinking: The transmitter drivers do not source current; instead, they sink a nominal current of approximately 10 mA to 12 mA from the lines to ground.
  2. Voltage Swing: When the transmitter sinks current through one of the 50-ohm pull-up resistors at the receiver, it causes a voltage drop of approximately 500 mV across that resistor.
  3. Logic Levels:
    • When a driver is off, no current flows, and the line is pulled up to the high common-mode rail of 3.3 V (logic high).
    • When a driver is on, it sinks current, pulling the line down to approximately 2.8 V (logic low).
  4. Common-Mode Operating Point: Because one line of the differential pair is pulled low while the other remains high (or alternates), the resulting DC common-mode voltage (\(V_{cm}\)) of the active differential pair typically floats around 3.05 V to 3.1 V during normal data transmission.

Without the stable +3.3 V termination voltage supplied by the receiver, the current-mode transmitter drivers cannot generate the differential voltage swing required to decode TMDS video, audio, and auxiliary data.
