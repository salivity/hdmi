Why HDMI Does Not Support Daisy Chaining

Unlike DisplayPort, which supports native daisy-chaining through Multi-Stream Transport (MST), the HDMI standard does not natively support running multiple independent video displays off a single source port. This fundamental difference stems from distinct design philosophies, underlying transmission architectures, and the target markets each standard was originally built to serve. While DisplayPort was engineered from the ground up for desktop computing and modular monitor setups, HDMI was developed primarily for consumer audio/video electronics where single, dedicated point-to-point connections are standard.

Fundamental Transmission Architectures

The primary technical barrier preventing HDMI from daisy-chaining lies in how it packages and transmits data. DisplayPort relies on a packetized data architecture similar to Ethernet or PCI Express. It breaks down audio and video into discrete micro-packets, assigns them stream identifiers, and routes them across available data lanes. Because of this structure, DisplayPort 1.2 was able to introduce Multi-Stream Transport (MST), allowing a single cable to carry multiple independent video streams that compatible monitors can demultiplex, display, and pass along to the next screen down the chain.

HDMI, on the other hand, was built around Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) and later Fixed Rate Link (FRL) in HDMI 2.1. These protocols are fundamentally streaming-based rather than packet-routed. An HDMI connection establishes a strict, dedicated clock and data pipeline between one transmitter (the source) and one receiver (the sink). Because the data is delivered as a continuous, synchronized stream without a packetized multiplexing layer, an HDMI port cannot divide its bandwidth into discrete independent streams within the native specification.

Divergent Market Priorities

HDMI and DisplayPort were created by different groups with different goals:

  • HDMI: Governed by consumer electronics manufacturers (including Sony, Panasonic, and Philips), HDMI was tailored for living room setups connecting game consoles, cable boxes, and Blu-ray players directly to televisions or AV receivers. In this ecosystem, one source invariably feeds one screen, making daisy-chaining an unnecessary complexity. Instead, the HDMI Forum focused development on features like Consumer Electronics Control (CEC), Audio Return Channel (eARC), and Variable Refresh Rate (VRR).
  • DisplayPort: Governed by the Video Electronics Standards Association (VESA), DisplayPort was created explicitly for the PC industry. Workstation productivity, multi-monitor trading desks, and clutter-free laptop docking stations made multi-monitor routing a core business requirement.

Cost, Hardware, and Backward Compatibility

Implementing daisy-chaining requires hardware logic not just at the graphics card, but inside the display itself. A daisy-chainable monitor must contain an integrated routing hub, display controller, and downstream transmitter to extract its intended stream and forward the remaining data down the line.

Integrating this hardware into HDMI displays would raise manufacturing costs for a feature largely irrelevant to the television market. Furthermore, the HDMI Forum places a massive emphasis on strict backward compatibility across all consumer gear. Overhauling HDMI's signal framework to support stream demultiplexing would introduce fragmentation, driver conflicts, and potential handshake failures across millions of legacy devices.
