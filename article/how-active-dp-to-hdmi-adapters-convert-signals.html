How Active DP++ to HDMI Adapters Convert Signals

Active DisplayPort to HDMI adapters enable modern graphics sources to drive HDMI displays by translating disparate electrical and data protocols at the hardware level. While Dual-Mode DisplayPort (DP++) ports can natively output HDMI-compatible signals using simple passive level shifters, active adapters contain dedicated internal microchips that process, decode, and regenerate the signal independently. This process ensures reliable video transmission, enables high-bandwidth formats like 4K at high refresh rates, and overcomes the hardware limitations of multi-display graphics cards.

The Limit of Passive DP++ and Why Active Processing Is Needed

A standard DP++ port can detect an HDMI sink via a passive adapter and switch its internal physical layer (PHY) to output Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS), which HDMI uses. However, a graphics card has a limited number of internal clock generators (phase-locked loops, or PLLs) dedicated to TMDS. When driving multiple displays, or when pushing beyond basic resolutions (such as HDMI 2.0’s 4K at 60Hz), the GPU cannot supply the required native TMDS clock.

An active adapter solves this by forcing the DP++ port to transmit standard DisplayPort packetized data. Instead of relying on the GPU to shift its output, the active adapter handles the entire protocol conversion internally.

1. DisplayPort Receiver (RX) and Signal Reception

The conversion begins at the adapter's input stage via a dedicated DisplayPort receiver integrated circuit (IC):

  • Differential Signaling: The IC receives AC-coupled differential signals over 1, 2, or 4 DisplayPort Main Link lanes.
  • Depacketization: Unlike HDMI, which sends a continuous stream of rasterized pixel lines and dedicated clock signals, DisplayPort transmits data in micro-packets (similar to Ethernet). The receiver demultiplexes these packets to extract the raw digital video stream, embedded audio channels, and metadata (such as HDR info-frames).

2. Clock Reconstruction and Frequency Synthesis

DisplayPort embeds its clock signal directly into the data lanes using 8b/10b or 128b/132b line coding. Standard HDMI (TMDS), by contrast, requires a separate physical clock lane running continuously alongside three data lanes.

  • The active adapter uses an internal Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) and clock synthesizer to extract the timing information embedded within the DisplayPort stream.
  • It generates a synchronous, low-jitter TMDS pixel clock specifically matched to the resolution and refresh rate requested by the target display.

3. Auxiliary (AUX) to DDC Bus Translation

HDMI and DisplayPort manage display identification and capabilities through different sideband channels:

  • DisplayPort uses the AUX Channel: A high-speed, bidirectional, AC-coupled differential Manchester-encoded link.
  • HDMI uses the Display Data Channel (DDC): A lower-speed, I2C-based serial bus.

The adapter’s processing unit acts as a bridge between these two protocols. When the display sends its Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) or responds to hot-plug detection (HPD), the adapter translates the I2C transactions into AUX packets for the source GPU, ensuring seamless handshake negotiation.

4. Content Protection (HDCP) Decryption and Re-encryption

Because modern media streams are typically protected, the active chip contains dedicated cryptographic hardware:

  • The adapter negotiates an HDCP link on the DisplayPort input side (e.g., HDCP 1.3 or 2.2).
  • It temporarily decrypts the incoming protected stream inside the secure memory boundary of the IC.
  • It immediately re-encrypts the data using the corresponding HDMI HDCP standard (e.g., HDCP 1.4 or 2.2/2.3) before sending it to the display, preventing signal disruption while maintaining copy protection compliance.

5. TMDS/FRL Transmitter (TX) and Level Shifting

Once the data is depacketized, synchronized, and encrypted, the adapter prepares the signal for the HDMI cable:

  • Encoding: The core logic re-encodes the raw parallel pixel data into 10-bit TMDS characters (or Fixed Rate Link/FRL packets for HDMI 2.1 adapters).
  • Physical Output Driver: The integrated transmitter outputs DC-coupled current-mode logic (CML) signals at standard 3.3V logic levels over four shielded twisted pairs (Clock, Data 0, Data 1, and Data 2).
  • Power Supply: The adapter’s internal ICs draw power either directly from DisplayPort Pin 20 (which provides +3.3V) via an internal low-dropout (LDO) voltage regulator or through an external USB power source on ultra-high-bandwidth models.
