How HDMI Cable Testers Work: Pinout and Continuity

An HDMI cable tester evaluates the physical and electrical integrity of an HDMI cable by transmitting low-voltage electrical signals through each of the cable's 19 individual pins. By connecting a main transmitter unit to one end of the cable and a remote receiver unit to the other, the device systematically tests for open circuits (continuity), bridging between conductors (shorts), and proper pin-to-pin alignment (wire pair mapping). This diagnostic process ensures all audio, video, control, and power pathways are intact and free of manufacturing defects or physical damage.

Checking for Pin Continuity

Continuity testing confirms that an unbroken electrical path exists from the connector on one end of the cable to the connector on the other. HDMI connectors feature 19 distinct pins, plus an outer ground shield.

  1. Sequential Stepping: The tester's transmitter utilizes a microcontroller to send a sequential direct current (DC) pulse down each pin one at a time, starting from Pin 1 through Pin 19 (and the chassis shield).
  2. Signal Verification: As the pulse is sent down a specific line, the remote receiver module listens on the corresponding pin.
  3. Fault Detection: If the receiver registers the expected voltage on the matching pin, continuity is confirmed. If no voltage is detected, the tester flags that specific pin as "Open," indicating a severed wire, broken solder joint, or corroded contact. Basic testers display this via an advancing sequence of LEDs, while digital testers show an open pin error on an LCD screen.

Detecting Electrical Shorts

A short circuit occurs when two or more conductors make unintended physical or electrical contact, which can corrupt high-speed data or damage connected hardware (such as shorting the +5V power pin to a data line).

  1. Isolation Monitoring: When the tester energizes Pin 1 with a test voltage, the receiver scans all other pins (Pins 2 through 19 and the shield) to verify they remain completely inactive.
  2. Current Leakage Detection: If voltage registers simultaneously on Pin 1 and Pin 4, for example, the tester detects that these two conductors are bridged.
  3. Reporting: Advanced testers can pinpoint the exact conductors involved in the short, allowing technicians to determine whether an internal cable twist has compressed and stripped internal insulation or if solder bridged two adjacent pins inside the connector head.

Wire Pair Mapping and Polarity Verification

HDMI relies on Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) to transmit ultra-high-definition video and audio. This architecture uses four shielded twisted pairs: TMDS Data 0, Data 1, Data 2, and the Clock channel (in HDMI 1.4/2.0). Each channel requires three distinct lines: a positive pin (+), a negative pin (-), and a dedicated ground shield.

  • Miswire and Reversal Detection: Wire pair mapping verifies that the individual lines within each twisted pair have not been reversed or swapped. For instance, in TMDS Data 0 (Pins 7, 8, and 9), Pin 7 (Data 0+) must route exclusively to Pin 7, Pin 8 (Shield) to Pin 8, and Pin 9 (Data 0-) to Pin 9. If Pin 7 routes to Pin 9, the pair is inverted, which will break differential signaling.
  • Crossed Pairs (Split Pairs): If an installer or manufacturer accidentally pairs a conductor from Data 0 with a conductor from Data 1, the tester identifies the cross-connection. While simple continuity might pass, the tester recognizes that the physical mapping does not follow standard HDMI pin configurations.
  • Auxiliary Line Verification: Beyond the high-speed data pairs, mapping ensures that individual functional lines—such as Pin 13 (CEC), Pin 14 (Utility/HEAC), Pin 15 (SCL), Pin 16 (SDA), Pin 18 (+5V Power), and Pin 19 (Hot Plug Detect)—are routed to their exact specifications without interference.
