How HDMI Equalizers Open Closed Eye Diagrams

High-speed HDMI signals degrade as they travel through copper cables, where frequency-dependent attenuation smears data transitions and collapses the signal's eye diagram. An HDMI cable equalizer resolves this by applying high-frequency peaking filters—typically implemented as Continuous-Time Linear Equalizers (CTLE)—to selectively amplify the lost high-frequency components relative to low frequencies. By dynamically tuning its filter response to match the exact loss profile of the cable, the equalizer flattens the channel's overall frequency response, eliminates inter-symbol interference (ISI), and reopens the closed eye to restore signal integrity at the receiver.

The Cause of a Closed Eye

Copper HDMI cables suffer from physical limitations, primarily the skin effect and dielectric losses. These phenomena cause attenuation that increases dramatically with frequency:

  • Low-frequency signals pass through the cable with minimal loss.
  • High-frequency components, which define the sharp rising and falling edges of digital data transitions, suffer heavy attenuation.

When high-frequency edges lose amplitude and slow down, adjacent bits bleed into one another. In an eye diagram—which overlays millions of transmitted bit cycles—this inter-symbol interference causes the vertical eye height (voltage margin) and horizontal eye width (timing margin) to collapse entirely. A "closed eye" means the receiver cannot distinguish between a digital '1' and a digital '0', leading to bit errors or total signal loss.

High-Frequency Peaking Filters (CTLE)

To counteract this loss, HDMI receivers employ active equalizers containing high-frequency peaking filters. The transfer function of these filters is designed to be the mathematical inverse of the cable's attenuation curve:

  1. Low-Frequency Attenuation or Flat Gain: The filter passes low-frequency signals with little or no amplification (or applies slight attenuation to lower the baseline).
  2. High-Frequency Peaking: The filter introduces a zero in its frequency response followed by a pole, creating an active boost peak near the fundamental Nyquist frequency of the HDMI data rate.

By multiplying the cable's low-pass response with the equalizer's high-pass peaking response, the combined channel transfer function becomes approximately flat across the entire signal bandwidth.

How the Equalizer Adjusts the Peaking Response

HDMI cables vary in length, gauge, and quality, meaning the amount of high-frequency loss is unknown until a connection is established. To adaptively open a closed eye, the equalizer uses internal feedback loops to tune the peaking filter:

  • Frequency Spectrum Sensing: The equalizer splits the incoming signal into low-frequency and high-frequency paths using analog bandpass filters. By comparing the energy ratio between the low-frequency baseline and high-frequency content, the circuit estimates the degree of cable loss.
  • Tuning Resistor-Capacitor (RC) Networks: Based on the detected loss, the equalizer adjusts programmable capacitor arrays and variable resistor degeneration within its differential amplifier stages. Increasing the capacitive degeneration shifts the peaking frequency and increases the gain peak.
  • Adaptive Finite State Machines (FSM): Digital control logic sweeps through discrete equalization settings during the HDMI link-training or startup phase. It monitors internal transition metrics—such as edge jitter, transition density, or early/late phase detector feedback from the Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) circuit.
  • Iterative Optimization: The FSM increments the peaking gain until the CDR successfully locks and horizontal edge jitter is minimized. If the peaking gain is set too low (under-equalization), the eye remains closed due to slow edge transitions. If set too high (over-equalization), high-frequency noise and overshoot re-close the eye. The equalizer locks onto the optimal setting that maximizes both eye height and eye width.

The Reopened Eye

Once the peaking filters are tuned to balance the channel loss, the signal's rise and fall times are restored to their original steepness. High-frequency transitions arrive in phase with low-frequency transitions, clearing the center of the eye diagram. This wide-open aperture provides the receiver’s decision threshold slicer with ample voltage and timing margins to reliably sample the digital stream without bit errors.
