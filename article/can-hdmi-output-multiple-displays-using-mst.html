Can HDMI Output Multiple Displays Using MST?

Unlike DisplayPort, a native HDMI port cannot output two distinct video streams to extend your desktop using Multi-Stream Transport (MST). HDMI architecture is fundamentally limited to Single Stream Transport (SST), meaning a standard HDMI port can only transmit one independent video feed at a time. This article explores why HDMI lacks native MST capability, how passive and active splitters behave, and the alternative technologies you can use to connect multiple extended displays.

The Technical Limitation of HDMI

DisplayPort introduced Multi-Stream Transport in version 1.2, allowing graphics cards to multiplex multiple independent video signals across a single cable. A compatible monitor or an MST hub can then demultiplex those signals and distribute them to separate displays, enabling an extended desktop.

HDMI does not support this protocol. The HDMI specification was designed strictly around single-stream transmission for consumer electronics, such as connecting a Blu-ray player, console, or PC to a single television or monitor. Because the physical transmission layer and protocol lack native multiplexing capabilities, your graphics card cannot assign separate display identifiers (EDIDs) to multiple monitors through a single HDMI connector.

The Problem with HDMI Splitters

Users looking to connect two monitors to a single HDMI output often turn to inexpensive HDMI splitters. However, because the port only delivers a single video stream, these devices can only replicate the exact same image across multiple screens (mirror mode).

An HDMI splitter takes the single incoming signal, duplicates the packets, and broadcasts them simultaneously. It cannot communicate back to your operating system to request a secondary, extended workspace.

What About HDMI 2.1 "Dual View"?

HDMI 2.1 introduced a feature known as "Dual View," which allows two independent video streams to be transmitted concurrently over the same link. However, this is not equivalent to MST for PC multi-monitor setups.

Dual View is intended for a single display to show two different full-screen video feeds at the same time—such as two players seeing distinct perspectives on the same TV via specialized 3D glasses or split-view technology. Operating systems like Windows and macOS do not use Dual View to extend desktops across two separate physical monitors.

Solutions for Running Multiple Extended Displays

If your computer only has one video output or you need more displays than available physical ports, there are alternative methods:

  • USB-C with DisplayPort Alternate Mode: Many modern laptops feature USB-C ports that support "DP Alt Mode." USB-C docks that provide dual HDMI outputs do not actually run on HDMI from the host; they use the internal DisplayPort signal combined with an onboard MST hub to drive two extended HDMI screens. Note that macOS does not support MST over USB-C and will only mirror displays in this configuration.
  • DisplayLink (USB to Video Adapters): DisplayLink-enabled adapters connect to a standard USB-A or USB-C port. They use your computer's CPU and a software driver to encode display data as standard USB data packets, converting it into a separate video output at the adapter. This bypasses your GPU's port limitations entirely and supports extended mode.
  • Display Splitters / Processors: Specialized hardware, such as ultra-wide span controllers, can present itself to your operating system as one single ultra-wide display (for example, 3840x1080). The hardware device then physically crops the image in half, sending the left half to one monitor and the right half to the other via separate HDMI cables.
