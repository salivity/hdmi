HDCP 2.3 Locality Check Round-Trip Time Limit

High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) 2.3 incorporates a locality check to ensure that receiving devices are physically co-located on the same local network or direct connection, rather than streaming over wide-area networks. This article covers the specific round-trip time limit enforced by the HDCP 2.3 specification, the mechanics of how the locality verification operates over HDMI and bridging interfaces, and the consequences of exceeding the timing window.

The 7 Millisecond Round-Trip Time Limit

Under the HDCP 2.3 specification, the transmitter enforces a strict round-trip time (RTT) limit of 7 milliseconds (7 ms) during the locality check.

The verification begins when the transmitter issues an 8-byte random nonce (r_tx) to the receiver and immediately starts an internal watchdog timer. The receiver must process this nonce, compute the required cryptographic response, and return it to the transmitter. If the transmitter does not receive the response within the 7 ms window, the locality verification fails.

Purpose of the Locality Check

The primary objective of the locality check is to prevent content piracy and unauthorized distribution. Without a timing constraint, protected content could be decrypted, re-encapsulated, and tunneled across the internet or remote cloud servers. Because transmission across the public internet almost universally incurs latencies greater than 7 ms due to routing, propagation delays, and switching, the 7 ms threshold acts as an effective cryptographic boundary that restricts distribution to a local physical environment.

Execution and Failure Handling

During the authentication phase:

  1. Initiation: The transmitter transmits the LC_Init message containing r_tx and starts the timer.
  2. Response: The receiver calculates the response using its derived session keys and returns the LC_Send_L_prime message.
  3. Validation: If the response arrives within 7 ms and the cryptographic signature (L') matches the transmitter's independently computed value (L), the locality check succeeds.
  4. Timeouts and Retries: If the response takes longer than 7 ms, the transmitter considers the check failed. The specification permits the transmitter to retry the locality check up to a maximum of 1,024 attempts before abandoning the authentication handshake entirely, at which point content transmission is blocked.

Relevance to HDMI and Network Extensions

While native, uncompressed point-to-point HDMI cables introduce latency measured in nanoseconds, modern HDMI distribution frequently relies on HDMI extenders, HDBaseT adapters, and AV-over-IP bridging systems. When HDCP 2.3 protected streams are transmitted through these systems, any network congestion, packet queuing, or processing overhead introduced by media converters must remain well below the 7 ms ceiling to prevent playback interruption.
