How HDCP Authentication Works Over HDMI

High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) prevents unauthorized duplication of copyrighted digital media as it travels between source devices and displays. Across a High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) connection, HDCP establishes security through a multi-step cryptographic handshake known as authentication. This process verifies that the receiving display is authorized to handle the content, establishes shared encryption keys between the devices, and continuously monitors the connection to ensure the link remains secure before transmitting any protected video or audio data.

The Role of Devices in the Link

An HDCP-protected HDMI link consists of at least two primary roles: the transmitter (source), such as a Blu-ray player, gaming console, or streaming box, and the receiver (sink), typically a television or monitor. If an audio-video (AV) receiver or splitter is in the chain, it acts as an HDCP repeater, which decrypts the content, processes audio or distributes the video, and re-encrypts the stream for the next downstream receiver.

Step 1: The Initial Handshake and Key Exchange

Authentication begins immediately when an HDMI connection is made and protected content is requested. Communication for authentication occurs over the HDMI Display Data Channel (DDC), an I²C bus separate from the high-speed audiovisual lines.

  1. Initiation: The transmitter initiates authentication by generating a pseudo-random value and sending it to the receiver along with its own identifier.
  2. Key Selection Vector (KSV) Exchange: In HDCP 1.x links, each authorized device is provisioned at manufacturing with a set of secret private keys and an associated Key Selection Vector (KSV). The receiver sends its KSV back to the transmitter. In modern HDCP 2.2 and newer versions (designed for 4K and 8K content), this mechanism is upgraded to use industry-standard public-key cryptography (RSA or ECDSA) and Diffie-Hellman key exchanges instead of the proprietary KSV matrix.
  3. Revocation Check: The transmitter cross-references the receiver’s identifier or public certificate against a System Renewability Message (SRM) stored in its firmware. If the receiver’s key is identified as compromised, the source revokes access and halts transmission.

Step 2: Shared Secret Computation and Link Verification

Once both devices have exchanged public values, they independently calculate a shared secret value.

In HDCP 1.x implementations, both devices mathematically combine their internal private keys with the counterpart’s KSV. Because of the mathematical properties of the HDCP key matrix, this process yields the exact same secret value on both sides without ever transmitting the secret across the wire. In HDCP 2.x, this shared master key is derived via Diffie-Hellman exchange.

To confirm that both devices arrived at the same secret:

  • Both devices use the secret to compute a verification hash.
  • The receiver sends its calculated hash back to the transmitter within a strict time limit (typically 100 milliseconds).
  • The transmitter compares the receiver's hash against its own.

If the values match, the receiver is validated as an authentic, licensed HDCP device.

Step 3: Repeater Authentication and Topology Mapping

If the connected device identifies itself as a repeater rather than an end-display sink, a second authentication phase occurs. The repeater queries all downstream devices connected to it, collects their identifiers, and forwards the entire downstream device list back to the source. The source verifies that the total number of connected devices does not exceed HDCP topology limits (up to 127 devices across 7 tiers) and checks all downstream identifiers against its revocation list.

Step 4: Stream Encryption and Ongoing Integrity

Once authentication is successful, the shared secret generates the running cipher keys used to encrypt the raw pixel and audio data. The encrypted data is transmitted across the HDMI Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) data lines.

To prevent an attacker from swapping out the authenticated display mid-stream, the devices perform continuous link integrity checks. In HDCP 1.x, a new verification hash is generated and verified every two seconds. In HDCP 2.x, the stream uses continuous counter-mode AES encryption, ensuring that any interruption, tampering, or severed connection immediately breaks decryption and causes the display to blank out.
