How HDMI Splitters Handle HDCP Handshakes

When an HDMI splitter distributes a single encrypted video signal to two different display sinks, it must negotiate High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) to ensure the content remains secure. Because the source device cannot establish two separate cryptographic handshakes over a single port, the HDMI splitter acts as an active intermediary (technically classified as an HDCP repeater). This article explains how splitters process HDCP handshakes between mismatched displays, the methods they use to resolve capability differences, and the common results of these negotiations.

The Splitter as an HDCP Repeater

Under the HDCP specification, an HDMI splitter operates as a "repeater" rather than a passive wire tap. When connected:

  1. Upstream Handshake: The splitter authenticates itself to the content source (such as a media player or gaming console) as an authorized HDCP receiver.
  2. Downstream Handshake: The splitter independently negotiates authentication and key exchange with each connected display sink (Display A and Display B).
  3. Topology Verification: The splitter gathers the downstream device count and Key Selection Vectors (KSVs) and reports them back to the source to confirm that repeater limits (depth and device count) are not exceeded.

Because two different monitors or TVs often support different HDCP revisions (such as HDCP 2.2 for 4K UHD content versus HDCP 1.4 for 1080p), the splitter must resolve the discrepancy using one of three standard strategies.

1. The Lowest Common Denominator Method

The most standard approach used by consumer splitters is enforcing the lowest common denominator across all connected displays.

  • The Process: If Display A supports HDCP 2.2 and Display B only supports HDCP 1.4, the splitter advertises HDCP 1.4 compliance back to the source.
  • The Result: The source restricts the transmission to whatever is allowable under HDCP 1.4. In streaming environments, this typically causes the source to throttle resolution from 4K down to 1080p, ensuring both displays receive an active picture simultaneously without violating content restrictions.

2. Primary Port Delegation (Port 1 Priority)

Some splitters bypass negotiation arbitration by assigning authority to a primary port (typically labeled "Output 1" or "Main").

  • The Process: The splitter copies the Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) and HDCP specifications strictly from the display on Output 1 and mirrors those exact capabilities to the source.
  • The Result: If Output 1 is connected to an HDCP 2.2 display, the source transmits an HDCP 2.2-encrypted 4K stream. Output 1 will display the picture correctly. However, if Output 2 is connected to an HDCP 1.4 display that cannot decrypt the stream, Output 2 will show a black screen, an HDCP unauthorized error, or static noise.

3. Active Protocol Conversion and Downscaling

Advanced, powered splitters contain internal processing chips capable of active decryption, downscaling, and re-encryption.

  • The Process: The splitter completes an HDCP 2.2 handshake with the source, receives the fully encrypted high-bandwidth stream, and decrypts it internally. It then performs two independent downstream handshakes: sending the native HDCP 2.2 4K signal to Display A, while simultaneously downscaling the video to 1080p and re-encrypting it under HDCP 1.4 for Display B.
  • The Result: Both displays operate at their highest native capability without forcing the source device to downgrade the stream for the more capable display.

Failure Modes During the Handshake

If the downstream authentication fails on either branch—due to an incompatible cable, an unsupported display, or an uncompliant splitter—the HDCP state machine aborts transmission. The source will cease sending video data and instead transmit a continuous stream of unencrypted visual noise (snow), display a static "HDCP Error" message, or shut off the HDMI TMDS/FRL clock, resulting in "No Signal" on the receiving screens.
