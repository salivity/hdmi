Why Modern TVs Dropped DTS HDMI Decoding

Over the past decade, major television manufacturers such as Samsung and LG phased out native DTS (Digital Theater Systems) decoding and passthrough capabilities over HDMI. This shift was primarily driven by financial disputes regarding licensing fees with DTS’s parent company, Xperi, combined with the streaming industry’s universal adoption of rival Dolby formats. Because television margins are thin and mainstream media consumption has moved away from physical discs, manufacturers decided that paying licensing royalties for DTS decoders offered no viable return on investment.

The Cost of Redundant Licensing Royalties

Television manufacturers must pay a royalty fee for every single unit sold that includes proprietary audio decoders. For years, consumer displays included licenses for both Dolby Digital and DTS to ensure compatibility with physical media like DVDs and Blu-ray discs.

As hardware prices fell and display panels became commoditized, manufacturers faced tighter profit margins. DTS licensing—administered by Xperi—requires dedicated per-device royalty payments. Television brands argued that paying licensing fees to both Dolby and DTS was a redundant cost, especially when consumer behavior shifted decisively toward digital distribution. To reduce production costs and avoid paying licensing fees on millions of units where the feature went unused, companies chose to drop DTS entirely.

The Rise of Dolby in the Streaming Era

The licensing tension was further compounded by the content distribution shift toward subscription video-on-demand (SVOD) platforms. Providers like Netflix, Disney+, Amazon Prime Video, and Apple TV standardized their multi-channel audio delivery on Dolby Digital Plus (E-AC-3) and Dolby Atmos.

Because Dolby provided efficient compression optimized for internet bandwidth, streaming platforms had little incentive to support DTS formats. TV manufacturers realized that a Dolby license was non-negotiable for a modern smart TV, while a DTS license was largely irrelevant for streaming apps. Consequently, retaining DTS decoding exclusively benefited a small niche of physical media enthusiasts, making the licensing fees financially unjustifiable to manufacturers.

The Impact of All-or-Nothing Licensing Terms

The removal of DTS was not merely a choice to omit internal decoding; it frequently impacted HDMI ARC and eARC passthrough functionality as well. Licensing frameworks traditionally restricted television processors from even routing an encoded bitstream to an external AV receiver or soundbar without a certified, licensed implementation.

Rather than pay licensing fees simply to pass unaltered DTS audio signals over HDMI eARC, brands like Samsung (starting in 2018) and LG (between 2020 and 2022) eliminated DTS support at the firmware and hardware levels. When a source device, such as a Blu-ray player or media server, sends a DTS-HD Master Audio or DTS:X track to the TV over HDMI, the television refuses the handshake, resulting in silence or forcing the player to downmix the audio to stereo PCM.

Resolution and Workarounds

The industry pushback achieved its goal of eliminating unwanted royalty overhead, though it alienated home theater users who rely on physical media. To bypass the lack of native TV decoding, users must route their source devices directly into an external audio system—such as an AV receiver or compatible soundbar—before sending the video signal to the television, completely circumventing the TV's audio pipeline. Recently, minor concessions have emerged, with select manufacturers reintroducing DTS passthrough on premium lines, but native DTS decoding remains absent across the broader consumer television market.
