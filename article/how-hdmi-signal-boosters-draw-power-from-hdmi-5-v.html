How HDMI Signal Boosters Draw Power from HDMI 5V

An HDMI signal booster overcomes signal degradation across extended cable runs by regenerating the high-speed audio and video stream. While some signal repeaters rely on external wall adapters, inline bus-powered boosters draw their operational energy directly from the HDMI interface itself. By tapping into the standard HDMI +5V rail, regulating that voltage internally, and operating within strict current thresholds, these compact devices process and amplify high-bandwidth signals without requiring an additional power plug.

The Dedicated 5V Pin Architecture

The standard 19-pin Type-A HDMI connector allocates specific pins for low-speed control, clocking, data transmission, and power. Operating power for an inline booster is harvested through two specific pins:

  • Pin 18 (+5V Power): Delivers direct current (DC) from the transmitting source device (such as a PC, Blu-ray player, or gaming console).
  • Pin 17 (DDC/CEC Ground): Acts as the ground return path for the +5V power rail, completing the electrical circuit.

Under the official HDMI specification, source devices are required to provide a continuous +5V supply on Pin 18. This supply was originally intended to power the Display Data Channel (DDC) and the EEPROM chip inside the display (sink) so the source could read the Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) even if the display was turned off. Inline boosters leverage this pre-existing rail to energize their own internal circuitry.

Current Limits and Voltage Regulation

According to standard HDMI requirements, a source must deliver a minimum of 55 milliamps (mA) at 4.8V to 5.3V. However, most modern source devices supply significantly more—typically between 100mA and 500mA.

Because signal-processing silicon operates at lower voltages than 5V, an inline booster converts the incoming power through an internal power-management circuit:

  1. Voltage Conversion: A high-efficiency Low-Dropout Regulator (LDO) or DC-DC step-down (buck) converter drops the +5V rail down to the operating levels required by the active chipset, typically 3.3V, 1.8V, or 1.2V.
  2. Filtering: Decoupling capacitors smooth out line noise and voltage ripples caused by long cable runs or power variations from the source hardware.
  3. Low-Power Active Equalization: The regulated current drives an equalizer or "redriver" integrated circuit (IC). These microchips are engineered to draw very little power, often requiring less than 50mA to 80mA to function reliably.

Driving the Signal Regeneration

Once powered, the internal IC counteracts high-frequency attenuation:

  • Equalization: The chip measures the incoming, attenuated Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) signals and compensates for high-frequency losses.
  • Limiting Amplification and Buffering: The boosted power rail is used to re-establish the differential voltage swing, sharpening the rise and fall times of digital data pulses.
  • Power Passthrough: The booster allows a portion of the original +5V line to continue through to the display end of the cable, ensuring the destination display still detects the Hot Plug Detect (HPD) sequence and reads EDID information accurately.

Operating Limitations of the 5V Line

While powering a booster solely via Pin 18 creates a clean, cable-free installation, the technique depends heavily on the source hardware. If a source device outputs only the minimum required 55mA, an active booster may experience voltage sags, leading to black screens, audio dropouts, or digital "sparkles" on screen. Similarly, extremely long copper cables upstream of the booster introduce resistance that drops the voltage below the chip's operational threshold. For this reason, many high-bandwidth boosters include a secondary port (such as Micro-USB or USB-C) to inject supplemental power if the HDMI 5V rail proves insufficient.
