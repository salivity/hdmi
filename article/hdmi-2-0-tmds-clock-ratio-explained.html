HDMI 2.0 TMDS Clock Ratio Explained

The TMDS (Transition Minimized Differential Signaling) clock ratio introduced in HDMI 2.0 allows high-bandwidth video signals to travel reliably over consumer cables by altering the relationship between the pixel clock frequency and the data transmission rate. When video bandwidth demands exceed the legacy limits of HDMI 1.4, switching the TMDS clock ratio from 1:10 to 1:40 drops the physical clock frequency significantly. This mechanism enables HDMI 2.0 to deliver 18 Gbps total bandwidth—sufficient for 4K video at 60Hz with HDR—without causing excessive electromagnetic interference (EMI) or signal degradation over standard copper wiring.

The Legacy 1:10 TMDS Architecture

In HDMI versions prior to 2.0 (such as HDMI 1.4b), TMDS encoding converts 8-bit color values into 10-bit characters to balance DC bias and minimize state transitions. Under this standard design, the TMDS clock lane operated at a fixed 1:10 ratio relative to the data lanes. This meant the clock frequency was exactly one-tenth of the bit rate per data channel.

For example, the maximum data rate under HDMI 1.4 was 3.4 Gbps per channel across three data channels (yielding 10.2 Gbps total). At this maximum limit, the clock ran at:

  • 3.4 Gbps ÷ 10 = 340 MHz

Because 340 MHz was generally the physical ceiling for reliable clock signaling across standard cables, pushing resolutions higher required a new approach.

The Challenge of HDMI 2.0 Bandwidth

HDMI 2.0 increased the per-channel bandwidth from 3.4 Gbps to 6.0 Gbps (yielding an aggregate bandwidth of 18.0 Gbps). If HDMI 2.0 had maintained the legacy 1:10 clock ratio, the TMDS clock would have had to operate at:

  • 6.0 Gbps ÷ 10 = 600 MHz

A 600 MHz clock frequency creates significant engineering challenges. High frequencies suffer from steep attenuation over copper lines, severe crosstalk between twisted pairs, and heightened electromagnetic emissions that can disrupt Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and cellular signals in adjacent devices.

How the 1:40 Ratio Solves the Problem

To prevent these signal integrity issues, the HDMI 2.0 specification introduced the 1:40 TMDS clock ratio.

When transmitting at bit rates greater than 3.4 Gbps per channel, the source device drops the clock frequency by a factor of four relative to the data stream. Instead of ticking once for every 10-bit character, the TMDS clock ticks once every 40 bits (or once every four characters).

At the maximum HDMI 2.0 rate of 6.0 Gbps per channel, the clock operates at:

  • 6.0 Gbps ÷ 40 = 150 MHz

By reducing the clock line from a theoretical 600 MHz down to 150 MHz, the physical stress on the cable and the interface hardware is dramatically lessened. The sink device (such as a 4K television or monitor) receives this 150 MHz reference clock and uses internal Phase-Locked Loops (PLLs) to multiply the frequency back up, reconstructing the timing required to deserialize the 6.0 Gbps data streams.

Configuration via SCDC

The selection of the TMDS clock ratio is not automatic; it is negotiated using the Status and Control Data Channel (SCDC) introduced alongside HDMI 2.0:

  1. Detection: The source queries the sink’s SCDC structure over the DDC (Display Data Channel) bus via I2C to verify HDMI 2.0 support.
  2. Bandwidth Evaluation: If the required video format demands a lane rate of 3.4 Gbps or lower, the source keeps the default 1:10 ratio active.
  3. Register Configuration: If the format requires a data rate between 3.4 Gbps and 6.0 Gbps, the source writes to the TMDS_Bit_Clock_Ratio bit within the sink's SCDC register, setting it from 0 (1:10) to 1 (1:40).
  4. Scrambling Activation: Concurrently, the source enables TMDS data scrambling, which helps smooth the spectral emission of the high-speed data lanes.

Summary of Function

The primary function of the TMDS clock ratio setting is to balance high throughput with physical signal integrity. By providing a 1:40 operational mode alongside the backward-compatible 1:10 mode, HDMI 2.0 accommodates modern high-resolution, high-frame-rate video formats without requiring radically redesigning standard cable pinouts or causing severe electromagnetic interference.
