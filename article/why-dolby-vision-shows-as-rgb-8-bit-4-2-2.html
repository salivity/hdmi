Why Dolby Vision Shows as RGB 8-Bit 4:2:2

When viewing HDMI diagnostic menus while playing Dolby Vision content, seeing the signal identified as an 8-bit RGB or 4:2:2 stream can be alarming, but it is entirely normal. This readout is the result of Dolby's proprietary transmission method—known as HDMI tunneling—rather than a reduction in video quality. Instead of sending an uncompressed, high-bandwidth 12-bit signal that legacy HDMI specifications cannot natively interpret, the source device packages the high-bit-depth data into an 8-bit container that HDMI diagnostics report at face value.

The Purpose of Dolby HDMI Tunneling

When Dolby Vision was developed, standard HDMI 1.4b and 2.0 specifications did not natively support dynamic metadata transmission alongside 12-bit color. To ensure broad hardware compatibility across source players, AV receivers, and displays, Dolby created a transport mechanism called "RGB Tunneling."

Under this method:

  • The player takes the full 12-bit YCbCr 4:2:2 video signal and dynamic metadata.
  • It repackages (or "tunnels") that data into a standard 8-bit RGB 4:4:4 transport stream.
  • To the HDMI pipeline, switches, and intermediate receivers, the signal appears to be a conventional, uncompressed 8-bit SDR transmission, preventing intermediate hardware from altering or discarding the proprietary data.

Player-Led vs. Display-Led Dolby Vision

Dolby Vision operates in two primary modes depending on hardware capabilities:

  1. Display-Led (Standard): The source sends the tunneled 12-bit data and dynamic metadata straight to the TV, and the TV’s internal Dolby processing chip handles tone mapping.
  2. Player-Led (Low Latency Dolby Vision / LLDV): Developed initially for Sony televisions lacking dedicated internal processing chips, tone mapping is calculated on the source player (such as an Apple TV, Xbox, or Shield TV).

In Player-Led mode, the source processes the metadata internally. Depending on the device implementation, it either transmits the processed signal over a standard YCbCr 4:2:2 10/12-bit pipeline or packages it inside an 8-bit transport wrapper. When a diagnostic menu reports contradictory terms like "RGB 8-bit 4:2:2," it is typically reading the 8-bit RGB container header on one end of the handshake while detecting the underlying 4:2:2 chroma subsampling format of the payload.

Why Diagnostic Menus Read the Container, Not the Content

HDMI diagnostic menus—such as those found on LG TVs, Denon/Marantz AV receivers, or external video processors—only inspect the outer HDMI transport layer.

The menu queries the HDMI InfoFrame, which describes the wrapper rather than the encoded payload. Because the wrapper is strictly structured as an 8-bit stream to maintain transmission integrity, the diagnostic utility reports an 8-bit signal. The TV’s internal Dolby Vision decoder identifies the hidden signaling flag, bypasses the standard display pipeline, unpacks the encapsulated 12-bit data, and renders the content in full dynamic range and color depth.

Conclusion

An HDMI diagnostic reading of RGB 8-bit or 4:2:2 during Dolby Vision playback is not an error, nor does it indicate color banding, chroma compression, or a degraded signal. It simply confirms that the HDMI tunneling protocol is functioning as intended, safely delivering full-fidelity HDR data to your display.
