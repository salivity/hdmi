How HDMI 2.0 Enables 4K 60Hz HDR with 4:2:0

HDMI 2.0 supports 4K video at 60Hz with 10-bit High Dynamic Range (HDR) by leveraging 4:2:0 chroma subsampling to drastically reduce video data transmission rates. Because HDMI 2.0 is hardware-limited to a maximum throughput of 18 Gbps, uncompressed 4K 60Hz 10-bit video exceeds the cable's physical capacity. By compressing color information while leaving brightness data intact, 4:2:0 chroma subsampling slashes the necessary data footprint, allowing full-resolution, high-frame-rate HDR video to operate within the 18 Gbps ceiling.

The HDMI 2.0 Bandwidth Limit

HDMI 2.0 specifies a maximum Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) clock rate of 600 MHz, translating to a total raw bandwidth limit of 18.0 Gbps. Factoring in 8b/10b encoding overhead—which uses two extra bits per byte for signal synchronization and error prevention—the usable video data payload tops out at roughly 14.4 Gbps.

Why Uncompressed 4K 60Hz 10-Bit Fails on HDMI 2.0

To display HDR properly, a display requires at least a 10-bit color depth (allowing 1,024 shades per color channel to eliminate banding) rather than the standard 8-bit depth (256 shades per channel). Calculating the bandwidth for an uncompressed video signal requires multiplying horizontal pixels, vertical pixels, refresh rate, and color bit depth across three channels (Red, Green, Blue or YCbCr 4:4:4):

  • Resolution: 3840 × 2160 (accounting for blanking intervals: ~4400 × 2250 total pixel clock)
  • Refresh Rate: 60 Hz
  • Color Depth: 10 bits per channel (30 bits per pixel)

At full 4:4:4 uncompressed color, this calculation yields an aggregate data stream of roughly 20.05 Gbps. Because 20.05 Gbps exceeds the 18.0 Gbps physical maximum of HDMI 2.0, an uncompressed 4K 60Hz 10-bit HDR signal simply cannot pass through the interface.

How 4:2:0 Chroma Subsampling Works

Human vision relies on rods (sensitive to luminance, or brightness) and cones (sensitive to chrominance, or color). Because the human eye has far greater acuity for luminance details than for subtle shifts in color, video compression systems separate the video signal into a luminance channel (Y) and two chrominance channels (Cb and Cr).

Chroma subsampling takes advantage of this biology by selectively discarding color data without altering brightness data:

  • 4:4:4: Full-bandwidth video where every single pixel maintains its own discrete luminance and color values.
  • 4:2:0: Full luminance detail is captured for every pixel, but color is sampled only once for every 2×2 block of four pixels. Both horizontal and vertical color resolutions are halved.

By reducing the chrominance resolution to one-quarter of the total pixels, 4:2:0 subsampling cuts the overall color payload in half compared to a 4:4:4 signal.

Meeting the Bandwidth Budget

When an HDMI 2.0 transmitter shifts from 4:4:4 to 4:2:0, the required bits per pixel for a 10-bit signal drop from 30 bits down to 15 bits.

Applying this compression to the 4K 60Hz signal reduces the data throughput requirement from ~20.05 Gbps down to approximately 11.14 Gbps. Because 11.14 Gbps is well below the 18.0 Gbps hard ceiling of HDMI 2.0, the signal passes reliably without packet loss or signal degradation.

This engineering balance allows televisions, media players, and game consoles using HDMI 2.0 to deliver 60 frames per second and wide dynamic range color palettes simultaneously, reserving higher data rates (like uncompressed 4:4:4 10-bit at 4K 60Hz or 120Hz) for newer HDMI 2.1 interfaces.
