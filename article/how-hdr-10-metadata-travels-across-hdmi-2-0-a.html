How HDR10 Metadata Travels Across HDMI 2.0a

This article explores how static HDR10 metadata is transmitted across an HDMI 2.0a connection, bridging the gap between media sources and display hardware. It covers the introduction of the CTA-861.3 standard, details how metadata is packaged into Dynamic Range and Mastering (DRM) InfoFrames, and explains how this data rides within the HDMI blanking interval to enable proper display tone mapping.

The Role of HDMI 2.0a and CTA-861.3

The baseline HDMI 2.0 standard possessed sufficient bandwidth (18 Gbps) to carry 4K video at 60 frames per second with 10-bit color depth, but it lacked a signaling mechanism to tell displays how to interpret High Dynamic Range (HDR) content. Released in early 2015, the HDMI 2.0a update resolved this by adopting the CTA-861.3 (formerly CEA-861.3) specification.

CTA-861.3 defined an electronic signaling architecture that allows a source device (such as an Ultra HD Blu-ray player, gaming console, or streaming box) to transmit static HDR metadata over the HDMI link to an HDR-capable television or monitor (the sink device).

The Packaging: Dynamic Range and Mastering (DRM) InfoFrames

HDMI transports video, audio, and auxiliary data using Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) clock and data channels. Auxiliary data is not mixed directly into the active video pixels; instead, it is transmitted during the vertical and horizontal blanking intervals—periods between lines and frames where no active pixel data is being drawn. Within these intervals lies the Data Island period.

HDMI 2.0a utilizes this Data Island period to transmit a specific auxiliary packet called the Dynamic Range and Mastering (DRM) InfoFrame.

The DRM InfoFrame carries the essential static metadata defined by SMPTE ST 2086, which includes:

  • Electro-Optical Transfer Function (EOTF): Identifies the gamma curve used, typically SMPTE ST 2084 (Perceptual Quantizer, or PQ) for HDR10.
  • Color Primaries and White Point: The x and y chromaticity coordinates of the mastering display (usually targeting the DCI-P3 color space within a BT.2020 container).
  • Luminance Range: The minimum and maximum mastering display luminance (e.g., 0.005 nits to 1,000 or 4,000 nits).
  • Content Light Levels (CTA-861.3 extensions):
    • MaxCLL (Maximum Content Light Level): The brightest single pixel in the entire program.
    • MaxFALL (Maximum Frame Average Light Level): The highest average brightness of any single frame in the program.

Transmission and Display Processing

Because HDR10 metadata is "static," the values representing MaxCLL, MaxFALL, and mastering display limits remain constant throughout the entire runtime of the video. However, the HDMI transmitter does not send the DRM InfoFrame only once.

To ensure that the display consistently maintains its HDR state—and to account for mid-stream events such as source switching, resolution renegotiation, or hot-plugging—the source repeatedly transmits the DRM InfoFrame at least once per video field or frame during the Data Island period.

When the HDMI receiver (the display) decodes the incoming TMDS stream, it separates the active video payload from the Data Island packets:

  1. Extraction: The display’s HDMI controller extracts the DRM InfoFrame from the blanking interval.
  2. Configuration: The display switches into its HDR mode, engaging the ST 2084 PQ curve and expanding its color pipeline to BT.2020.
  3. Tone Mapping: Using the static parameters from the DRM InfoFrame (primarily MaxCLL and mastering luminance), the display’s internal image processor applies a static tone-mapping curve. This compresses highlights and adjusts color tracking to match the physical capabilities of its panel without clipping detail.
