12 GHz HDMI: Skin Depth Impact on Conductor Resistance

At high-frequency operating conditions such as 12 GHz HDMI signaling, skin depth decreases to sub-micron levels, confining electrical current to an extremely thin layer along the conductor's outer perimeter. This article examines how this reduction drastically escalates alternating current (AC) conductor resistance, magnifies the deleterious effects of copper surface roughness, and drives severe signal attenuation. It also outlines the resulting signal integrity challenges—such as eye closure and jitter—along with the fabrication and design techniques required to mitigate these high-frequency losses.

The Physical Reduction of Skin Depth at 12 GHz

Skin depth (\(\delta\)) defines the distance beneath the surface of a conductor where current density drops to approximately \(1/e\) (about 37%) of its surface value. The value is calculated using the formula:

\[\delta = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f \mu}}\]

Where:

  • \(\rho\) is conductor resistivity (\(1.68 \times 10^{-8} \ \Omega\cdot\text{m}\) for annealed copper)
  • \(f\) is frequency (\(12 \times 10^9 \text{ Hz}\))
  • \(\mu\) is the magnetic permeability of the material (\(\mu_0 \approx 1.257 \times 10^{-6} \text{ H/m}\))

At direct current (DC), electrical charge is distributed uniformly throughout the entire cross-sectional area of a copper trace or cable core. However, at a 12 GHz clock or fundamental frequency, the skin depth in copper contracts to approximately 0.60 microns (600 nm).

Exponential Increase in Effective AC Resistance

Because current is restricted to a 600 nm shell, the effective cross-sectional area through which high-frequency currents flow is drastically truncated. This creates several direct consequences on conductor resistance:

  • Disproportionate Resistance Growth: AC resistance (\(R_{AC}\)) increases proportionally with the square root of frequency (\(\sqrt{f}\)). Compared to lower-standard HDMI clocks (such as HDMI 1.4 at 1.485 GHz, where skin depth is roughly 1.7 microns), the effective resistance at 12 GHz is nearly triple.
  • Current Crowding: In microstrip and stripline structures common on HDMI printed circuit boards (PCBs), electromagnetic interactions force current to concentrate heavily along the edges and the side of the trace closest to the reference ground plane, increasing local resistance beyond standard one-dimensional skin-effect estimates.

The Magnified Role of Surface Roughness

At 12 GHz, standard skin depth is smaller than the typical surface profile of standard electrodeposited (ED) copper foil. This mismatch leads to an additional increase in resistance:

  • Extended Path Length: Copper foil used in PCBs is treated with micro-nodules to ensure adhesion to dielectric substrates. Standard profiles have roughness values (\(R_z\)) between 1 to 3 microns. Because the 0.60-micron skin depth is thinner than these surface peaks and valleys, the current must follow the geometric contour of the copper teeth instead of traveling in a straight path.
  • Roughness-Induced Resistive Surge: This meandering current path artificially increases trace length and effective cross-sectional resistance. Models such as the Huray snowball model demonstrate that surface roughness can double or triple conductor loss over what smooth-conductor skin-effect equations predict at 12 GHz.

Signal Integrity Consequences for HDMI Channels

The surge in AC resistance at 12 GHz directly compromises high-speed transmission in HDMI implementations:

  • Frequency-Dependent Attenuation: High resistance creates severe insertion loss that scales rapidly with frequency. The fundamental components of the signal suffer attenuation far higher than the low-frequency components, causing edge degradation and rounding.
  • Inter-Symbol Interference (ISI) and Jitter: When trace resistance attenuates high-frequency signal edges, transitions do not reach their full rail within a single bit period. Energy from preceding bits spills into successive ones, producing significant deterministic jitter and ISI.
  • Eye Diagram Degradation: The combination of skin-depth resistance and dielectric loss rapidly flattens the eye height and narrows the eye width, potentially leading to link dropouts or high bit-error rates (BER) if the signal falls below the HDMI receiver's sensitivity threshold.

Mitigation Strategies in High-Speed Layouts

To manage elevated resistance caused by sub-micron skin depth at 12 GHz, hardware engineers implement targeted high-frequency design practices:

  • Very Low Profile (VLP) Foils: Using VLP or Hyper-Very Low Profile (HVLP) copper foils with surface roughness under 0.5 microns keeps the surface profile within the 600 nm boundary, minimizing roughness-induced resistive losses.
  • Wide Trace Geometry: Widening trace conductors increases the available surface perimeter, thereby expanding the available cross-sectional conduction band for the skin layer.
  • Active Equalization: HDMI 2.1 chipsets utilize continuous-time linear equalization (CTLE) and decision feedback equalization (DFE) within receiver PHYs to synthesize an inverse filter that compensates for the high-frequency resistive drop-off caused by skin effect.
