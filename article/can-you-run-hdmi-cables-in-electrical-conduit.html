Can You Run HDMI Cables in Electrical Conduit?

Running an HDMI cable through an in-wall conduit is permissible under building and fire codes only if the conduit is dedicated exclusively to low-voltage wiring. Under the National Electrical Code (NEC), running an HDMI cable through the same conduit as standard high-voltage electrical wiring (such as 120V or 240V lines) is a direct code violation. To remain compliant with fire and safety regulations, the HDMI cable must either occupy its own separate conduit or have a specific in-wall fire rating if run without conduit.

The Separation of High and Low Voltage

The National Electrical Code (NEC Article 725) strictly prohibits mixing Class 2 or Class 3 low-voltage cables—such as HDMI, ethernet, or speaker wire—in the same raceway, conduit, or junction box with Class 1 power and lighting circuits.

This rule exists for two primary reasons:

  1. Fire and Shock Hazards: If the insulation on standard 120V household wiring fails, high voltage can bridge directly to the HDMI cable. This can energize the low-voltage wire, destroy connected electronics (like televisions and AV receivers), shock occupants, or spark an in-wall fire.
  2. Signal Interference: Alternating current (AC) creates an electromagnetic field. Running an HDMI cable in close, parallel contact with power lines induces electromagnetic interference (EMI), leading to video dropouts, digital noise, or complete signal loss.

Using Dedicated Conduits

You can legally run an HDMI cable through in-wall conduit if that conduit is used solely for low-voltage signal lines. Installing a dedicated flexible non-metallic conduit (often called "smurf tube") or rigid PVC pipe from a television mount to an AV console is an industry-standard practice. A dedicated conduit isolates the cable, protects it from physical damage inside the wall cavity, and allows you to replace or upgrade cables easily in the future.

Required Cable Fire Ratings

Conduit provides mechanical protection, but fire codes still mandate specific jacket ratings for low-voltage cables depending on the installation environment:

  • CL2 or CL3 Rated: Standard residential in-wall installations require cables rated Class 2 (CL2) or Class 3 (CL3). These jackets are formulated to resist burning and prevent flames from traveling along the cable within wall cavities.
  • CMR (Riser) Rated: If the conduit passes vertically between floors, building codes often require a riser-rated cable to prevent flames from traveling from one level to another.
  • CMP (Plenum) Rated: If the cable or conduit passes through a plenum space (an area used for environmental air handling, such as dropped ceilings in commercial buildings), a plenum-rated cable is legally required to minimize smoke and toxic fumes in the event of a fire.

Standard, cheap HDMI patch cables purchased for desktop use generally do not possess CL2 or CL3 ratings and should not be placed behind walls without verifying the jacket stamps.

Summary of Best Practices

To wire an HDMI cable through a wall without violating building fire codes:

  • Never fish an HDMI cable into a conduit that already contains standard electrical power lines.
  • Install a separate, dedicated conduit exclusively for audio/video and data lines.
  • Maintain at least 8 to 12 inches of separation between parallel runs of low-voltage conduit and high-voltage electrical wiring. If the low-voltage conduit must cross electrical wiring, route it across at a 90-degree angle to minimize interference.
  • Verify that the HDMI cable jacket is marked with a CL2, CL3, or CMP fire rating to satisfy local building inspector requirements.
