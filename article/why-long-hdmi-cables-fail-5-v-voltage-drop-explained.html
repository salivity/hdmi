Why Long HDMI Cables Fail: 5V Voltage Drop Explained

Long passive copper HDMI cables frequently fail to display a picture, not necessarily because the high-speed video data degrades, but due to a critical voltage drop across the thin +5V conductor. This article explains the role of the 5V line in the HDMI handshake process, how electrical resistance in narrow-gauge copper wires causes power loss over distance, and why this drop triggers connection failures even when video signals remain intact.

The Function of Pin 18 (+5V Power)

Inside every standard HDMI cable is a dedicated line known as the +5V Power pin (Pin 18). This pin does not transmit video or audio data. Instead, it powers the initial communication between the source device (such as a PC, game console, or Blu-ray player) and the sink device (such as a TV or monitor).

The receiving display's circuitry relies on this 5V rail to energize the Display Data Channel (DDC) and the Hot Plug Detect (HPD) line. Through this system, the source reads the display's Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) and establishes High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP). If the 5V power fails to reach the display within acceptable limits, the handshake never completes, and the source refuses to output a video signal, resulting in a black screen.

How Voltage Drop Occurs

Voltage drop is dictated by Ohm’s Law, which states that voltage loss across a conductor is proportional to the current and the conductor's resistance (\(V = I \times R\)).

Copper has inherent resistance that increases with length and decreases with conductor thickness. To keep HDMI cables flexible and cost-effective, manufacturers typically use thin conductors rated between 28 AWG and 30 AWG. While adequate for short runs of 3 to 6 feet, a 28 AWG wire presents substantial resistance across runs of 25, 35, or 50 feet.

As current travels along the narrow +5V wire, electrical energy dissipates as heat along the length of the run. By the time the current reaches the far end of the cable, the original 5-volt supply can drop significantly.

The Critical Operating Threshold

The official HDMI specification mandates that a source supply 5.0V with a tolerance allowing down to 4.7V, capable of supplying at least 55 mA of current.

However, many source devices—particularly laptops, streaming sticks, and graphics cards—barely output the nominal 5.0V at the port, sometimes starting as low as 4.8V. When this borderline voltage encounters the resistance of a 30-foot run of thin copper wire, the delivered voltage at the display end can drop to 4.4V or lower.

At these reduced levels:

  • The receiving display's EEPROM cannot power up to send its EDID information.
  • The Hot Plug Detect mechanism fails to register that a cable is connected.
  • HDCP handshakes fail or continuously reset, causing intermittent blackouts or a permanent "No Signal" state.

Compounding Factors: Active Converters and High-Current Sinks

The issue is magnified when users attach inline accessories, such as HDMI switches, splitters, or audio extractors that do not have their own external power bricks. These devices draw operating power directly from Pin 18. Higher current draw (\(I\)) through the same resistive line directly multiplies the voltage drop (\(V\)), causing an immediate failure.

Preventing and Fixing 5V Drop

To prevent failure caused by voltage drop over long distances:

  • Use Thicker Conductors: Cables engineered with heavy 24 AWG or 26 AWG copper reduce resistance and maintain sufficient voltage over longer runs up to approximately 25 to 30 feet.
  • Power Injectors: Inline 5V voltage injectors can be placed near the destination to re-energize Pin 18 with an external power adapter.
  • Active Optical Cables (AOC): For runs exceeding 30 feet, fiber-optic hybrid cables replace copper data lines and use dedicated power-management circuits or thicker copper purely for power delivery, effectively eliminating voltage-drop-induced handshake failures.
