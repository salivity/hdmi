Why HDMI YCbCr 4:2:2 Uses a 12-Bit Container

YCbCr 4:2:2 video over HDMI is naturally constrained to a 12-bit container due to the mathematical alignment between HDMI’s physical transmission architecture and the 4:2:2 chroma subsampling structure. Under the Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) protocol, HDMI transmits data across three fixed channels at a baseline rate of 24 bits per pixel clock. Because a two-pixel block of 4:2:2 video contains four distinct color components, the 48 bits transmitted across two clock cycles are evenly divided into four 12-bit sample slots. Consequently, any YCbCr 4:2:2 signal—whether natively 8-bit, 10-bit, or 12-bit—is mapped into this fixed 12-bit transport structure.

The TMDS Channel Architecture

HDMI uses three primary TMDS data channels (Channels 0, 1, and 2) to convey active video. In standard operation:

  • Each TMDS channel transmits one 8-bit byte per pixel clock cycle.
  • Across all three channels, HDMI delivers exactly 24 bits of video payload per pixel clock.
  • The TMDS transmission clock runs synchronously at a 1:1 ratio with the pixel clock for baseline formats.

In standard uncompressed RGB or YCbCr 4:4:4 video, each channel carries one color component (such as Red, Green, and Blue, or Y, Cb, and Cr), allocating 8 bits per component for a standard 24-bit true-color pixel.

Chroma Subsampling and the 48-Bit Cycle

YCbCr 4:2:2 reduces the horizontal resolution of the chroma components while preserving full luma resolution. Color information is processed in two-pixel pairs:

  1. Pixel 1: Luma (\(Y_0\)) and shared Chroma Blue (\(Cb\))
  2. Pixel 2: Luma (\(Y_1\)) and shared Chroma Red (\(Cr\))

Across these two consecutive pixels, there are exactly four components to transmit: \(Y_0\), \(Y_1\), \(Cb\), and \(Cr\).

Because two pixels require two TMDS clock cycles, the HDMI interface provides a total data budget of:

\[\text{2 clock cycles} \times 24\text{ bits per cycle} = 48\text{ total bits}\]

Dividing the 48 available bits equally among the four components yields:

\[\frac{48\text{ bits}}{4\text{ components}} = 12\text{ bits per component}\]

Standardized Bit Packing and Padding

The Consumer Technology Association (CTA-861) and HDMI specifications define a fixed mapping scheme to pack these four components into the 48-bit frame:

  • The components are split across the three 8-bit channels over the two-clock cycle.
  • Each of the four components is allocated a dedicated 12-bit slot.
  • If the source content is 8-bit or 10-bit, the HDMI transmitter does not reduce the TMDS clock speed or alter the channel framing. Instead, it places the data into the most significant bits (MSBs) and pads the remaining least significant bits (LSBs) with zeros.

Why Lower Bit Containers Do Not Exist for 4:2:2

Creating an "8-bit container" for YCbCr 4:2:2 would yield 32 bits per two pixels (8 bits \(\times\) 4 components). Distributing 32 bits evenly across three physical TMDS channels would require fractional bit allocations per channel per clock (16 bits per pixel clock across 3 channels = \(5.\overline{33}\) bits per channel).

To avoid asynchronous clocking and complex fractional packaging, the HDMI specification mandates the 12-bit, 24-bit-per-pixel-clock container. As a result, YCbCr 4:2:2 always travels over HDMI as a 12-bit signal at the physical transport layer.
