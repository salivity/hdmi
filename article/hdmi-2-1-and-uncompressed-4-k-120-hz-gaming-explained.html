HDMI 2.1 and Uncompressed 4K 120Hz Gaming Explained

Modern gaming consoles like the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X are capable of outputting 4K resolution at 120 frames per second, providing an ultra-responsive, razor-sharp visual experience. Delivering this level of fidelity without visual compression requires transmitting massive amounts of raw data in real time. The HDMI 2.1 specification makes this possible by drastically increasing total bandwidth, overhauling how data travels across the physical cable, and modernizing packet encoding to fit uncompressed 4K 120Hz signals directly into display pipelines.

The Raw Bandwidth Problem

Transmitting uncompressed video requires multiplying horizontal pixels, vertical pixels, refresh rate, and color depth. A standard 4K frame consists of 3840 × 2160 pixels (roughly 8.3 million pixels per frame). At a 120Hz refresh rate, the source device must output nearly one billion pixels every second.

When combining this resolution and frame rate with High Dynamic Range (HDR) using a 10-bit color depth and full 4:4:4 RGB chroma subsampling (where every pixel retains its unique color and brightness data), the data rate exceeds 40 Gbps. Previous generation HDMI 2.0 cables maxed out at 18 Gbps, which limited uncompressed output to 4K at 60Hz with 8-bit color, or forced systems to degrade color data using 4:2:0 subsampling.

Transition from TMDS to Fixed Rate Link (FRL)

To cross the 40 Gbps threshold, HDMI 2.1 replaced the legacy Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) architecture with Fixed Rate Link (FRL) technology:

  • Lane Reallocation: Under TMDS, an HDMI cable utilized three data lanes and one dedicated clock lane. FRL eliminates the dedicated clock lane by embedding the clock signal directly into the data streams, converting all four physical differential pairs into high-speed data lanes.
  • Scalable Speed: FRL operates in stepped lane rates—ranging from 3 Gbps up to 12 Gbps per lane. By running all four lanes at their maximum 12 Gbps capability, HDMI 2.1 achieves a ceiling of 48 Gbps of raw bandwidth.

More Efficient Encoding

Alongside lane re-allocation, HDMI 2.1 switched from the older 8b/10b encoding scheme to a far more efficient 16b/18b encoding mechanism:

  • Lower Overhead: The older 8b/10b encoding incurred a 20% overhead penalty, meaning a fifth of all transmitted bits were used purely for signal balance rather than actual pixel data.
  • Higher Usable Throughput: The 16b/18b scheme reduces this overhead to approximately 11.1%. Out of the total 48 Gbps physical bandwidth, roughly 42.6 Gbps is reserved for usable payload data. Because uncompressed 4K 120Hz 10-bit RGB video requires approximately 40.1 Gbps, it fits within the uncompressed payload limits of full-bandwidth FRL without dropping frames or stripping color information.

Console Implementation

High-end consoles leverage these specifications differently depending on hardware architecture. For example, the Xbox Series X features a 40 Gbps FRL pipeline, providing just enough native throughput to carry 4K at 120Hz in 10-bit uncompressed RGB. The PlayStation 5 utilizes a 32 Gbps FRL pipeline; when running 4K at 120Hz, it automatically downsamples the chroma to 4:2:2 or applies VESA Display Stream Compression (DSC)—a visually lossless compression standard built into the HDMI 2.1 specification—to fit the payload within its designated bandwidth lane.

Through FRL signaling, four-lane architecture, and reduced encoding overhead, HDMI delivers the sustained, multi-gigabit data stream required to display genuine, low-latency, uncompressed 4K video at 120 frames per second.
