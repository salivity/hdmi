What Is an HDMI Type A Connector?

The HDMI Type A connector is the standard, full-sized HDMI plug found on most modern televisions, monitors, gaming consoles, and desktop graphics cards. First introduced with the initial High-Definition Multimedia Interface specification in 2002, Type A remains the most widely recognized audio/video connection in consumer electronics. This article covers the physical dimensions, internal pin configuration, and practical applications that distinguish the Type A connector from other HDMI variants.

Physical Design and Dimensions

The Type A plug features an asymmetrical, trapezoidal shape designed to prevent incorrect insertion. Externally, the male connector measures 13.9 mm wide by 4.45 mm high. The female receptacle measures approximately 14 mm wide by 4.55 mm high, allowing for a snug, friction-fit connection.

Unlike older display standards such as VGA or DVI, HDMI Type A does not use thumbscrews to secure the cable in place. It relies on internal mechanical friction and small retention clips within the port assembly to maintain a stable electrical connection.

Pinout and Electrical Configuration

The HDMI Type A interface contains 19 individual pins. These pins carry uncompressed digital video, multi-channel audio, auxiliary data, and power across a single cable assembly:

  • Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS): Pins 1 through 12 are dedicated to four differential TMDS pairs (Data 0, Data 1, Data 2, and the Clock channel), each accompanied by a dedicated ground shield pin.
  • Consumer Electronics Control (CEC): Pin 13 carries the CEC protocol, allowing a single remote to command multiple interconnected devices.
  • Utility and HEC: Pin 14 is reserved for utility functions, including the HDMI Ethernet Channel (HEC) introduced in HDMI 1.4.
  • Display Data Channel (DDC): Pins 15 (SCL) and 16 (SDA) form an I2C-based link used for EDID handshakes and HDCP copy protection communication.
  • Ground and Power: Pin 17 provides the DDC/CEC/HEC ground return, Pin 18 delivers a +5V power feed (minimum 55 mA) to power sink devices or active adapters, and Pin 19 handles Hot Plug Detect (HPD).

Comparison with Other HDMI Types

The HDMI specification defines five distinct physical connector profiles designated by letters:

  • Type A (Standard): 19 pins, measuring 13.9 mm × 4.45 mm.
  • Type B (Dual-Link): 29 pins, measuring 21.2 mm × 4.45 mm; designed for extremely high resolutions but never adopted in mass-market consumer products.
  • Type C (Mini HDMI): 19 pins, measuring 10.42 mm × 2.42 mm; retains the same electrical capabilities as Type A in a smaller footprint for camcorders and DSLR cameras.
  • Type D (Micro HDMI): 19 pins, measuring 5.83 mm × 2.20 mm; designed for ultra-compact devices like action cameras and compact single-board computers.
  • Type E (Automotive): 19 pins with a locking mechanical shell and internal friction tab, engineered to resist vibration and moisture in vehicles.

Because Types A, C, and D share the identical 19-pin electrical schema, simple passive adapter cables can bridge Type A ports to Mini or Micro connections without active signal processing.

Common Uses and Backwards Compatibility

Type A connectors support every HDMI standard release, from HDMI 1.0 through HDMI 2.1. While newer standards require higher-bandwidth cable construction to handle features like 4K at 120Hz, 8K resolutions, and Variable Refresh Rate (VRR), the physical form factor of the connector remains identical. A modern HDMI 2.1 Type A cable plugs directly into a legacy HDMI 1.0 Type A port from 2002, maintaining complete physical and electrical backwards compatibility across generations.
