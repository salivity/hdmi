How HDMI Transports 32-Channel LPCM Audio

This article examines how modern HDMI standards transmit up to 32 channels of uncompressed Linear Pulse Code Modulation (LPCM) audio to support immersive, object-based, and high-density spatial formats. It details the bandwidth expansion introduced in HDMI 2.0, the packetization methods used within HDMI Data Islands, clock synchronization mechanics, and the metadata frameworks that map discrete LPCM channels to physical 3D speaker layouts.

The Bandwidth Evolution: HDMI 1.4 vs. HDMI 2.0+

Legacy HDMI versions (HDMI 1.0 through 1.4) supported a maximum of 8 uncompressed audio channels at sample rates up to 192 kHz. To accommodate complex immersive formats such as 22.2 surround sound or high-count discrete spatial arrays (like 9.1.6 or 11.1.8), HDMI 2.0 expanded the audio architecture to support up to 32 audio channels.

Rather than specifying an absolute sample rate ceiling per channel, the standard establishes an aggregate audio sample rate limit of 1536 kHz. This pool can be partitioned across different channel counts:

  • 32 channels sampled at up to 48 kHz (32 × 48 kHz = 1536 kHz)
  • 16 channels sampled at up to 96 kHz (16 × 96 kHz = 1536 kHz)
  • 8 channels sampled at up to 192 kHz (8 × 192 kHz = 1536 kHz)

By supporting bit depths of 16, 20, or 24 bits across all 32 channels, HDMI delivers completely uncompressed PCM audio streams without introducing compression artifacts or latency.

Data Island Transmission and Packet Multiplexing

HDMI does not use dedicated physical conductors for audio. Instead, audio data is time-division multiplexed alongside video data across the primary Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) data lanes.

Audio data is transmitted exclusively during the Data Island periods, which occur during horizontal and vertical video blanking intervals. During these intervals:

  1. The interface switches to a robust 4-bit to 10-bit encoding scheme called TERC4 (TMDS Error Reduction Coding).
  2. Data is formatted into standard 32-byte Data Island packets, consisting of a 4-byte header and a 28-byte body protected by Bose-Chaudhuri-Hocquenghem (BCH) error-correcting code.
  3. Audio sample data and control info are interleaved directly between pixel lines.

Because modern 4K and 8K video modes have vast blanking intervals and run at higher aggregate pixel clocks, the capacity to insert Data Island packets far exceeds the requirements of high-channel LPCM.

3D Audio Sample Packets and Multi-Stream Audio

Standard HDMI Audio Sample Packets (ASP) contain four sub-packets, each holding up to two audio channels, creating an 8-channel ceiling. To overcome this limitation for 32-channel LPCM, HDMI introduced two dedicated packet architectures:

  • 3D Audio Sample Packets (3D ASP): Designed for discrete spatial loudspeaker systems. These packets preserve the temporal synchronization of up to 32 concurrent channels destined for a single multi-channel spatial render.
  • Multi-Stream Audio Sample Packets (MSASP): Designed to deliver multiple independent audio streams (for example, multi-language feeds or dual-view gaming) across the same link, assigning distinct channel sub-groups to different destinations.

Within 3D ASPs, sample payloads are labeled with stream and sub-packet IDs that indicate the precise temporal phase of the sample, ensuring that phase alignment across all 32 channels is maintained down to the microsecond level.

Audio Metadata Packets and Channel Allocation

For an AV receiver or spatial audio processor to correctly render 32 discrete uncompressed streams, it requires positional context. HDMI manages this through periodic Audio Metadata Packets (AMP).

The AMP communicates:

  1. 3D Channel Allocation (CA): Aligns each logical channel index (Channel 1 to Channel 32) to standard spatial positions defined by the CTA-861 standard, covering height, ear-level, top-middle, and low-frequency channels.
  2. Channel-to-Speaker Mapping: Informs the sink device of the target speaker geometry, enabling proper routing for layouts such as 7.1.4, 9.1.6, or NHK 22.2.
  3. Downmix Metadata: Provides coefficients and instructions for the sink device to downmix the 32-channel stream to lower speaker counts if the receiving environment cannot deploy all discrete channels.

Audio Clock Regeneration

Because audio and video share the same physical clock, the audio sample clock must be regenerated at the receiver. HDMI accomplishes this without a dedicated audio clock pin via Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) packets.

The source transmits two values:

  • \(N\): A predetermined integer value defined by the specification for specific sample rates and video clocks.
  • \(CTS\) (Cycle Time Stamp): A measured metric representing the number of video clock cycles counted within a specific audio cycle window.

The sink uses a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) synthesizer to calculate the original audio master clock (\(f_{audio}\)) using the formula:

\[f_{audio} = f_{pixel} \times \frac{N}{128 \times CTS}\]

For 32-channel LPCM streams running at the maximum 1536 kHz aggregate limit, high-precision ACR ensures low jitter and eliminates phase drift between height, surround, and screen channels.
