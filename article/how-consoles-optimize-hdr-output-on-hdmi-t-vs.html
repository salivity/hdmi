How Consoles Optimize HDR Output on HDMI TVs

Modern video game consoles optimize High Dynamic Range (HDR) for HDMI-connected televisions through automated hardware handshakes, source-side tone mapping, and system-level calibration. By communicating directly with the display’s Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) over HDMI, systems like the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X determine the exact peak brightness, black levels, and color capabilities of the screen. This allows the console to render lighting and color dynamically within the television's specific physical limits, preventing visual artifacts such as clipped highlights, crushed blacks, or increased input latency.

HDMI Handshaking and EDID Negotiation

When a console connects to a TV via HDMI, it performs an initial handshake that queries the display’s EDID and DisplayID blocks. This metadata informs the console whether the television supports HDR standards such as HDR10, Dolby Vision, or HLG. It also conveys the TV's native color gamut coverage (typically DCI-P3 within a Rec. 2020 container) and signal limits. Modern HDMI 2.0 and HDMI 2.1 connections use this data exchange to negotiate color depth (10-bit or 12-bit) and pixel format (full RGB 4:4:4 vs. chroma-subsampled 4:2:2), ensuring the visual pipeline avoids color banding while staying within interface bandwidth limits.

Source-Based Tone Mapping (SBTM) and HGiG

Traditional HDR implementation in movies relies on the television to adapt the incoming signal to its own brightness capabilities, a process called display tone mapping. For video games, display-side tone mapping can introduce input lag and inaccurate clipping because the TV cannot predict the dynamic range of an interactive 3D scene.

To solve this, consoles utilize standards established by the HDR Gaming Interest Group (HGiG) alongside HDMI 2.1’s Source-Based Tone Mapping (SBTM):

  • HGiG Mode: When enabled on both the TV and console, the television disables its internal dynamic tone mapping. The TV reports its raw minimum, maximum, and full-frame luminance capabilities directly to the console.
  • Console-Side Mapping: The console's GPU performs all luminance adjustments natively during the rendering pass. Because the game engine handles the compression of brightness curves before sending the frame through the HDMI cable, specular highlights and shadow details are preserved without double-processing.

System-Level Calibration Profiles

Both PlayStation and Xbox operating systems feature built-in HDR setup wizards that create a global profile applied across all compatible games:

  1. Black Level (Minimum Luminance): Sets the absolute black floor, allowing the console to account for the difference between self-emissive displays (OLEDs with near-zero nits) and backlit LCDs (which have elevated black levels).
  2. Peak White (Maximum Luminance): Identifies the highest nit value the TV can produce on a small highlight window before hard-clipping white detail.
  3. Full-Field White: Defines the maximum brightness the TV can sustain across the entire panel, accounting for Automatic Brightness Limiters (ABL) found in modern displays.

Game engines query these system-level profile values to dynamically position the dynamic range of in-game exposure, sun glare, and dark environments to match the TV screen.

Latency Optimization with ALLM

HDR image processing historically added significant processing overhead on modern TVs. Modern consoles mitigate this via HDMI Auto Low Latency Mode (ALLM). Upon detecting an active gaming load, the console sends an InfoFrame over HDMI that commands the TV to switch to its dedicated Game Mode. This turns off post-processing filters that cause latency while keeping the wide color gamut and high-contrast HDR processing paths fully active.
