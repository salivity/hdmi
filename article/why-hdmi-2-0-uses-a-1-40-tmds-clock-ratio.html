Why HDMI 2.0 Uses a 1/40 TMDS Clock Ratio

HDMI 2.0 expands transmission bandwidth to support 4K resolution at 60 Hz by increasing the maximum data rate from 3.4 Gbps to 6.0 Gbps per lane. To achieve these higher speeds across standard copper cables without causing severe signal degradation, the HDMI specification altered the physical relationship between the transmitted clock and data. For lane rates exceeding 3.4 Gbps, the standard switches from the traditional 1/10 TMDS clock ratio to a 1/40 clock ratio, mitigating high-frequency transmission loss, minimizing electromagnetic interference (EMI), and ensuring backward compatibility.

The Legacy 1/10 Clock Ratio

HDMI uses Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) across three primary data lanes and one dedicated clock lane. Under the HDMI 1.4 specification and earlier, the system uses an 8b/10b-style encoding scheme where every 8-bit byte of raw data is encoded into a 10-bit TMDS character.

In this legacy scheme, the TMDS clock channel operates at the character rate—exactly one clock cycle for every 10 serial bits transmitted. This establishes a 1/10 clock-to-data ratio:

\[\text{TMDS Clock Frequency} = \frac{\text{Bit Rate per Lane}}{10}\]

At the maximum HDMI 1.4 speed of 3.4 Gbps per lane, the physical TMDS clock runs at 340 MHz.

The High-Frequency Problem at 6.0 Gbps

HDMI 2.0 raised the ceiling to 6.0 Gbps per lane (18 Gbps total across all three data lanes). If the system had retained the 1/10 ratio at 6.0 Gbps, the dedicated clock lane would be required to transmit at:

\[\frac{6000 \text{ Mbps}}{10} = 600 \text{ MHz}\]

Transmitting a 600 MHz reference clock over passive, low-cost copper cables introduces major physical challenges:

  1. High-Frequency Attenuation: Signal loss increases dramatically as frequencies rise, resulting in closed "eye diagrams" and clock jitter at the receiver.
  2. Electromagnetic Interference (EMI): A discrete, single-frequency 600 MHz clock radiates significant RF energy, making regulatory compliance (such as FCC and CE limits) difficult to meet.
  3. Crosstalk: A high-frequency clock signal couples into adjacent high-speed data pairs inside the cable assembly, degrading the overall signal-to-noise ratio.

How the 1/40 Ratio Solves the Problem

To prevent these physical-layer issues, HDMI 2.0 defines a clock-divided mode for any signal operating above 3.4 Gbps. Instead of transmitting the clock at the full character rate, the transmitter divides the character clock by four, resulting in a 1/40 ratio relative to the serial bit rate:

\[\text{TMDS Clock Frequency} = \frac{\text{Bit Rate per Lane}}{40}\]

At the maximum HDMI 2.0 rate of 6.0 Gbps per lane, the clock channel operates at:

\[\frac{6000 \text{ Mbps}}{40} = 150 \text{ MHz}\]

A 150 MHz signal is lower in frequency than the 340 MHz clock used by maximum-spec HDMI 1.4 transmissions. This low frequency ensures that standard HDMI Category 2 cables can transmit the clock channel reliably over practical distances without excessive attenuation, skew, or radiated emissions.

Implementation at the Receiver

Because the clock frequency is divided on the cable, the sink device (display or receiver) cannot directly sample characters using the incoming clock edges. The receiver handles the 1/40 ratio through the following steps:

  • Clock Multiplication: The receiver uses an internal Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) or frequency multiplier to multiply the 150 MHz incoming clock by 40 to reconstruct the 6.0 GHz bit clock, and by 4 to reconstruct the character clock.
  • Scrambling: Because high-speed data lanes run at full rate (6.0 Gbps) and can produce EMI spikes, HDMI 2.0 mandates data scrambling on lanes exceeding 3.4 Gbps to distribute spectral energy evenly.
  • SCDC Negotiation: The source and sink coordinate the ratio switch using the Status and Control Data Channel (SCDC) via the \(I^2C\) line. The source reads the display's capabilities and configures the TMDS_Bit_Clock_Ratio bit in the sink's registers to 1 (indicating 1/40) before sending high-bandwidth signals.
