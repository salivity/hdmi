How AVRs Prevent Audio Pops During HDMI Re-Sync

When an HDMI display connection changes resolution, frame rate, or dynamic range (such as switching from SDR to HDR), the connection performs a handshake or clock re-sync. This abrupt interruption cuts the continuous digital data stream, which can cause severe, speaker-damaging audio spikes or "pops" if left unmanaged. AV receivers (AVRs) prevent these loud transients through a multi-stage defense system that combines digital stream monitoring, DSP soft-muting, analog hardware relays, and clock-stabilization delays.

The Source of the HDMI Audio Pop

HDMI multiplexes uncompressed or bitstream audio packets directly into the video blanking intervals. When a video mode changes, the HDMI source, repeater (the AVR), and sink (the display) renegotiate the High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) and Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) handshakes.

During this split-second re-sync, the incoming audio clock and packet structure break down. If a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) attempts to process this fragmented, incomplete, or corrupted digital data, it interprets missing samples as massive, instantaneous voltage changes—resulting in full-scale digital noise or loud DC offset pops sent directly to the amplifiers.

Digital Stream Monitoring and DSP Auto-Mute

To combat this, the AVR's HDMI receiver chip and Digital Signal Processor (DSP) continuously monitor the digital stream for valid preamble sync words, channel status blocks, and HDMI Audio InfoFrames.

The moment a video re-sync begins, the HDMI receiver detects a loss of signal lock or anomalous jitter in the Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) packets. Before corrupted packets reach the DAC, the DSP triggers an instantaneous digital auto-mute. Rather than cutting the sound instantly—which could itself generate an audible click—modern DSPs apply a rapid digital ramp-down (a "soft mute") over a few milliseconds, reducing the signal level to absolute zero.

Hardware Muting Circuits and Physical Relays

Digital muting protects the DAC inputs, but changes within the DAC's internal power states and digital filter resets during a re-sync can also produce low-frequency analog thumps. To neutralize this, AVRs use hardware-level muting circuits positioned along the analog output stage.

  • Solid-State Muting Transistors: High-speed JFETs or bipolar transistors are wired parallel to the analog signal path, shunting the pre-amplifier signal directly to ground whenever a mute flag is raised.
  • Mechanical Relays: Many AVRs feature physical relays that physically disconnect the pre-out stage from the power amplifier, or the amplifier stage from the speaker terminals. The distinct, faint mechanical "click" heard inside an AVR during an input or format switch is this relay engaging to protect both the speakers and the listener's ears.

Zero-Crossing Detection

Abruptly opening or closing a circuit while an alternating current (AC) audio wave is at its peak produces a steep, square-like voltage step that the human ear perceives as an audible tick or snap.

Quality AVRs employ zero-crossing detectors in their muting circuits. The receiver monitors the audio waveform and only engages or disengages the mute state at the precise microsecond the electrical signal crosses the zero-volt reference line. This ensures a completely silent transition into and out of the muted state.

Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) Stabilization and Delayed Unmuting

Once the HDMI video handshake completes and the display displays an image, the audio is not unmuted immediately. The AVR must wait for its Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) circuits to accurately synchronize with the incoming HDMI audio clock values (the Cycle Time Stamp, or CTS, and audio clock divider, or N).

The receiver enforces a buffer delay, typically lasting between 200 to 500 milliseconds after the video signal appears stable. During this window, the receiver verifies that the bitstream (such as Dolby Atmos, DTS:X, or multi-channel PCM) is consistent, uncorrupted, and correctly formatted. Once stable lock is confirmed, the AVR releases the hardware mute and ramps the digital volume back up smoothly to the user's set listening level.
