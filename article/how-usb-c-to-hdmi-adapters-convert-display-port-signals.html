How USB-C to HDMI Adapters Convert DisplayPort Signals

Modern USB-C to HDMI adapters bridge two fundamentally incompatible display standards by using active protocol converter chipsets. When a host device connects to a display via USB-C, it transmits video using DisplayPort Alternate Mode (DP Alt Mode), sending packetized video, audio, and control data across high-speed differential lanes. Because HDMI uses a continuous, raster-based transmission scheme with different electrical characteristics, an internal converter chip inside the adapter decodes the DisplayPort packets, extracts the raw pixel data, retimes the signal, and encodes it into HDMI-compliant Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) signals.

DisplayPort Alternate Mode Negotiation

The conversion process begins at the physical USB-C port. Through the Configuration Channel (CC) pins, the host device (such as a laptop or smartphone) and the adapter negotiate a connection using the USB Power Delivery (USB-PD) protocol. The adapter advertises itself as a video sink capable of accepting DisplayPort signals.

Once recognized, the host switches its high-speed data lanes into DisplayPort Alternate Mode. The USB-C connector allocates either two or four of its high-speed differential pairs to carry native DisplayPort signals directly from the host's Graphics Processing Unit (GPU), bypassing the standard USB data controller.

The Incompatibility Between DisplayPort and HDMI

DisplayPort and HDMI were engineered with completely different architectures:

  • Data Transmission: DisplayPort operates like a network protocol, breaking video, audio, and metadata into discrete micro-packets over an embedded-clock link using 8b/10b or 128b/132b encoding. HDMI (prior to HDMI 2.1) transmits video as an unpacketized, continuous raster stream with a separate, dedicated physical clock lane using TMDS.
  • Electrical Signaling: DisplayPort uses low-voltage, AC-coupled differential signaling with variable voltage swing and pre-emphasis levels. Legacy HDMI uses 3.3V DC-coupled Current Mode Logic (CML).
  • Control Channels: DisplayPort manages display identification and status via a bi-directional, packetized Auxiliary (AUX) channel. HDMI relies on the I2C-based Display Data Channel (DDC).

Because passive wiring cannot resolve these structural and electrical mismatches, active processing is required.

The Role of the Protocol Converter Chip

Inside the USB-C to HDMI adapter sits a dedicated protocol converter integrated circuit (from manufacturers such as Realtek, Parade Technologies, or Synaptics). The chip functions as a complete receiver-transmitter pipeline:

  1. Physical Layer Deserialization: The chip’s DP receiver captures the incoming differential signals, applies equalization to eliminate cable-induced signal degradation, and recovers the embedded clock using a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL).
  2. De-packetization: The processor strips away DisplayPort packet framing, error-checking data, and link headers, unpacking the raw stream of uncompressed pixel data (RGB or YCbCr) and digital audio streams.
  3. Line and Frame Buffering: Because DisplayPort packet bursts do not align perfectly with HDMI's continuous timing requirements, the chip temporarily stores pixel data in a small internal FIFO (First-In, First-Out) memory buffer to synchronize the timing.
  4. HDMI Encoding: The chip reads the pixel data out of the buffer and feeds it into an HDMI transmitter engine. For HDMI 2.0 and earlier, it applies TMDS 8b/10b encoding across three data lanes and synthesizes a synchronous fourth clock lane. For HDMI 2.1 (4K at 120Hz or 8K), it encodes the data into Fixed Rate Link (FRL) packets without a separate physical clock lane.
  5. Level Shifting: The synthesized digital signals are passed through high-speed drivers that adjust the electrical voltages to HDMI’s CML standards.

Sideband and Control Signal Translation

Beyond the primary video and audio data, the adapter constantly translates metadata in real time:

  • EDID Translation: The adapter translates HDMI I2C DDC transactions into DisplayPort AUX channel commands. This enables the host GPU to read the display's Extended Display Identification Data (EDID), learning its supported resolutions, refresh rates, and color formats.
  • HDCP Conversion: If protected content is streaming, the adapter performs High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) conversion, acting as an authorized repeater. It decrypts HDCP 1.3 or 2.2/2.3 streams originating from the DisplayPort host and immediately re-encrypts them into the corresponding HDMI HDCP standard before transmission to the screen.
  • Hot-Plug Detect (HPD): The adapter monitors the physical HDMI HPD pin. When an HDMI display is plugged or unplugged, the adapter signals an interrupt on the DisplayPort AUX channel to alert the host system instantly.
