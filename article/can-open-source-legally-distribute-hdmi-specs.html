Can Open Source Legally Distribute HDMI Specs?

Open-source software projects cannot legally distribute proprietary HDMI specification documents. The HDMI Forum maintains strict copyright, trademark, and contractual controls over all official documentation, restricting access exclusively to paying members who sign non-disclosure agreements (NDAs). Redistributing these copyrighted documents or publishing code that directly exposes confidential architectural details violates intellectual property laws and licensing contracts, creating significant hurdles for open-source graphics drivers and operating systems.

Copyright and Proprietary Ownership

The High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) standard is not an open standard. It is developed and licensed by HDMI Licensing Administrator, Inc. and the HDMI Forum. The technical specification documents are original creative works protected under international copyright law. Unauthorized copying, hosting, or distribution of these texts without explicit authorization constitutes direct copyright infringement.

Non-Disclosure Agreements and Membership Terms

Access to modern HDMI specifications—particularly HDMI 2.1 and newer—requires paid membership in the HDMI Forum. Members must sign restrictive NDAs that legally prohibit them from sharing technical documentation, implementation guidelines, and test specifications with external third parties. Because open-source software relies on public repositories and community distribution, publishing the documents or transcribing proprietary mechanisms into open-source code violates these binding agreements.

The Conflict Between Open Source and HDMI Licensing

The conflict between open-source principles and proprietary standards became prominent when the HDMI Forum officially barred hardware manufacturers, such as AMD, from implementing certain advanced features (such as 4K at 120Hz and Variable Refresh Rate) in open-source Linux drivers. The Forum ruled that open-sourcing the driver code would effectively expose proprietary architecture and compliance tests to the public, violating their policy against public disclosure.

Legal Alternatives for Developers

Open-source developers facing HDMI restrictions typically rely on limited alternatives:

  • Clean-Room Reverse Engineering: Developers may study hardware behavior externally to write drivers, provided they do not access, copy, or distribute the proprietary documentation or violate anti-circumvention provisions.
  • Proprietary Firmware/Blobs: Hardware vendors sometimes isolate proprietary HDMI functionality within closed-source user-space libraries or binary firmware blobs to keep implementation details private.
  • Open Display Standards: Many open-source platforms advocate for genuinely open standards like DisplayPort, which provide high-bandwidth display capabilities with far fewer legal barriers.

Under no circumstances does open-source licensing override proprietary copyrights. Any project that hosts, mirrors, or publicly circulates official HDMI specification documents risks DMCA takedown notices, breach-of-contract lawsuits, and statutory copyright damages.
