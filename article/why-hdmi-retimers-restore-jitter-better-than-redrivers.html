Why HDMI Retimers Restore Jitter Better Than Redrivers

As HDMI bandwidth demands have scaled with the HDMI 2.0 (up to 18 Gbps) and HDMI 2.1 (up to 48 Gbps) standards, maintaining signal integrity across circuit board traces and long cables has become a critical engineering challenge. Both analog redrivers and retimers serve as signal conditioning devices, but they handle high-frequency degradation in fundamentally different ways. While a redriver merely amplifies and equalizes an incoming analog waveform—passing along and sometimes worsening timing errors—an HDMI retimer completely reconstructs the digital signal using Clock and Data Recovery (CDR). Consequently, a retimer actively restores horizontal timing margins and eliminates accumulated jitter, whereas an analog redriver cannot.

How an Analog Redriver Handles Signals

An analog redriver is essentially an equalization and amplification device. It typically employs a Continuous-Time Linear Equalizer (CTLE) at its input and a programmable gain amplifier at its output.

When a high-speed differential HDMI signal travels through a channel, high-frequency components attenuate more rapidly than low-frequency components, causing Inter-Symbol Interference (ISI) and closing the eye diagram vertically. The redriver compensates for this channel loss by boosting the attenuated high frequencies and increasing the signal's voltage amplitude.

However, a redriver operates entirely in the analog domain without awareness of the underlying digital clock. While it successfully restores vertical eye height (voltage margin), it passes the signal's existing phase variations directly to the receiver. Furthermore, the active components inside the redriver introduce their own intrinsic noise and thermal jitter, meaning the signal exiting a redriver often carries more total horizontal jitter than the signal entering it.

How an HDMI Retimer Restores Jitter Margins

An HDMI retimer is a mixed-signal device that combines analog equalization with a digital regeneration block centered on a Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) circuit.

Instead of just amplifying the degraded waveform, a retimer executes a multi-step restoration process:

  1. Equalization: Like the redriver, the retimer’s input stage uses CTLE (and often a Decision Feedback Equalizer, or DFE) to open the closed eye diagram.
  2. Clock and Data Recovery (CDR): The CDR circuit extracts the embedded clock from the data stream and uses a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) to generate a clean, low-jitter local clock.
  3. De-jittered Re-sampling: Using this clean local clock, the retimer samples the equalized incoming signal at optimal points within the eye, resolving each symbol into a definitive logic 0 or 1.
  4. Re-transmission: The retimer's output driver transmits the digital bits using a freshly synthesized clock with exceptionally low phase noise.

By re-sampling the data, the retimer acts as a "jitter barrier." High-frequency random jitter (RJ) and deterministic jitter (DJ) that accumulated across the preceding channel are effectively stripped away. The horizontal eye width is reset back to near-ideal specifications, restoring crucial timing margins for the downstream HDMI sink.

Summary Comparison: Retimer vs. Redriver

  • Jitter Handling: A redriver amplifies and accumulates both random and deterministic jitter. A retimer extracts the data and transmits it on a new clock, resetting the jitter budget.
  • Eye Diagram Impact: Redrivers primarily open the eye diagram vertically (voltage margin). Retimers open the eye diagram both vertically (voltage margin) and horizontally (timing margin).
  • Suitability for Modern HDMI: At HDMI 2.1 Fixed Rate Link (FRL) rates of up to 12 Gbps per lane, the unit interval (UI) shrinks to approximately 83 picoseconds. In these narrow timing windows, voltage boosting alone is insufficient. Retimers are required to keep total jitter within HDMI compliance masks across complex board routing and extended cable distances.
