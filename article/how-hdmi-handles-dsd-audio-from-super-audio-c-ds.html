How HDMI Handles DSD Audio from Super Audio CDs

This article explains how High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) transmits Direct Stream Digital (DSD) audio from Super Audio CD (SACD) players to modern audio-video receivers (AVRs) and processors. It covers the technical specifications introduced in HDMI version updates, the transmission methods used—including native bitstreaming and Pulse-Code Modulation (PCM) conversion—and how hardware limitations and digital signal processing impact the final playback.

The Origin of DSD Support over HDMI

Super Audio CD uses Direct Stream Digital (DSD), a 1-bit delta-sigma modulation format with a sampling rate of 2.8224 MHz (64 times the sampling rate of standard Red Book CD audio). Because early digital connections like S/PDIF (optical and coaxial) lacked the bandwidth and copy-protection protocols required by the SACD standard, DSD playback was initially restricted to proprietary connections or analog outputs.

This changed with the introduction of HDMI 1.2 in 2005. HDMI 1.2 added native support for "One-Bit Audio" streams, enabling up to 8 channels of uncompressed DSD audio to travel across a standard HDMI cable protected by High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP).

Method 1: Native DSD Bitstreaming

When configured for native DSD output, the SACD player acts purely as a transport. The raw 1-bit DSD bitstream is packaged into HDMI audio sample packets and sent directly across the interface to the AVR.

  1. Audio InfoFrames: The source device transmits an HDMI Audio InfoFrame that specifies the audio coding type as "One Bit Audio."
  2. Clock Synchronization: Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) packets maintain clock synchronization between the player and the receiver to minimize jitter.
  3. Decoding: The receiver's internal Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) must natively support DSD. The DAC converts the 1-bit stream directly into analog audio signals without intermediate conversion.

Limitation: Most modern AVRs rely on digital signal processors (DSPs) to perform bass management, distance delays, and room correction (such as Audyssey, Dirac Live, or YPAO). DSP chips operate strictly in the multi-bit PCM domain. Consequently, native DSD bitstreaming usually forces the AVR into a "Pure Direct" mode, bypassing all room equalization and digital processing.

Method 2: DSD-to-PCM Conversion

To preserve digital room correction and bass management, or to accommodate receivers that lack native DSD DACs, DSD is often transcoded into high-resolution Linear PCM (LPCM). This conversion can happen at two stages:

  • In the Player: The SACD player converts the 1-bit/2.8224 MHz DSD stream into multi-channel 24-bit PCM at a sample rate of 88.2 kHz or 176.4 kHz (direct integer multiples of 44.1 kHz). The converted PCM stream is then transmitted over HDMI, which is compatible with virtually every HDMI-capable receiver since HDMI 1.0.
  • In the Receiver: The player sends native DSD over HDMI, but the AVR immediately transcodes the incoming DSD to PCM internally before applying room correction filters and sending the data to the DAC.

Because 88.2 kHz/24-bit or 176.4 kHz/24-bit PCM provides sufficient dynamic range and frequency response to capture the full spectrum of a 2.8224 MHz DSD file, transcoding preserves the sonic detail of the original SACD recording while granting full access to modern speaker-calibration features.

Hardware Handshaking and EDID

Whether an SACD player transmits native DSD or transcoded PCM depends on the Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) handshake. When connected, the receiver sends its EDID data to the player, listing its audio capabilities. If the EDID reports support for One-Bit Audio, the player enables native DSD transmission options in its settings menu. If the AVR does not declare One-Bit Audio support, the player automatically defaults to internal DSD-to-PCM conversion.
