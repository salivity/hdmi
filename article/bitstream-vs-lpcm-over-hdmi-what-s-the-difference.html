Bitstream vs LPCM Over HDMI: What's the Difference?

When configuring home theater systems, gaming consoles, or media streamers via HDMI, users frequently encounter the choice between Bitstream and LPCM (Linear Pulse-Code Modulation) audio outputs. The fundamental difference lies in which device decodes the audio track: Bitstream sends raw, compressed data across the HDMI cable to be decoded by the AV receiver or soundbar, whereas LPCM decodes the compressed audio at the source device first, sending raw, uncompressed audio channels to the sound system. While both methods can deliver identical fidelity, the choice impacts support for spatial audio formats, system sounds, and latency.

How LPCM Works

Linear Pulse-Code Modulation (LPCM) is an uncompressed, digital representation of an analog audio signal. When an HDMI source—such as a PC, PlayStation 5, or Apple TV—is set to LPCM, the source decodes the original media format (such as Dolby Digital, Dolby TrueHD, or DTS-HD Master Audio) internally.

Once unpacked, the source device transmits the individual, uncompressed audio channels directly over the HDMI cable to the receiver. Because the heavy lifting of audio decoding is finished before transmission, the receiver only needs to convert the digital PCM signal into analog power for the speakers.

  • Advantages: LPCM allows the source device to mix multiple audio streams simultaneously, such as in-game audio and voice chat or menu clicks over background video. It also minimizes processing latency, making it the preferred format for gaming and computing.
  • Limitations: Standard multi-channel LPCM cannot carry traditional proprietary spatial metadata, which means full Dolby Atmos or DTS:X playback may be disabled unless encapsulated inside specialized containers like Dolby MAT (Metadata-enhanced Audio Transmission).

How Bitstreaming Works

Bitstreaming bypasses the source device's internal audio processor entirely. The source reads the compressed audio data straight from the disk, file, or stream and transmits the raw bitstream across the HDMI interface untouched.

When the AV receiver or soundbar receives the bitstream, its internal digital signal processor (DSP) identifies the codec, unpacks the data, and maps the audio channels to the connected speaker setup.

  • Advantages: Bitstreaming is essential for passing object-based spatial audio metadata, including Dolby Atmos and DTS:X, directly to an external processor. It also allows the AV receiver's display to show the exact codec being processed (e.g., "Dolby TrueHD"), confirming that the audio is playing in its intended format.
  • Limitations: The source device cannot inject its own sounds (such as user interface effects or secondary audio commentary) into the stream without entirely re-encoding the signal on the fly, which can introduce audio lag.

Audio Quality Comparison

From a pure sound quality perspective, Bitstream and LPCM are functionally identical. A lossless audio track (such as Dolby TrueHD) contains the exact same acoustic data whether it is unpacked by a media player's processor or an AV receiver's processor. Neither approach inherently sounds "better" than the other, as decompression algorithms follow strictly standardized mathematical formulas.

Which Setting Should You Choose?

  • Choose Bitstream when using dedicated Blu-ray players, streaming media players, or home theater software where the priority is passing lossless Dolby Atmos or DTS:X spatial audio directly to an AV receiver.
  • Choose LPCM for video game consoles and PCs connected directly via HDMI or eARC. This ensures minimal audio lag and allows the device to mix dynamic audio tracks, notifications, and game sounds together without compression artifacts.
