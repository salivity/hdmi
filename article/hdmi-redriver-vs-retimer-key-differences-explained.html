HDMI Redriver vs Retimer: Key Differences Explained

High-speed HDMI signals degrade rapidly over long circuit traces and cables, necessitating signal-conditioning components to maintain data integrity. This article explores what an HDMI redriver is, how it functions, and the fundamental architectural and operational differences between an analog redriver and an active digital HDMI retimer in terms of jitter elimination, signal restoration, latency, and power consumption.

What is an HDMI Redriver?

An HDMI redriver is an analog signal conditioner designed to compensate for high-frequency signal losses caused by printed circuit board (PCB) traces, connectors, and transmission cables. At higher HDMI specifications, such as HDMI 2.0 (18 Gbps) and HDMI 2.1 (up to 48 Gbps), channel attenuation degrades the signal, causing the "eye" in an eye diagram to close.

A redriver operates primarily through two mechanisms:

  • Continuous Time Linear Equalization (CTLE): Boosts high-frequency components relative to low-frequency components to counter frequency-dependent channel losses (such as dielectric loss and skin effect).
  • Flat Gain Amplification: Increases the overall voltage amplitude of the differential signal pairs to meet receiver sensitivity thresholds.

Because a redriver is an analog device, it acts purely on the wave shape. It does not decode or re-clock the data stream. Consequently, while it restores amplitude and sharpens edge transitions, it also amplifies any inherent phase noise and deterministic jitter present in the incoming signal.

What is an Active HDMI Retimer?

An active HDMI retimer is a mixed-signal device that fully regenerates the physical layer of the signal. Rather than merely boosting the incoming analog waveform, a retimer integrates a Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) circuit.

The retimer operates through the following steps:

  1. Equalization: Applies input equalization to open the degraded signal eye.
  2. Clock and Data Recovery (CDR): Extracts the timing reference from the incoming signal and determines whether incoming bits are logical 1s or 0s.
  3. Re-clocking: Re-samples the extracted data using a clean, locally generated internal clock (typically synthesized via a Phase-Locked Loop, or PLL).
  4. Re-transmission: Outputs a completely refreshed, standardized signal with newly shaped edges and precise timing.

By performing this process, a retimer breaks the jitter budget of a transmission channel, completely eliminating accumulated random and deterministic jitter up to that point.

Key Operational Differences

1. Jitter Management

  • Redriver: Transparent to timing. It passes both the signal and the accumulated jitter to the receiver. If a signal has significant timing distortion, a redriver cannot fix it and may even worsen jitter margins.
  • Retimer: Jitter-resetting. By sampling the data and re-transmitting it via an internal PLL, the retimer discards incoming jitter, delivering a clean signal compliant with HDMI receiver specifications.

2. Latency

  • Redriver: Operates completely in the analog domain, introducing virtually zero latency (sub-nanosecond).
  • Retimer: The CDR, digital sampling, and serialization stages introduce a slight delay, usually measured in several nanoseconds to clock cycles, though this is negligible for standard video streams.

3. Power Consumption and Cost

  • Redriver: Employs simpler silicon architectures, resulting in lower power consumption, minimal thermal output, and lower unit cost.
  • Retimer: Contains complex digital circuitry, PLLs, and CDR blocks, which require more operating power, generate more heat, and command a higher bill-of-materials (BOM) cost.

4. Channel Reach and Bandwidth Suitability

  • Redriver: Ideal for short-to-medium channel extensions (such as routing signals across a motherboard to an external port) where jitter budgets remain within specification and only attenuation needs mitigation.
  • Retimer: Essential for high-bandwidth interfaces (such as HDMI 2.1 Fixed Rate Link modes) and long transmission paths, where channel loss and jitter exceed the margins allowed by standard HDMI sink devices.
