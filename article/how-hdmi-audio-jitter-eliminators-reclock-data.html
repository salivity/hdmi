How HDMI Audio Jitter Eliminators Reclock Data

HDMI audio jitter eliminators isolate incoming digital audio from unstable video-derived clocks, store the audio samples in a temporary buffer, and release them using an ultra-precise, independent local clock before the signal reaches a digital-to-analog converter (DAC). This process, known as reclocking, completely severs the temporal link between the noisy source device and the sensitive conversion stage, ensuring that digital-to-analog conversion occurs at mathematically exact time intervals to prevent audible distortion.

The Cause of HDMI Audio Jitter

HDMI was engineered primarily for video transmission. In the HDMI protocol, audio data packets are interleaved within the blanking intervals of the video stream. Because there is no dedicated audio clock line, the receiver must reconstruct the audio clock from the video pixel clock using Audio Clock Regeneration (ACR) parameters known as Cycle Time Stamp (CTS) and \(N\) values.

This indirect regeneration method introduces significant timing instability, known as phase jitter. When jittered data reaches a DAC, timing variations at the clock edges cause phase modulation and distortion in the reconstructed analog waveform, resulting in lost micro-detail, a collapsed soundstage, and harsh high frequencies.

Step 1: Packet Demultiplexing and Extraction

The jitter eliminator receives the high-speed Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) HDMI stream. The device’s input processor strips away the video data, metadata, and copy-protection layers (HDCP), isolating the raw linear pulse-code modulation (LPCM) audio packets and their associated sampling frequency flags.

Step 2: Asynchronous FIFO Buffering

Once the audio stream is isolated, the device breaks the connection to the source clock domain using a First-In, First-Out (FIFO) digital memory buffer.

  • The incoming audio data is written into the FIFO buffer using the recovered, jitter-prone clock derived from the HDMI input.
  • The data sits momentarily in memory, stripped of its original timing characteristics.
  • By temporarily storing samples in memory, the timing errors of the incoming HDMI transmission line no longer dictate when the samples will be processed downstream.

Step 3: Local Precision Clock Generation

While data is held in the buffer, the eliminator activates an onboard, ultra-low-phase-noise oscillator system. High-end jitter eliminators generally use dual-frequency crystal oscillators—one dedicated to base frequencies of 44.1 kHz (and its multiples, such as 88.2 kHz and 176.4 kHz) and one for base frequencies of 48 kHz (and its multiples, such as 96 kHz and 192 kHz).

These oscillators (often Temperature Compensated Crystal Oscillators [TCXO] or Oven-Controlled Crystal Oscillators [OCXO]) generate an exceptionally stable Master Clock (MCLK) signal with phase noise measured in picoseconds or femtoseconds.

Step 4: Reclocking and Readout

The core reclocking action occurs as data exits the FIFO buffer:

  1. The stable, local oscillator drives the read-control circuitry of the buffer.
  2. The samples are clocked out of the memory strictly according to the edges of the local clock signal, completely discarding the original HDMI timing.
  3. If slight drift occurs between the incoming stream rate and the local read rate, the buffer uses an adaptive phase-locked loop (PLL) with an extremely low loop bandwidth or asynchronous sample rate conversion (ASRC) to adjust tracking without passing input phase noise to the output.

Step 5: Formatting for DAC Delivery

Once reclocked, the timing-corrected data is synthesized into standard digital audio formats—typically Inter-IC Sound (I2S) or S/PDIF. The I2S format carries separate physical lines for:

  • Bit Clock (BCLK): Defines individual data bit transitions.
  • Word Select (LRCLK): Identifies left and right channels.
  • Master Clock (MCLK): Provides the high-frequency reference for the DAC chips.
  • Serial Data (SDATA): The aligned audio payload.

Because these lines are now synchronized to the pristine local oscillator, the DAC's internal switches trigger with microscopic precision. This uniform conversion eliminates jitter-induced sideband frequencies, delivering a lower noise floor and true-to-source analog output.
