How HDMI Passthrough Fixed the HDR Bottleneck

Early HDMI passthrough devices, including first-generation VR breakout boxes, capture cards, and audio-video receivers, created a notorious bottleneck by stripping or blocking High Dynamic Range (HDR) signals between source consoles and modern displays. This issue primarily stemmed from hardware that lacked the physical bandwidth and signaling protocols required to negotiate HDR metadata. Subsequent hardware revisions resolved this limitation by implementing upgraded HDMI transceivers, redesigning internal circuit routing, improving EDID emulation, and expanding total throughput to meet modern video standards.

Bandwidth and Controller Upgrades

The primary reason early passthrough boxes failed to transmit HDR was the reliance on older HDMI chipsets restricted to HDMI 1.4 or early HDMI 2.0 implementations. These legacy silicon controllers capped bandwidth at 10.2 Gbps, which was sufficient for standard 4K video at 30Hz or 4K SDR at 60Hz using 8-bit 4:2:0 color subsampling, but incapable of carrying uncompressed 4K HDR.

Hardware revisions replaced these legacy transceivers with full-bandwidth 18 Gbps HDMI 2.0b controllers. This extra headroom allowed the passthrough units to transport uncompressed 4K 60Hz signals alongside 10-bit or 12-bit Deep Color spaces (Rec. 2020) and static HDR10 metadata without choking the signal.

Transparent EDID Passthrough

In initial hardware iterations, passthrough boxes acted as an active intermediary that intercepted the Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) handshake between the source (such as a gaming console) and the television. If the passthrough box did not explicitly recognize HDR color profiles within its own internal firmware registers, it modified the EDID handshake to report that the connected screen was only SDR-capable.

Updated revisions solved this by integrating dedicated EDID passthrough microcontrollers or hardware bypass switches. These chips allow the display’s raw EDID block to pass directly to the source device unaltered. As a result, the source accurately detects the television's HDR10 or Dolby Vision capabilities without interference from the passthrough box's local processing logic.

Dedicated Signal Retimers and PCB Redesigns

Pushing 18 Gbps—and eventually 48 Gbps in HDMI 2.1 units—over an intermediary device introduces severe signal attenuation and jitter. Early passthrough boxes suffered from passive trace loss, which led to blackouts or dropped frames when high-bandwidth HDR signals were attempted.

Subsequent hardware revisions overhauled the internal printed circuit board (PCB) layouts. Manufacturers shortened high-speed differential traces, added electromagnetic shielding, and integrated active redriver and retimer integrated circuits (ICs). These components actively regenerate and clean the digital signal before sending it out of the passthrough port, maintaining signal integrity across the combined cable run.

The Shift to HDMI 2.1 and FRL

The latest generation of passthrough hardware made the jump to HDMI 2.1 architectures, replacing the older Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) with Fixed Rate Link (FRL). By adopting FRL and bandwidth capacities up to 48 Gbps, hardware revisions eliminated the final HDR bottlenecks: the inability to transmit dynamic HDR (HDR10+ and Dolby Vision) concurrently with Variable Refresh Rate (VRR), Auto Low Latency Mode (ALLM), and 4K resolution at 120Hz. Modern passthrough hardware now functions with complete transparency, matching the performance of a direct cable connection.
