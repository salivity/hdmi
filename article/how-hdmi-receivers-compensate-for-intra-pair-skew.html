How HDMI Receivers Compensate for Intra-Pair Skew

High-definition multimedia interface (HDMI) systems rely on high-speed differential signaling to transmit audio and video data across copper cables. As transmission speeds increase in standards like HDMI 2.0 and HDMI 2.1, physical mismatches between the positive and negative traces of a differential pair introduce intra-pair skew, which distorts transition edges and closes the data eye. To maintain data integrity, modern HDMI receivers employ integrated equalization and deskew architectures that dynamically detect phase misalignments, apply differential delay adjustments, and shape distorted waveforms before decoding.

The Nature of Intra-Pair Skew

HDMI utilizes Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) in legacy modes and Fixed Rate Link (FRL) in HDMI 2.1. Both architectures transmit data across complementary differential lines (\(D+\) and \(D-\)). Intra-pair skew occurs when signals traveling along these complementary lines arrive at the receiver at slightly different times.

This phase difference is primarily caused by physical trace length variations, printed circuit board (PCB) fiber-weave effects, cable bends, and non-uniform dielectric constants. When \(D+\) and \(D-\) are out of phase, the differential voltage swing decreases, high-frequency common-mode noise increases, and deterministic jitter expands, severely shrinking the differential eye diagram.

Phase Detection and Variable Delay Adjustments

To directly counteract intra-pair skew, modern HDMI receiver front-ends incorporate active deskew circuits placed before or alongside the main equalizer blocks:

  1. Common-Mode Sensing: When differential signals are perfectly balanced, their sum produces a steady DC common-mode voltage. Intra-pair skew causes asymmetric zero-crossings, resulting in high-frequency voltage spikes in the common-mode domain. On-chip sensing circuitry monitors these common-mode artifacts to calculate the magnitude and direction of the timing mismatch between \(D+\) and \(D-\).
  2. Independent Delay Tuning: Once the skew is detected, control logic adjusts programmable delay elements—such as digitally controlled delay lines (DCDLs), variable-capacitance varactor stages, or phase interpolators—embedded in the individual \(D+\) or \(D-\) analog input paths. By delaying the faster trace, the circuit realigns the rising edge of one line with the falling edge of its complement.

Continuous-Time Linear Equalization (CTLE)

Following initial phase balancing, the differential signal enters an integrated Continuous-Time Linear Equalizer (CTLE). Intra-pair skew compounds channel loss by attenuating higher-frequency harmonics and slowing down edge transitions.

The CTLE provides an active, frequency-dependent peaking response that boosts high-frequency components while attenuating low-frequency components. By sharpening the transition edges of the realigned differential pair, the CTLE reduces skew-induced transition spread and partially restores the vertical and horizontal openings of the eye diagram.

Common-Mode Filtering and Termination

Because skew converts differential energy into common-mode noise, receivers must eliminate common-mode reflections that could disrupt the input stage. Integrated circuits use center-tapped termination networks or active common-mode rejection filters at the front end. These circuits present a low-impedance path to ground for common-mode disturbances while maintaining standard 50-ohm termination for differential signals, effectively dissipating the energy generated by the temporal mismatch.

Decision Feedback Equalization (DFE)

Residual intra-pair skew often produces complex inter-symbol interference (ISI) that linear filtering cannot fully resolve. HDMI 2.1 receivers operating under FRL frequently implement Decision Feedback Equalizers (DFE).

Unlike linear equalizers, a DFE evaluates previously decided bits, scales them through feedback taps, and subtracts their residual voltage from the current incoming bit. This nonlinear process neutralizes post-cursor ISI caused by residual skew and high-frequency dispersion without amplifying high-frequency noise.

Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) Integration

After the integrated equalization circuits align the phases, reject common-mode noise, and reopen the differential eye, the cleaned signal feeds into the Clock and Data Recovery (CDR) unit. The CDR locks onto the optimal sampling phase at the center of the restored eye diagram, ensuring low bit-error-rate (BER) data recovery even in the presence of severe upstream cable skew.
