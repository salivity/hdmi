How HDMI Crosspoint Switches Route Signals in AV Receivers

An HDMI crosspoint switch inside an Audio/Video Receiver (AVR) functions as a high-speed matrix router, dynamically directing gigabit-speed differential video and audio signals from multiple input sources to selected outputs. By employing a non-blocking internal architecture coupled with active signal conditioning, such as equalization and retiming, the switch ensures that high-bandwidth HDMI 2.0 (TMDS) and HDMI 2.1 (FRL) data streams traverse the circuit board without excessive jitter, attenuation, or crosstalk.

The Physical Nature of HDMI Lanes

Modern HDMI standards transmit digital data across multiple high-speed differential pairs using Current Mode Logic (CML). In HDMI 2.0, Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) utilizes three data lanes and one dedicated clock lane, achieving speeds up to 6 Gbps per lane. HDMI 2.1 replaces or expands this with Fixed Rate Link (FRL), which operates across three or four data lanes at speeds up to 12 Gbps per lane, embedding the clock directly within the data streams. Routing these signals requires strict 100-ohm differential impedance matching on the printed circuit board (PCB) to prevent reflections and signal degradation.

The Crosspoint Switch Matrix Architecture

At the core of the crosspoint IC is a non-blocking matrix grid composed of input buffers, switching arrays, and output drivers.

  • Input Buffers and Equalization: When a high-speed lane enters the switch, it immediately passes through an input stage equipped with Continuous-Time Linear Equalization (CTLE) or Decision Feedback Equalization (DFE). This compensates for high-frequency losses caused by long external HDMI cables and internal PCB traces.
  • The Switch Fabric: The equalized digital signals enter a high-frequency analog multiplexer array. Unlike standard digital bus routers that serialize or buffer frames in packet memory, a high-speed crosspoint operates primarily at the physical layer (PHY). High-bandwidth transistor switches connect any given differential input pair to any one—or multiple—differential output drivers concurrently without introducing frame latency.
  • Retiming and Clock/Data Recovery (CDR): In HDMI 2.1 FRL applications, the signal is often fed into an integrated CDR circuit. The CDR extracts the embedded clock, cleans timing jitter, and re-samples the data before passing it to the output stage.

Signal Regeneration and Output Driving

Once routed through the matrix, the signal passes to programmable output drivers. These drivers apply pre-emphasis or de-emphasis to compensate for downstream transmission losses between the switch and the display device or internal AVR components. The drivers convert the internal low-voltage core signals back into standard CML levels suitable for exiting the AVR via HDMI Type-A receptacles or internal downstream processing chips.

Sideband and Control Plane Integration

Routing the high-speed data lanes is only part of the operation. The main system microcontroller in the AVR controls the crosspoint switch via an I2C or SPI interface. When a user selects an input, the microcontroller instructs the crosspoint switch to configure its internal routing paths. Concurrently, auxiliary circuits route sideband signals:

  • DDC (Display Data Channel): Switched alongside the high-speed lines to allow EDID exchange and HDCP cryptographic handshakes.
  • HPD (Hot Plug Detect): Managed to trigger source re-negotiation when paths change.
  • CEC (Consumer Electronics Control): Bridged across active inputs and outputs.

Supporting Multi-Zone and Audio Extraction

Because the crosspoint matrix is non-blocking, a single input source can be broadcast to multiple destinations simultaneously. Inside an AVR, this allows an incoming high-speed stream to be sent concurrently to the main HDMI display output, a secondary Zone 2 output, and an internal audio extraction PHY that decodes multi-channel audio formats (such as Dolby Atmos or DTS:X) for the receiver’s digital signal processor (DSP).
