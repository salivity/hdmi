Why Unpowered Active HDMI Accessories Trip Power Rails

Connecting an unpowered active HDMI accessory can trip the source device’s internal power rail due to unexpected electrical current demand that violates standard HDMI port limitations. This article examines the specifications of the HDMI +5V power pin, the electrical behavior of active accessories when external power is absent, and the protective circuitry within source devices that causes power rails to shut down.

The HDMI Pin 18 Power Limitation

The HDMI standard includes a dedicated power line on Pin 18 designed to output +5V. Under the official HDMI specification, a source device is only required to supply a baseline current of 55 mA, primarily intended to power the Display Data Channel (DDC) and read the sink device’s Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) EEPROM even if the display is turned off. While some modern host devices provide up to 500 mA on this line, it was never engineered to serve as a primary power supply for demanding peripheral hardware.

Parasitic Power Draw and Inrush Current

Active HDMI accessories—such as HDMI-to-VGA converters, active optical cables (AOCs), signal boosters, and wireless transmitters—contain integrated circuits, signal equalizers, and logic chips that require substantial operating current, often ranging between 200 mA and over 1 A. When these devices are plugged in without their required external power supply (such as an auxiliary USB power cable), the internal components automatically attempt to pull operating current entirely through Pin 18.

Furthermore, these active circuits incorporate decoupling and filtering capacitors. When connected to a live HDMI port while unpowered, these discharged capacitors temporarily behave like an electrical short circuit. This causes an instantaneous surge of inrush current that vastly exceeds the standard operating limits of the HDMI port.

Activation of Overcurrent Protection (OCP)

To protect host motherboards and graphics cards from damage caused by short circuits or excessive loads, manufacturers integrate protection mechanisms on the +5V rail. These mechanisms commonly include:

  • Polymeric Positive Temperature Coefficient (PTC) Devices: Often called polyfuses, these components rapidly increase in resistance when excessive current generates heat, choking off power to the port until the load is removed and the fuse cools.
  • Load Switches with Integrated Overcurrent Protection: Modern devices frequently use solid-state power switches with built-in current limiters. If the connected accessory demands more current than the programmed threshold, the chip immediately shuts down the rail or enters a current-limiting hiccup mode to prevent trace overheating and voltage drops across the system.

When an unpowered active accessory is plugged in, the combination of high sustained current draw and steep inrush spikes triggers these protective components. The source device's power management system identifies this condition as a fault, immediately cutting power to the HDMI rail or restarting the host controller to prevent permanent hardware damage.
