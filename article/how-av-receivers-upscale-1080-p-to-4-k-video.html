How AV Receivers Upscale 1080p to 4K Video

When an AV receiver upscales an HDMI 1080p signal to 4K Ultra HD, it uses a dedicated video processing chip to increase the frame resolution from 1920x1080 (roughly 2.1 million pixels) to 3840x2160 (roughly 8.3 million pixels) in real time. This article breaks down the exact digital signal processing (DSP) pipeline involved in this transformation, covering signal ingestion, spatial interpolation, edge detection, color space reconstruction, and output re-serialization.

1. HDMI Receiver and Signal Demuxing

The process begins at the HDMI input controller (typically supporting HDMI 2.0 or 2.1 specifications).

  • TMDS/FRL Decoding: The incoming Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) or Fixed Rate Link (FRL) signals are deserialized and decoded.
  • Stream Extraction: The processor strips the HDMI packet wrappers, separating the digital video payload from embedded multi-channel audio, HDCP encryption metadata, and InfoFrames.
  • Frame Buffering: The uncompressed digital video frames (usually in YCbCr 4:2:2 or 4:2:0 format at 8 or 10 bits per channel) are loaded into high-speed DDR memory for line-by-line DSP access.

2. Pre-Processing and Noise Reduction

Before multiplying pixels, the DSP chip cleans the incoming 1080p image to prevent upscaling compression artifacts.

  • De-blocking and De-ringing: The processor applies temporal and spatial filters to smooth out discrete cosine transform (DCT) block boundaries and ringing noise typical of compressed 1080p sources (such as cable boxes or streaming sticks).
  • Temporal Noise Reduction (TNR): Comparing consecutive frames allows the DSP to identify and suppress random luminance and chrominance noise without softening moving objects.

3. Spatial Interpolation (Pixel Multiplication)

Because 4K contains exactly four times the pixels of 1080p, the scaler must generate three artificial pixels for every original pixel. Modern AVRs use adaptive interpolation rather than simple nearest-neighbor or linear scaling:

  • Polyphase Filtering: The chip uses mathematical filters (such as bicubic, Lanczos, or proprietary B-spline curves) to calculate luminance (\(Y\)) and chrominance (\(Cb/Cr\)) values based on a grid of surrounding original pixels.
  • Directional Edge-Adaptive Interpolation: To avoid pixelation or jagged edges ("staircasing"), the DSP calculates gradients across neighboring pixels to determine edge angles. Interpolation is applied along the contour of edges rather than across them, preserving sharp object boundaries.

4. Detail Enhancement and Edge Sharpening

Interpolation inherently introduces softness. The DSP applies high-frequency restoration algorithms to recreate the appearance of native 4K:

  • High-Pass Filtering: The processor isolates high-frequency spatial data to identify textures and contours.
  • Unsharp Masking and Contrast Peaking: Micro-contrast is boosted along localized edges to simulate higher resolution.
  • Over-shoot/Under-shoot Suppression: The algorithm clamps luminance spikes near sharp transitions to prevent "haloing" (bright white or dark outlines around objects).

5. Chroma Resampling and Precision Management

Video scaling engines perform internal math at high bit depths to avoid precision loss.

  • Bit-Depth Expansion: An 8-bit incoming signal is typically expanded to 10-bit or 12-bit precision during processing to eliminate round-off errors and banding.
  • Chroma Upsampling: If the signal enters as YCbCr 4:2:0, the DSP interpolates the sub-sampled color channels up to 4:2:2 or 4:4:4 before or during the scaling stage to maintain color edge alignment.

6. Frame Formatting, HDCP 2.2, and HDMI Serialization

Once the 3840x2160 pixel grid is fully rendered, the output stage preps the signal for delivery to the display:

  • Dithering: If the display requires an 8-bit output format, spatial error diffusion or temporal dithering is applied to scale back down from the internal 10/12-bit processing pipeline without introducing contour lines.
  • HDCP Handshake: The outgoing video data is re-encrypted using HDCP 2.2 or 2.3 protocols, which are mandatory for 4K content transmission.
  • HDMI Transmitter: The 4K digital frame buffer is serialized into higher-bandwidth TMDS lanes (up to 18 Gbps) or FRL lanes (up to 48 Gbps) alongside the synchronized, delay-compensated audio stream and transmitted to the television or projector.
