How HDCP 2.3 Locality Checks Prevent Remote HDMI Routing

High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) 2.3 incorporates strict verification protocols designed to prevent the unauthorized transmission of high-value audiovisual content across wide-area networks or cloud environments. This article explains the technical mechanics of the HDCP 2.3 locality check, detailing how round-trip time measurements and cryptographic handshakes verify that connected devices are physically co-located, thereby blocking remote routing and unauthorized HDMI stream redistribution.

The Purpose of the Locality Check

In digital transmission architectures like HDMI-over-IP and wireless display standards, an unconstrained stream could theoretically be captured, piped through a VPN or long-haul internet connection, and decrypted thousands of miles away. While HDCP 2.3 encrypts data payload transfers, standard encryption alone does not inherently dictate physical proximity. The locality check bridges this gap by validating that the downstream receiver (sink) resides on the same local network or physical perimeter as the upstream transmitter (source).

The Challenge-Response Handshake

The locality check occurs during the HDCP authentication protocol, typically right after the Authentication and Key Exchange (AKE) phase generates the master keying material.

  1. Nonce Generation: The transmitter generates a 64-bit pseudo-random value known as \(r_{tx}\) and transmits it to the receiver.
  2. Cryptographic Computation: The receiver must compute a locality check value, designated as \(L'\) (or \(V'\) depending on phase/mode), using an HMAC-SHA256 algorithm. This computation takes the nonces (\(r_{tx}\), \(r_{rx}\)) and binds them mathematically to the derived secret master key (\(K_D\)) established during the initial pairing.
  3. Response Verification: The receiver sends the computed response back to the transmitter. The transmitter independently computes the expected value (\(L\)) and compares it to the receiver’s response.

Because the response relies on the unique session key, an intermediate man-in-the-middle node cannot alter, forge, or relay fake responses without knowing the secret master keys stored in secure hardware.

Round-Trip Time (RTT) Enforcement

The core mechanism preventing remote relay attacks is a strict timing threshold applied to the challenge-response cycle.

When the transmitter emits the \(r_{tx}\) challenge, it starts a high-resolution hardware timer. The receiver must calculate the cryptographic digest and transmit the response back within a microsecond-level timing window:

  • Maximum Permissible Latency: The HDCP specification typically enforces a maximum round-trip limit of approximately 7 to 20 milliseconds (standard network configurations target under 7 ms).
  • Speed-of-Light Limitation: While local network switches, certified repeaters, and direct physical cables process this exchange within 1 to 2 milliseconds, routing packets across standard internet infrastructure, proxies, or multi-hop wide-area networks introduces transit latency that far exceeds the millisecond threshold.
  • Pre-computation Prevention: Because the challenge relies on the random \(r_{tx}\) generated in real time, a remote receiver cannot pre-calculate the result to compensate for network latency.

Failure Handling and Stream Termination

If the receiver fails to return the correct cryptographic response within the designated time limit, the locality check fails. The transmitter allows a small number of immediate retries (typically up to two attempts) to account for minor local packet collisions.

If the check consistently fails or times out:

  • The transmitter invalidates the cryptographic keys for that session.
  • The HDMI stream's visual output is either muted entirely or replaced with a standard HDCP error screen.
  • High-bandwidth payload encryption is disabled until a complete re-authentication cycle is executed from scratch.

Through this combination of cryptographic verification and sub-millisecond round-trip time thresholds, HDCP 2.3 ensures that protected streams remain confined strictly to local displays and cannot be mirrored or piped over external wide-area networks.
