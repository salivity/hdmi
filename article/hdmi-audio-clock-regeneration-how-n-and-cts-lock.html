HDMI Audio Clock Regeneration: How N and CTS Lock

In an HDMI transmission, audio clock regeneration (ACR) allows a sink device to accurately reconstruct the source’s original audio sampling clock without requiring a dedicated physical clock line. Because HDMI carries audio packets interleaved within the video data stream during blanking intervals, the receiver relies on two values transmitted in ACR packets—the Numerator (\(N\)) and the Cycle Time Stamp (\(CTS\))—along with the recovered Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) clock. By feeding these parameters into a localized Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) or frequency synthesizer, the receiver locks onto and regenerates a stable, jitter-free audio master clock (\(128 \times f_s\)).

The ACR Mathematical Relationship

The relationship between the audio master clock (\(f_s \times 128\)), the TMDS pixel clock (\(f_{TMDS}\)), and the parameters \(N\) and \(CTS\) is defined by the formula:

\[128 \times f_s = \frac{f_{TMDS} \times N}{CTS}\]

  • \(N\): A static integer specified by the HDMI specification designed to yield an exact fractional frequency relationship for standard audio rates (such as 32 kHz, 44.1 kHz, or 48 kHz).
  • \(CTS\): A dynamic counter representing the exact number of TMDS clock cycles that elapsed during a specific number of audio clock cycles.

Parsing the ACR Packets

  1. Packet Identification: The HDMI receiver continuously decodes the data island periods of the incoming TMDS stream. It identifies Audio Clock Regeneration packets by their specific packet header type code (0x01).
  2. Payload Extraction: Each ACR packet contains four identical subpackets to protect against transmission errors. The receiver unpacks the 20-bit \(N\) and 20-bit \(CTS\) values from these subpackets.
  3. Mode Determination: The source operates in either coherent mode (where the audio and video clocks derive from a single master oscillator and \(CTS\) is constant) or non-coherent mode (where clocks are independent and \(CTS\) varies slightly over time). The receiver identifies fluctuations in \(CTS\) to configure its tracking response.

The Locking Process in the Receiver PLL

To lock onto the audio clock, the receiver passes the recovered parameters through an on-chip frequency synthesis loop, typically implemented as an analog Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) or a Digitally Controlled Oscillator (DCO).

  1. Pre-Dividing Reference Signals: The receiver's TMDS clock recovery block first locks onto the physical video clock channel. The recovered TMDS clock is routed to a counter that divides the frequency by the extracted \(CTS\) value.
  2. Feedback Division: The output of the local audio Voltage-Controlled Oscillator (VCO)—which runs at the audio clock rate (\(128 \times f_s\))—is fed back through a frequency divider set to divide by \(N\).
  3. Phase and Frequency Comparison: A Phase Frequency Detector (PFD) compares the divided TMDS reference signal (\(f_{TMDS} / CTS\)) with the divided audio feedback signal (\(f_{audio} / N\)).
  4. Loop Filtering and Jitter Suppression: Because packet transmission jitter can introduce timing variance, and non-coherent sources cause the \(CTS\) value to step incrementally between packets, the receiver passes the error signal through a low-bandwidth loop filter. This dampens rapid fluctuations and prevents jitter from affecting audio fidelity.
  5. Phase Lock Acquisition: The loop filter drives the VCO until the phase and frequency error between the two divided signals reaches zero. Once equalized, the PLL asserts an internal "lock" status, providing an uninterrupted, phase-aligned master clock to the audio Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs) or digital audio processors.
