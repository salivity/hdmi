How HDMI Adapters Draw Phantom Power From Displays

Active HDMI adapters, signal boosters, and active optical cables often require more electrical power than a source device can deliver on its own. While traditional active adapters rely on an external USB cable for power, phantom-powered active adapters harvest supplemental power directly from the display input. This article explains how these adapters draw current from the sink (display) interface using TMDS termination voltage and control lines, eliminating the need for external power supplies.

The Source Power Limitation

The standard HDMI specification includes a dedicated power rail on Pin 18 (+5V Power). Transmitting devices (sources) like laptops, media players, and graphics cards are only required to deliver a minimum of 55 mA at 4.8V to 5.3V, though some provide up to 300 mA. Active adapters, which convert signals (e.g., HDMI to DisplayPort or VGA) or equalize degraded signals over long distances, frequently demand 200 mA to 500 mA or more. When the source port fails to supply enough current, the connection drops, stutters, or fails entirely.

TMDS Line Termination and Bias-Tee Harvesting

The primary mechanism a phantom-powered HDMI adapter uses to draw supplemental energy from a display input (sink) is harvesting DC voltage from the Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) lines.

  1. Current-Mode Logic (CML) Architecture: HDMI data and clock channels utilize Current-Mode Logic. Inside the receiving display, each differential signal line is pulled up to an internal 3.3V rail through a 50-ohm termination resistor.
  2. Extracting Common-Mode DC: Because the high-frequency video data is alternating differential AC, a DC voltage offset remains present on the transmission lines. Phantom-powered adapters tap into this offset using passive inductors (chokes) or bias-tee circuits.
  3. Filtering Signal from Power: The high-frequency video signal passes through coupling capacitors to the display receiver, while the inductive chokes isolate and pull the DC current from the display's 3.3V termination rail without degrading the video data.

This process mirrors the classic phantom power method used in professional audio microphones, running power and high-frequency data across the exact same physical conductors.

Supplemental Harvesting from HPD and DDC Lines

Beyond the primary TMDS data lanes, phantom-powered active adapters can also scavenge small amounts of standby energy from the display's control pins:

  • Hot Plug Detect (HPD - Pin 19): Monitors and displays hold the HPD line high (typically 3.3V to 5V) via an internal pull-up resistor to signal that a display is connected and ready to receive data. Ultra-low-power logic circuits inside the adapter can sip microamps from this rail.
  • Display Data Channel (DDC): The I2C clock (SCL, Pin 15) and data (SDA, Pin 16) lines used for EDID handshaking are pulled high inside the display. In certain circuit designs, diodes tap transient power from these lines during idle states to supplement internal microcontrollers.

Dynamic Power Path Management

Drawing power from both an HDMI source (Pin 18 at 5V) and an HDMI sink (TMDS termination at 3.3V) creates an electrical challenge: the voltages must not back-feed into either device, as reverse current can damage the silicon in graphics cards or display controller boards.

To prevent this, phantom-powered adapters incorporate a Power Path Management circuit using low-forward-voltage Schottky diodes or an ideal diode controller (ORing MOSFETs):

  • Source Priority: When the HDMI source provides sufficient 5V power, the adapter runs entirely off Pin 18, keeping sink current draw to a minimum.
  • Supplemental Sink Draw: When source voltage dips due to high impedance or load, the adapter switches or supplements current from the display's termination lines.
  • Buck-Boost Conversion: The adapter employs an onboard high-efficiency DC-DC buck-boost converter. It takes the disparate, fluctuating voltages—the dropping 5V rail from the source and the 3.3V scavenged from the display—and stabilizes them into a clean 1.2V, 1.8V, or 3.3V rail required by the adapter's active signal processing chipsets.
